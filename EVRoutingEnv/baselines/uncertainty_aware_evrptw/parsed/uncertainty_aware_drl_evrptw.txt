 A B S T R A C T
Urban freight accounts for approximately 20% of transport-related greenhouse gas emissions, yet electric 
vehicle (EV) routing faces critical barriers: limited range, charging constraints, and energy uncertainty. 
Existing methods cannot quantify stranding risk or support chance-constrained battery feasibility, as energy 
consumption exhibits 30%–40% variability. We address the EVRPTW under operational uncertainty via 
probabilistic energy forecasting and hybrid optimization. Bidirectional LSTM networks deliver calibrated 
uncertainty quantification (94.7% empirical coverage for 95% confidence intervals), enabling model-based 
chance-constrained battery feasibility. Graph neural networks encode battery-aware spatial–temporal depen-
dencies, while Proximal Policy Optimization with optional mixed-integer programming refinement balances 
real-time adaptability (0.3–0.8 s inference) with offline solution quality. Evaluation on 30 Solomon-derived 
EVRPTW benchmark instances (60–100 customers) shows an 8.8% cost reduction over the strongest learning 
baseline (MVMoE), rising to 12.2% under dynamic conditions. Real-world deployment with a 25-vehicle 
electric fleet over 13 weeks validates €143,000 in projected annual savings, 98.1% on-time delivery, 15.5% 
energy reduction, and 96% planning time reduction, with fleet emissions at approximate carbon parity with 
diesel on Italy’s transitional grid alongside elimination of local air pollutants. Transfer learning protocols reduce 
region-adaptation retraining effort by ∼95% (from ∼72 h to 3–5 h of fine-tuning), requiring only 400–600 local 
routes. This work demonstrates a pathway toward more sustainable urban logistics through uncertainty-aware 
optimization with measurable environmental, economic, and operational benefits.
1. Introduction
The global transition toward sustainable urban systems has placed 
considerable focus on decarbonizing freight logistics, a sector respon-
sible for approximately 8% of global CO2 emissions and up to 20% 
of urban transport emissions [1,2]. Electric vehicle adoption in freight 
operations offers a compelling pathway toward decarbonization, with 
well-to-wheel emissions reductions of 40%–60% compared to conven-
tional diesel vehicles [3,4]. Major logistics operators have announced 
ambitious electrification targets, and policy frameworks reinforce this 
transition, with the European Green Deal mandating zero-emission 
urban logistics in major cities by 2030 [5–7].
Despite this momentum, a critical operational barrier constrains 
EV deployment: energy consumption uncertainty. Real-world electric 
delivery operations exhibit 30%–40% consumption variability based on

The central research question is: Can uncertainty-aware deep rein-

vehicle routing that simultaneously achieves competitive solution quality, 
model-based chance-constrained feasibility, and computational tractability 
for operational deployment? Addressing this requires advancing neu-
ral architectures that explicitly represent battery constraints, creating 
probabilistic energy models with calibrated confidence intervals, and 
designing hybrid protocols that balance learning and optimization.
This work advances uncertainty-aware electric vehicle routing

These contributions provide evidence of measurable environmen-
tal, economic, and operational benefits through both computational 
validation and real-world deployment, offering concrete guidance for 
fleet operators pursuing cost-effective electrification, cities working 
toward decarbonization objectives, and policy makers designing incen-
tive structures—reconciling environmental mandates with operational 
realities.
2. Literature review
2.1. Electric vehicle routing: From deterministic to stochastic formulations
The Electric Vehicle Routing Problem with Time Windows
(EVRPTW) has emerged as a critical extension of classical vehicle 
routing optimization, incorporating the unique operational constraints 
of battery-electric vehicles. Schneider et al. [11] established the foun-
dational mathematical framework by introducing battery capacity con-
straints, recharging station visits, and nonlinear charging functions into 
the traditional VRP-TW formulation. Early exact solution approaches, 
including the branch-and-price framework by Desaulniers et al. [20], 
demonstrated optimality guarantees for small-scale instances (≤25 
customers) but remained computationally intractable for realistic oper-
ational scales. Related work on charging infrastructure planning, such 
as the flow refueling location model (FRLM) framework by Park and 
Lee [13], addresses optimal charging station placement considering 
heterogeneous chargers and nonlinear charging times, though these 
location models assume fixed routing patterns rather than jointly 
optimizing routes and charging decisions.
Recognizing these computational limitations, researchers developed 
metaheuristic approaches that trade solution optimality for scalability. 
Keskin et al. [21] introduced partial recharging strategies that sig-
nificantly reduced total travel time by allowing vehicles to recharge 
only as needed rather than to full capacity. Mavrovouniotis et al. 
[22] applied ant colony optimization with dynamic pheromone updat-
ing mechanisms tailored to battery state constraints. More recently, 
matheuristic approaches combining mathematical programming with 
heuristic search have achieved state-of-the-art performance: Bruglieri 
et al. [12] reported superior solution quality through Random Kernel 
Search that iteratively refines kernel solutions via mixed-integer pro-
gramming, while Zhang et al. [23] employed column generation within 
a branch-and-price framework for instances with up to 100 customers.
However, a significant limitation in much of this classical optimiza-
tion literature is the assumption of deterministic energy consumption. 
Real-world electric vehicle operations exhibit substantial energy vari-
ability — ranging from 30% to 40% — driven by traffic conditions, 
weather parameters, payload variations, and driver behavior [8]. This 
determinism-reality gap represents an important challenge for the prac-
tical deployment of optimization-based routing systems, motivating the 
development of stochastic and robust formulations.
The inherent uncertainty in electric vehicle energy consumption has 
motivated research into stochastic and robust optimization approaches 
that explicitly model operational variability. Yuan et al. [14] address 
battery status uncertainty through two-stage stochastic programming, 
where first-stage decisions establish routes based on expected battery 
status and second-stage recourse actions handle realized uncertainty 
through customer skipping or late returns. Their scenario-based ap-
proach, solved via adaptive large neighborhood search, achieves 10.3% 
cost reduction versus deterministic models while maintaining stable 
performance across uncertainty realizations. However, the computa-
tional burden of scenario generation and evaluation limits real-time 
applicability, particularly for large fleets operating under time pressure. 
Zhang et al. [24] similarly employ scenario-based formulations but 
require extensive computational resources that remain prohibitive for 
operational decision-making.
Alternative formulations have explored different uncertainty han-
dling mechanisms that avoid explicit scenario enumeration. Yan et al. 
[25] propose diversified tour-driven DRL that generates multiple candi-
date solutions to implicitly capture uncertainty, achieving up to 7.54% 
improvement in optimality gap through Kullback–Leibler divergence-
based regularization encouraging solution diversity. Bi et al. [26] ex-
plicitly model speed-varying range in time-windowed routing, rec-
ognizing that EV range decreases at higher speeds due to aerody-
namic drag, and apply automated entropy regularization in DRL to

consumption deterministically and therefore neither quantifies predic-
tive uncertainty nor enforces chance-constrained battery feasibility, 
which is the gap our probabilistic formulation addresses.
2.2. Deep reinforcement learning for combinatorial optimization
The application of deep reinforcement learning to combinatorial 
optimization has substantially advanced solution methodologies for 
routing problems. Kool et al. [15] introduced attention-based encoder–
decoder architectures that learn constructive heuristics directly from 
problem structure, demonstrating competitive performance with clas-
sical heuristics on traveling salesman and capacitated vehicle routing 
problems while requiring only milliseconds of inference time. Kwon 
et al. [16] enhanced this paradigm through Policy Optimization with 
Multiple Optima (POMO), utilizing multiple solution rollouts during 
both training and inference to improve solution diversity and quality. 
Kim et al. [31] further refined these approaches with collaborative 
learning mechanisms that enable policy networks to leverage auxiliary 
optimization signals.
Recent advances have explored foundation models and large-scale 
pretraining for routing optimization. Zhou et al. [32] developed MV-
MoE, a multi-task vehicle routing solver employing mixture-of-experts 
with hierarchical gating mechanisms to enhance model capacity across 
16 VRP variants simultaneously, achieving strong zero-shot general-
ization on unseen problem types. Hudson et al. [33] proposed graph 
neural diffusion models (GNARKC) that iteratively refine solution dis-
tributions, while Zhao et al. [34] investigated generative diffusion 
approaches for solution space exploration. Despite impressive perfor-
mance on canonical benchmarks, these foundation models face signifi-
cant challenges when applied to energy-constrained routing: they typ-
ically lack explicit battery state tracking mechanisms, provide limited 
uncertainty quantification for energy consumption, and face difficul-
ties with the multi-objective nature of feasibility-versus-cost tradeoffs 
inherent to EVRPTW. Complementary work by Cao et al. [35] pro-
posed progressive training paradigms that gradually increase problem 
complexity during neural combinatorial optimization, improving con-
vergence stability—a principle we similarly employ through curriculum 
learning in our framework.
Specialized neural approaches for electric vehicle routing are still 
emerging. Lin et al. [36] combined neural construction heuristics with 
simplified energy prediction models but employed deterministic con-
sumption estimates that fail to capture operational uncertainty. Zong 
et al. [37] addressed uncertainty through distributional reinforcement 
learning, learning value distributions rather than point estimates, yet 
their framework omits charging infrastructure modeling and provides 
limited guidance for route feasibility under battery constraints. Wang 
et al. [38] developed attention-enhanced architectures specifically for 
EVRP, incorporating heterogeneous attention mechanisms to distin-
guish customers from charging stations, though without probabilistic 
energy modeling. Liu et al. [39] explored heterogeneous autonomous 
vehicle scheduling for last-mile delivery using attention-based DRL but 
focused on vehicle-passenger matching rather than energy-constrained 
routing with charging decisions.
Recent works have begun addressing specific EVRP complexities 
through learning-based methods. Wu et al. [40] tackled battery swap-
ping station operations with uncertain demand using deep reinforce-
ment learning, though their focus remained on charging infrastruc-
ture management rather than integrated routing optimization. Moon 
et al. [41] investigated energy and time optimization for EV charging 
routes under uncertainty in charging station availability, introducing 
time-aware charging selection but without comprehensive route-level 
optimization. Maity and Sarkar [19] proposed graph convolutional 
network-based DRL for EVRP with stochastic customer requests, incor-
porating probabilistic energy consumption estimation, representing a 
step toward uncertainty-aware neural routing. However, their approach 
lacks explicit confidence-based planning mechanisms and hybrid op-
timization refinement. Shahbazian et al. [17] developed knowledge-
guided hybrid DRL combining multi-agent deep Q-networks with vari-
able neighborhood search for dynamic multi-depot EVRP, demonstrat-
ing over 70% distance reduction on small instances but facing scalabil-
ity challenges and lacking probabilistic energy modeling.
2.3. Hybrid learning-optimization approaches
Recognition that pure learning and pure optimization approaches 
each possess complementary strengths has motivated hybrid method-
ologies. Hottung et al. [42] demonstrated that neural construction 
heuristics combined with improvement procedures (e.g., 2-opt, 3-opt 
local search) significantly outperform either component in isolation. 
Cheng et al. [43] employed learned heuristics for neighbor selection 
within large-scale neighborhood search, using neural networks to pre-
dict promising move types dynamically. Ye et al. [44] proposed guided 
local optimization with partitioning (GLOP), where learning handles 
global solution structure while mathematical optimization addresses lo-
cal refinement. Drakulic et al. [45] introduced bisimulation quotienting 
for neural combinatorial optimization (BQ-NCO), using learned neural 
bounds to guide branch-and-bound search and achieving substantial 
pruning of the search space.
In the electric vehicle domain, hybrid approaches remain nascent. 
Baum et al. [46] combined reinforcement learning with strategic
charging decisions but relied on heuristic routing construction
rather than optimization-based refinement. Su et al. [47] integrated
physics-informed neural networks for battery state estimation but ap-
plied deterministic routing methods that do not leverage learned energy 
models for robust planning. Wang et al. [18] developed DRL-based 
adaptive large neighborhood search for capacitated EVRP, treating 
operator selection as a Markov decision process and using neural net-
works to adaptively select both destroy-repair operators and charging 
strategies based on search state features. Zhang et al. [48] proposed 
collaborative EVRP with mobile battery swapping vans, employing 
extended adaptive large neighborhood search with two-dimensional 
encoding to simultaneously optimize EV routes and BSV synchroniza-
tion, though lacking explicit uncertainty handling. Çatay and İhsan 
Sadati [49] examined battery swapping policies within matheuristic 
frameworks, clarifying assumptions about swapping time and introduc-
ing new policies for synchronized mobile battery swapping, demon-
strating the importance of recharging strategy within hybrid solution 
approaches.
Critical analysis reveals that existing hybrid approaches for EVRP 
have not yet integrated three essential capabilities: (1) graph neu-
ral network-based state representation that captures complex spatial–
temporal dependencies in energy-constrained routing, (2) probabilistic 
energy consumption prediction with explicit uncertainty quantification

2.4. Research gaps and opportunities
Recent research has explored specialized EVRP extensions—life-
cycle assessment for airport towing vehicles [50], soft time windows 
with weight-related discharging [51], and multi-mode hybrid vehicles 
optimizing operation mode selection per road segment [52]. While 
these works address important operational nuances, they largely rely 
on deterministic frameworks that do not account for energy consump-
tion uncertainty or provide adaptive replanning capabilities. Critical 
synthesis of the broader literature reveals three fundamental gaps:
Integration of Realistic Uncertainty with Neural Routing. Ex-
isting DRL approaches demonstrate strong performance on canonical 
problems but typically do not incorporate probabilistic energy con-
sumption models with calibrated uncertainty estimates. Conversely, 
stochastic optimization approaches model uncertainty rigorously but 
often lack the computational efficiency essential for operational de-
ployment. Few frameworks combine graph neural network-based policy 
learning with calibrated probabilistic energy forecasting that provides 
explicit confidence intervals for battery feasibility.
Formal Uncertainty Quantification for Operational Safety.
While several works acknowledge energy consumption variability, few 
provide formal mechanisms for risk-aware decision-making through 
chance-constrained planning with specified confidence levels. Existing 
deterministic safety margins (e.g., reserving 20% battery capacity) 
often lack theoretical grounding and may prove either excessively 
conservative or insufficient.
Computational Efficiency-Solution Quality Balance. Pure neural 
approaches achieve millisecond inference but may sacrifice solution 
quality (5%–10% optimality gap), while pure optimization approaches 
require substantial computation time that can limit real-time adapta-
tion. Most hybrid approaches have not fully integrated learned poli-
cies with mathematical refinement to enable dual-mode operation: 
rapid neural inference for real-time decisions and optional optimization 
refinement for offline planning.
These gaps motivate the problem formulation and solution approach 
developed below.
3. Problem formulation
This section formalizes the EVRPTW under operational uncertainty 
as a stochastic optimization problem, introducing the graph repre-
sentation, energy consumption model under uncertainty, deterministic 
constraints, and chance-constrained battery feasibility. The resulting 
computational complexity motivates the hybrid deep reinforcement 
learning approach presented subsequently.

we acknowledge that in settings with highly multimodal traffic pat-
terns, alternative distributions such as log-normal or Gaussian mixture 
models may provide better fit, and we identify this as a direction for 
future work.

Computational complexity: The EVRPTW under uncertainty be-
longs to the class of stochastic mixed-integer nonlinear programs
(SMINLP) with individual chance constraints (one per (node, vehicle) 
pair, as formalized in Eq. (8)). This individual formulation is distinct 
from a joint chance constraint, which enforces a single route-level 
guarantee through Bonferroni aggregation and which we discuss as a 
possible extension in Section 7. Even the deterministic variant (setting 
𝜎2𝑖𝑗 = 0 ) is NP-hard as it generalizes the classical vehicle routing 
problem. The integration of stochastic battery constraints, nonlinear 
energy models, and time dependencies renders exact solution methods 
computationally intractable for realistic instance sizes (𝑛 > 50). This 
motivates our hybrid deep reinforcement learning approach, which pro-
vides rapid heuristic solutions with optional MILP refinement, enabling 
both real-time deployment (sub-second inference) and high-quality 
offline planning when computational budgets permit.
4. Methodology
This section presents our hybrid deep reinforcement learning frame-
work for solving the uncertainty-aware EVRPTW. We first overview the 
four-phase architecture, then detail three core technical components: 
Graph Attention Networks for spatial–temporal encoding, LSTM-based 
probabilistic energy prediction, and Proximal Policy Optimization with 
optional MILP refinement. We conclude with transfer learning protocols 
and computational complexity analysis.
Table  1 summarizes the role of each component, distinguishing 
learned neural components from fixed optimization modules.
4.1. Framework architecture
The EVRPTW under uncertainty requires simultaneously capturing 
complex spatial–temporal dependencies, quantifying energy consump-
tion uncertainty with calibrated confidence intervals, and balancing 
real-time decision-making with high-quality offline optimization. Our 
hybrid framework addresses this through four integrated phases.
Phase 1 (Offline Pre-training) establishes general routing com-
petencies through curriculum learning across 35,000 episodes with 
problem sizes ranging from 10 to 100 customers, jointly training the 
GAT encoder, LSTM energy predictor, and PPO policy. Phase 2 (Trans-
fer Learning) adapts pre-trained models to target operational contexts 
using 400–600 local routes, reducing adaptation (retraining) time from 
72 h to 3–5 h. Phase 3 (Online Routing) generates real-time solutions 
in 0.3–0.8 s with model-based chance-constrained battery feasibility.
Phase 4 (Hybrid Refinement) optionally improves solutions by 2.8% 
on average, rising to 4.1% on the largest instances (Table  6) through 
MILP post-optimization when computational budgets permit.
These phases rely on learned modules (GAT encoder, LSTM predic-
tor, PPO policy) whose parameters are optimized during training, and
fixed modules (chance constraint checking, MILP solver) that apply 
deterministic rules or mathematical optimization. Fig.  1 illustrates the 
complete architecture and information flow between components.

4.3. Probabilistic energy prediction
The GAT encoder requires energy consumption estimates 𝜇𝑖𝑗 and 
uncertainty measures 𝜎𝑖𝑗 for each arc (𝑖, 𝑗) to compute battery-aware 
attention weights. As established in the literature review, energy con-
sumption in electric vehicles exhibits 30%–40% variability due to 
traffic, weather, payload, and terrain. Deterministic estimates fail to 
capture this uncertainty, leading to either overly conservative routes 
(if safety margins are too large) or feasibility violations (if margins are 
insufficient). We address this through a bidirectional LSTM network 
that predicts both mean energy consumption 𝜇𝐸 and variance 𝜎2𝐸 , 
enabling model-based chance-constrained planning (Eq. (8)).
The predictor is trained to output both the mean and variance 
of energy consumption through a heteroscedastic loss function that 
explicitly penalizes miscalibrated uncertainty:

4.5. Transfer learning and deployment
The trained framework achieves strong performance on bench-
mark instances similar to its training distribution. However, real-world 
deployment requires adaptation to new geographic regions with differ-
ent traffic patterns, weather distributions, and charging infrastructure 
layouts. Our transfer learning protocol reduces adaptation time by 
95% through strategic fine-tuning of only the components sensitive to 
domain shift.

predictor, as the LSTM energy predictor is most sensitive to domain 
shift—local traffic speeds, weather patterns, and road grades differ from 
training regions. We fine-tune 𝜃𝐸 on 400–600 local routes (correspond-
ing to 2–3 weeks of historical data) using the heteroscedastic loss in 
Eq. (17), while keeping the GAT encoder and policy network frozen.

Our executed baselines span three learning paradigms, all run on 
the same 30 instances under the adaptation protocol detailed below. 
Early neural approaches include the Attention Model (AM) [15] and 
Policy Optimization with Multiple Optima (POMO) [16]. Recent neural 
methods include MVMoE [32], adapted for EVRPTW through fine-
tuning with battery state features. Hybrid approaches are represented 
by DACT [54]. For classical-optimization context, we additionally cite 
the Random Kernel Search (RKS) matheuristic [12]; RKS was not re-
executed on our instance set, and its published results on comparable 
standard Solomon-derived instances are referenced for context only 
rather than as a controlled comparison.
To ensure a fair comparison, all neural baselines (AM, POMO, 
MVMoE, DACT) were adapted for EVRPTW with identical access to the 
uncertainty-aware information available to our framework:
• Battery state. Current battery 𝑏∕𝐵, current load 𝑤∕𝑄, and cur-
rent time 𝑡∕𝑇max are concatenated to node embeddings as dy-
namic agent-state features, matching the vehicle-context features 
injected into our GAT encoder (Algorithm 1, line 3).
• Charging stations. Represented as a separate node type with mask-
ing rules: a station cannot be visited at full battery, and only 
nodes reachable under the chance-constraint check are made 
available in the action space.
• Uncertainty information. The predicted arc-level standard devia-
tion 𝜎𝑖𝑗 from our LSTM predictor is concatenated to each base-
line’s edge-feature vector, identical to the edge representation in 
Eq. (14). This isolates the contribution of our architectural choices 
(GAT encoding, chance-constraint checking, MILP refinement) 
from any informational asymmetry between methods.
• Charging decisions. The action space of every baseline is extended 
to include charging-station insertion; routes that still violate the 
chance constraint after autoregressive construction are repaired 
post hoc by the same greedy charger-insertion procedure used in 
our framework, applied identically to every neural baseline and 
to our own method so that no method gains an advantage from a 
different repair routine.
• Tuning protocol. Each baseline was trained for 35,000 episodes 
(matched with our method), on identical GPU hardware (NVIDIA 
A100 40 GB), with five random seeds (0, 42, 123, 456, 789) and an 
equalized hyperparameter search budget of 50 configurations per 
baseline, sampled from each method’s published recommended 
range.
All neural methods use the same problem instances, time windows, and 
energy consumption models.
Implementation employs PyTorch 2.0 on NVIDIA A100 GPUs
(40 GB) with 128-dimensional GAT embeddings across 4 layers and 8 
attention heads. The LSTM energy predictor uses 64 hidden units with 
bidirectional processing. PPO employs learning rate 3 × 10−4, discount 
factor 𝛾 = 0.99, and GAE parameter 𝜆 = 0.95. Optional MILP refinement 
uses Gurobi 10.0 with 300–1200 s time limits and 1% optimality gap. 
All experiments employ five random seeds (0, 42, 123, 456, 789). For 
each of the 30 instances, we report the mean cost across five seeds per 
method; method-to-method comparisons are reported descriptively as 
relative cost differences. Table  2 summarizes computational resources 
and training requirements. The complete implementation, including 
model architectures, training scripts, evaluation pipelines, and pre-
generated results, is publicly available at https://github.com/Aymen-
Anbar/evrptw-repo.
Evaluation metrics capture multiple performance dimensions: so-
lution quality (total operational cost including vehicle deployment, 
routing, charging, and time window penalties), computational effi-
ciency (wall-clock time), feasibility (percentage of routes satisfying 
battery constraints at 95% confidence), fleet utilization (number of 
vehicles deployed), and energy consumption (total kWh). For dynamic 
experiments, we additionally measure replanning frequency, on-time 
delivery rate, and vehicle stranding events.

Ablation studies on 12 medium-scale instances (20–40 customers)

5.3.1. Mechanistic analysis
Graph Attention Networks (6.4 pp): GAT’s advantage stems from 
learned battery-state-dependent spatial relationships. MLP encoders 
treat feasible customers uniformly, producing myopic decisions that 
minimize immediate travel cost without considering downstream bat-
tery constraints. Analysis reveals GAT attention adapts dynamically: 
when battery exceeds 70%, attention distributes broadly (entropy 𝐻 =
2.87 nats); when battery falls below 30%, attention concentrates on 
nearby charging stations (𝐻 = 1 .43 nats), reducing route length by 
6.2% and eliminating 83% of unnecessary charging detours.
Uncertainty-Aware Energy Prediction (3.9 pp):
Calibrated uncertainty enables context-adaptive charging strategies. 
Deterministic models use fixed 20% battery margins regardless of route 
context. The uncertainty-aware policy adapts margins dynamically: 
highway segments with high variance (𝜎2𝐸 = 12 .4 kWh2, CV = 38%) 
reserve 28%–32% battery; urban segments with low variance (𝜎2𝐸 = 3.8
kWh2, CV = 22%) accept 18%–22% margins. This reduces charging 
time by 17.3% while decreasing stranding risk from 8.2% to 0.7%.

ance term 𝜎2𝑐𝑢𝑚,𝑖 accounts for uncertainty accumulation along multi-
segment routes. Routes with many high-variance segments accumu-
late larger total uncertainty, triggering earlier preemptive charging.

5.3.2. Component interactions
GAT and uncertainty-aware prediction exhibit additive contribu-
tions (6.4 + 3 .9 = 10 .3 pp combined), indicating orthogonal mech-
anisms: GAT improves spatial reasoning while uncertainty modeling 
enhances temporal risk assessment. However, uncertainty-aware pre-
diction shows dependency on GAT: when paired with MLP encoders, 
uncertainty modeling contributes substantially less than the 3.9 pp 
it provides with GAT. This arises because effective use of confidence 
intervals requires spatially adaptive decisions — recognizing when to 
route toward charging stations based on cumulative variance — which 
MLP encoders cannot exploit without relational reasoning capacity.
5.4. Model interpretability analysis
Fig.  2 visualizes GAT attention weight distributions under varying 
battery states, confirming the adaptive behavior identified in the abla-
tion analysis: broad attention across distant customers at high battery 
(>70%) shifting to concentrated focus on nearby charging stations at 
low battery (<30%), with dynamic evolution across construction steps 
from exploration to exploitation.
Energy prediction calibration (Fig.  3) confirms that predicted 95% 
confidence intervals contain 94.7% of actual observations across 1000 
test routes, with heteroscedastic behavior: highway segments exhibit 
wider intervals (CV = 38%) while urban segments show narrower 
intervals (CV = 22%). The prediction error distribution (MAPE = 3.8%) 
is consistent with the approximately Gaussian assumption underlying 
the chance-constrained formulation. We note, however, that formal 
distributional diagnostics — quantile–quantile plots, normality tests 
(e.g., Shapiro–Wilk), and stress tests under heavy-tailed or multimodal 
deviations — were not performed in the present study; we identify 
these as a limitation and a priority for future work (Section 7).
5.5. Key findings
Taken together, the computational experiments establish three con-
clusions that motivate the real-world deployment study. First, integrat-
ing calibrated uncertainty quantification with learned routing policies 
produces consistent improvements over the learning baselines, con-
firming that neither component alone is sufficient. Second, the per-
formance advantage is substantially larger under dynamic operational 
conditions than under static benchmarks, suggesting that uncertainty-
awareness becomes increasingly valuable as real-world variability in-
creases. Third, the ablation analysis reveals that each architectural 
component addresses a distinct failure mode, with no single component 
dominating; this orthogonality suggests the framework is robust rather 
than dependent on any one design choice.
6. Real-world deployment and sustainability impact
Computational validation establishes technical superiority on
benchmark instances drawn from the same distribution as training 
data. Real-world deployment provides a more demanding test: the 
framework must transfer effectively to an unseen operational domain 
with different traffic patterns, charging infrastructure, and energy 
distributions. We deployed our framework with a mid-sized logistics 
operator managing 25 electric vehicles (150 kWh, 3-ton capacity) over 
13 weeks, serving 60–80 daily customers across 450 km2 with 12 
charging stations. Transfer learning reduced model adaptation from 
72 to 4 h using 423 historical routes, confirming that the three-
stage protocol successfully bridges the domain gap. Integration with 
existing transportation management systems proceeded through four 
phases: infrastructure setup (6 weeks), system training (4 weeks), pilot 
expansion (8 vehicles, 4 weeks), and full deployment (25 vehicles, 4 
weeks).

13-week deployment revealed practical limitations that are important 
to report.

solution, particularly for large instances (80–100 customers) where the

instances (Table  3), rising to 4.1% on the largest instances (80–100 
customers; Table  6), should be interpreted as an average across in-
stances where refinement succeeds, not a guarantee across all problem 
sizes. Practitioners should treat the MILP phase as a best-effort offline 
enhancement rather than a reliable quality guarantee.

7. Discussion and conclusions
This work demonstrates that integrating uncertainty-aware deep re-
inforcement learning with mathematical optimization can help address 
the tension between environmental mandates for rapid decarbonization 
and operational complexities that have constrained electric vehicle 
adoption in urban logistics. The convergence of computational per-
formance (8.8% cost reduction over the strongest learning baseline, 
12.2% improvement under dynamic conditions), operational validation 
(approximately €143,000 in projected annual savings, 98.1% on-time 
delivery, 96% planning time reduction), and multi-dimensional sustain-
ability impact (approximate carbon parity with diesel on the current 
grid alongside elimination of local air pollutants, improved driver 
satisfaction) provides evidence that several technological barriers to 
sustainable urban logistics can be addressed through uncertainty-aware 
optimization, while the broader transition also depends on infrastruc-
ture, policy, and market factors that lie beyond the scope of routing. 
The hybrid paradigm — combining neural networks’ pattern recog-
nition with mathematical optimization’s solution quality guarantees

training performance with 93% time reduction demonstrates that the 
model-adaptation component of deployment effort can be substantially 
lowered (cf. Section 6), lowering one barrier to adoption for operators 
who lack machine learning expertise.
This study has limitations that suggest opportunities for extension. 
The driver satisfaction component relied on a small convenience sample 
(𝑛 = 8 ), limiting statistical inference; findings should be interpreted as 
qualitative indicators. The framework assumes homogeneous fleets and 
next-day planning horizons; heterogeneous operations and same-day 
delivery require multi-agent architectures and streaming algorithms. 
Environmental assessment focuses on operational emissions, excluding 
manufacturing and battery lifecycle impacts. The 13-week deployment 
in a single region limits generalizability across different grid com-
positions and seasonal patterns. In addition, the chance-constrained 
feasibility guarantee is conditional on the Gaussian arc-level energy 
model and on the calibration of the learned uncertainty estimator; the 
effect of correlated residuals is analyzed in Section 5.3, whereas rigor-
ous distributional validation of the Gaussian assumption — quantile–
quantile plots, normality tests, and heavy-tailed stress tests — was not 
performed in the present study and is identified as a priority for future 
work, alongside the conservative correction and the joint-uncertainty 
models noted below. Future research could address these limitations 
through multi-agent extensions for heterogeneous fleets, streaming ar-
chitectures for same-day dynamic requests, explicit modeling of charger 
congestion and queueing at shared stations, hierarchical reinforcement 
learning for infrastructure-routing co-optimization, joint correlated-
uncertainty modeling (route-level Gaussian processes or copula-based 
dependence structures, complemented by Bonferroni-style aggregation 
of the individual chance constraints into a conservative joint route-
level guarantee), and comprehensive lifecycle frameworks integrating 
manufacturing, degradation, and circular economy considerations.
