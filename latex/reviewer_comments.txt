Handling Editor:
We received reports from two reviewers, both of whom raised critical concerns about the clarity of the manuscript and the appropriateness of the benchmarks used in the experiments. As someone who works on optimization for transportation networks, I read the paper myself and found a more fundamental problem that the reviewers did not identify. The paper claims to fill two main gaps. First, it argues that unlike eVRP, which solves the routing problem for a single vehicle, eTFRP must solve the problem for an entire fleet, requiring trucks to compete for limited charging resources. This is incorrect. There is extensive literature on fleet-level eVRP (e.g., bus fleets and ride-sharing fleets), including studies proposing reinforcement learning methods. Second, the paper presents operational uncertainty as a key challenge it addresses, but this too is well-studied, and published works have tackled it in comparable settings. Beyond these two claims, I do not see what genuinely new components the proposed DRL framework introduces over prior work. Given these concerns, I therefore have to reject the manuscript.


Reviewer #1: The manuscript addresses the problem of joint routing and charging decisions for electric trucks under operational uncertainty. It proposes GraphPPO, a learning-based framework that encodes shared charging resources, stochastic travel time, stochastic energy consumption, nonlinear CCCV fast charging, vehicle states, remaining delivery tasks, charging-station queues, and feasible action sets into heterogeneous state graphs and action graphs, combined with feasibility masking and PPO training. The paper further develops an event-driven simulation environment and compares GraphPPO with mathematical optimization, a heuristic method, PPO, and MaskPPO across different fleet sizes, task settings, and a single-truck eVRP case. Overall, the topic is practically relevant, and the attempt to combine GNNs, reinforcement learning, charging queues, and electric truck routing is valuable. However, the current manuscript has serious weaknesses in problem definition, methodological precision, baseline fairness, metric interpretation, and the strength of the conclusions. The reported evidence is not yet sufficient to support the paper's central claims about the intrinsic advantage of GraphPPO for realistic electric truck routing. My specific comments are as follows:
1. In the main eTFRP experiments, delivery points are pre-assigned to trucks, and in most cases the delivery order is fixed. Trucks are also not necessarily required to return to the depot. Therefore, the problem being solved is closer to energy-feasible execution and charging selection under a given task sequence than to full fleet routing, customer assignment, or route construction. The manuscript repeatedly frames the task as electric truck fleet routing, but many of the most difficult combinatorial decisions have already been removed. This weakens the scope of the claimed contribution.
2. The reported results show that MaskPPO is already close to GraphPPO in several settings, indicating that much of the performance gain may come from invalid-action masking rather than from the heterogeneous graph representation or action-graph architecture. The current experiments compare PPO, MaskPPO, and GraphPPO, but they do not isolate the effects of state-graph encoding, action-graph encoding, global pooling, active-truck embedding, and feasibility masking. The evidence therefore supports only the weaker claim that a masked structured policy outperforms generic PPO, not the stronger claim that the proposed GraphPPO architecture is itself the decisive factor.
3. The paper inconsistently uses band eto denote edge energy consumption; the clipping bounds in Eq. (2) are unclear; the notation in the charging integral in Eq. (7) is confusing; Eq. (19) appears to concatenate the receiver embedding twice, h_v ⊕▒h_v , rather than using sender and receiver embeddings; and Eq. (22) for the action-graph GCN update sums over h_arather than neighbor embeddings.
4. The paper states that Math. Opt. is solved conservatively and does not fully account for stochasticity. Moreover, its success rate drops substantially in larger settings, reaching only 59.5% in the 100T3S case. Under such conditions, a normalized reward equal to or greater than one does not demonstrate proximity to the true optimum; it only shows that GraphPPO performs better than a conservative and partially mismatched planner. The manuscript's repeated use of this benchmark to justify "near-optimal" or optimization-level performance is therefore overstated.
5. In Table 3, the heuristic method has a 0% success rate in 30T3S, 50T3S, and 100T3S, while still retaining a normalized reward of around 0.80. PPO also receives nontrivial rewards despite zero success in most multi-truck settings. This indicates that the reward function is strongly affected by partial deliveries, travel time, and penalty weights, and cannot be interpreted straightforwardly as scheduling quality or operational reliability. If a method can completely fail to produce feasible schedules while still achieving a relatively high normalized reward, then average reward is an insufficient primary indicator of method performance.
6. The heuristic baseline is only loosely described, the claim that PPO is the most capable discrete-action RL algorithm is unsupported, and the failure of MaskPPO at scale is not clearly attributed to algorithmic limitations, state-representation mismatch, insufficient training, or hyperparameter choices. For electric vehicle routing, stronger and more relevant baselines exist, including attention-based neural combinatorial optimization, constructive policies, local-search-augmented learning methods, and stronger hybrid heuristics. As a result, the conclusion that GraphPPO consistently outperforms baselines is valid only within the limited baseline set chosen in this paper.
7. The zero-shot experiment uses only one GraphPPO policy trained on 100T3S and evaluates it against the same conservative Math. Opt. reference, with only 50 random scenarios per setting. This does not establish robust generalization across different regions, charging networks, vehicle specifications, demand distributions, or operational policies. In addition, the reported high win ratios in large-scale settings are partly obtained when the Math. Opt. benchmark itself has degraded success rates. The evidence supports a narrower claim: GraphPPO performs well under the manuscript's own simulation distribution and benchmark design. It does not yet support a broad claim of robust generalization or superior real-world large-fleet dispatching capability.



Reviewer #2: Overall summary of the paper
This paper studies the electric truck fleet routing problem (eTFRP) under shared charging infrastructure and operational uncertainty. The authors formulate the problem as an event driven semi Markov decision process (SMDP) with stochastic travel times, energy consumption, and nonlinear charging dynamics. They propose a learning based framework called GraphPPO, which combines graph neural networks with a feasibility aware action mask to handle variable action spaces. Experiments comparing GraphPPO against other benchmarks show that GraphPPO achieves near optimal performance and outperforms the baselines in terms of solution quality, success rate, and scalability. Overall, I recommend major revision. My main concerns are twofold: (1) the model and algorithm require significantly more detailed explanation, and (2) the comparisons need to be more comprehensive (e.g., against state of the art attention based or transformer based RL methods, or against heuristics from closely related papers). Below I detail my suggestions for improvement.

Major concerns
1. On Page 1, the definition of eTFRP is somewhat confusing. Is this a well known problem in the field? The key difference from eVRP (electric vehicle routing problem) only becomes clear later - the main distinction is the shared charging resource. I strongly suggest clarifying the definition and core characteristics explicitly in the Introduction, so readers can immediately understand what makes eTFRP different from classic eVRP.
2. What exactly does "operational uncertainty" refer to? Is it demand uncertainty, travel time uncertainty, congestion uncertainty, or a combination? The paper would benefit from a more detailed explanation of the nature and sources of uncertainty early in the introduction.
3. The mathematical formulation does not include an explicit constraint that every customer (delivery) must be served. What happens if a customer is not served? Is there a penalty? Please clarify or add the necessary constraints.
4. In Section 4.1.3, the phrase "including stochastic edge traversing" appears in the description of Δt_i. What exactly does "stochastic edge traversing" refer to? Please rephrase or define the term clearly.
5. The reward function (18) uses coefficients λ₁, λ₂, λ₃, but only signs are given (λ₁>0, λ₂>0, λ₃<0). The actual numerical values used in experiments are missing. Please provide them for reproducibility.
6. In Section 5, charging actions are discretised into 1 hour steps (H = {1,…,12} hours). In reality, however, fast charging can take only 10 30 minutes, and a full charge may require less than one hour for many trips. This coarse discretisation may affect the quality of the learned policy. Please discuss the impact or provide a sensitivity analysis.
7. The optimisation model used as a benchmark is not sufficiently described. How does it handle queueing at charging stations? The authors say it "does not fully account for stochasticity" - does it use worst case queueing, expected values, or ignore queues? Is it an offline one shot planning model or an online rolling horizon framework? Please provide a detailed formulation (e.g., in an appendix) so readers can understand exactly what is being compared.
8. The rule based heuristic used in Section 5.2 is only vaguely described. Moreover, the paper cites several heuristic methods in the literature (e.g., [5] and [11]) that address similar problems. Have the authors compared against those specific heuristics? The proposed GraphPPO method appears general enough to be applied to those problem variants as well; such a comparison would better highlight the advantages of GraphPPO.
9. Also, for RL approaches, the paper compares only generic PPO and MaskPPO. However, recent years have seen attention based models (e.g., [18, 31]) and transformer based methods (e.g., [23]) for eVRP. Some of these might be stronger baselines. The authors should either include comparisons with such methods or provide a clear justification why those state of the art RL approaches cannot be readily applied to the eTFRP setting.

Minor issues
1. In Section 2.3.1, "The attention model of [18] demonstrated ..." should be "The attention model of [18] demonstrates ..." to maintain consistent present tense usage in the literature review.
2. In Equation (12), a comma is missing.
3. Please check the captions of all figures. Capitalisation style should be consistent across all figures (e.g., sentence case vs. title case). For example, all the first letter of each title word in Figure 3 should be capitalized; and in Figure 5(c), "c. Actor Network head" should be "(c) Actor Network Head".
4. In Table 2, the formula shown for energy consumption is actually the expression for the coefficient ξ, not the final energy consumption {\widetilde{e}}_{uv}. Please revise the table to avoid confusion.
