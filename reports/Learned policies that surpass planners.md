# Follow the plan, deviate only on evidence

The method most likely to make the graph policy match its planner in every category and beat it where room exists is a **plan-default action with gated deviation**: "execute what the planner's current plan prescribes" becomes one explicit member of the variable action set, the policy starts as the planner itself instead of as a clone of it, and PPO learns deviations that must clear a margin on their estimated advantage over the planner. It ranks first because it equals the planner by construction wherever the gate stays closed, keeps the event-driven MDP and the variable-action graph PPO policy as the decision maker, adds no decision-time compute, and was not found in the routing literature. Exact "never worse than the base policy" theorems exist only for rollout, policy switching and value-gated mixtures, and only with exact base-policy values; with a learned critic the claim becomes a paired statistical non-inferiority statement per category, and the paper must state it in that form. No fine-tuning recipe delivers parity, because every documented repair of PPO fine-tuning protects the starting clone, and the group's clone is already below the planner (95.5 % against 98.0 % success under hours of service). On gains the literature is blunt: **no method can deliver a noticeable gain on plain long haul (measured ceiling 0.2 to 0.5 % of hours) or on same-day requests (ceiling 1.2 %, measured 0.1 to 0.2 %)**; congested charging offers at most 1.6 % and hours of service 2 to 3.75 %, in both cases mostly from choosing among the three existing planners and not from learning; drayage is the only category with large room, PPO has already taken it, and the open task is to show that the gain survives a planner whose objective has been tuned. The ranking is: gated plan-default first, portfolio switching over the three planners second as its extension, policy-set planner parameters third (and required as a baseline in any case), rollout-verified deviation fourth as an offline instrument, and staged PPO fine-tuning alone last, although its recipe is how the first candidate should be trained.

## Long haul and same-day leave nothing noticeable to win

The figures in the table are the research group's own measurements on its event-driven simulator (25 training problems, 64 scenarios each, five trained policies), not results from the literature. In the first three rows each cell gives the success rate and the operating hours above the best-known reference; the last two rows give on-time services as a fraction of best-known. The headroom column for the first three rows picks, per episode and after the fact, the best of the listed options and reports its hours against the main planner.

| Category | Planner alone | Imitation only | Imitation then PPO | Measured headroom against the main planner |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Long haul | 99.6 %, +0.4 % | 99.8 %, +0.7 % | 99.6 %, +2.0 % | −0.35 % (best of three planners); −0.20 % (planner or policy); −0.51 % (all) |
| Congested charging | 99.2 %, +1.5 % | 99.1 %, +2.1 % | 98.9 %, +3.6 % | −1.43 %; −0.46 %; −1.60 % |
| Hours of service | 98.0 %, +3.2 % | 95.5 %, +4.4 % | 93.0 %, +4.8 % | −2.67 % with 98.8 % success (100 % on held-out problems); −2.02 %; −3.75 % |
| Same-day requests | 0.999 | 0.990 | 0.963 | Planner serves 98.8 % of tasks on time; ceiling +1.2 %; best of planner and policy +0.1 to +0.2 % |
| Drayage | 0.974 | 0.940 | 1.329 | Planner serves 46.9 % on time; with all requests known in advance it adds one or two services per episode; the PPO policy reaches 64 % |

Two properties of these numbers shape everything that follows. First, the hindsight ceilings and the gaps to best-known are almost the same quantity (+0.4 % against −0.51 %, +1.5 % against −1.60 %, +3.2 % against −3.75 %), because both are a virtual best over one portfolio of methods. Neither is an independent bound: the group's proven lower bound on long-haul hours is 8 to 100 % below the planner, and unpenalised perfect-information bounds are documented to be weak, "sometimes a factor of two or more larger" than penalised ones ([Brown and Smith 2022](https://jimsmith.host.dartmouth.edu/wp-content/uploads/2022/04/Information_Relaxation_Review_Paper.pdf)). "No room" therefore means no room that any method in hand has found; the exact branch-and-price code can certify it only on the full-information static instances it solves, and exact references of that kind are what the routing-benchmark literature recommends ([Accorsi et al. 2022](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2109.13983)). Second, the drayage planner gains almost nothing from knowing every request in advance, while the PPO policy serves far more tasks on time than either version of it. The drayage gain is therefore not the value of foresight as that planner measures it; the planner's objective or its search, not its missing information, is the binding limit. This matters for which candidates can work.

**Plain long haul: no method delivers a noticeable gain.** When a strong solver is already within a fraction of a percent, published learned methods do not improve on it. The organisers of the EURO Meets NeurIPS 2022 competition wrote that the static instances were "too small to permit improvements over the HGS baseline", and the ten static finalists finished within 0.085 % of one another, computed from the final table ([Kool et al. 2023](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v220/kool23a/kool23a.pdf)). Anticipatory methods lose to myopic re-optimisation at low dynamism: a learned value function was 1.95 % and 0.98 % worse than the myopic planner at degrees of dynamism 0.3 and 0.5, and its authors state it "does not perform better than myopic" there ([Joe and Lau 2020](https://cdn.aaai.org/ojs/6685/6685-40-9914-1-10-20200521.pdf)). For deterministic charging and rest planning of electric trucks, a rollout on two simple base solutions already reaches an average optimality gap within 1 % in under two seconds ([Bai et al. 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.08895)). The group's ceiling of 0.2 to 0.5 % is also at the edge of what the evaluation can detect. By the standard power calculation (about 7.9·(s/d)² paired scenarios for a mean difference d with paired standard deviation s; standard power arithmetic, not from a cited source, with s = 2 % as an illustration to be replaced by the measured value), a 0.5 % effect needs about 126 paired scenarios and a 0.2 % effect about 790. The realistic target here is parity inside a stated equivalence margin.

**Same-day requests: no method delivers a noticeable gain.** The value of anticipation falls when capacity is ample: it "decreases when the number of vehicles or the arrival rate of requests increases" ([Voccia et al. 2019](https://doi.org/10.1287/trsc.2016.0732)), and "given sufficient resources" a simple parametric rule "is an effective policy" ([Chen et al. 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/1910.11901)). A planner that serves 98.8 % of tasks on time is in that regime. Even on the best-documented dynamic benchmark, where the hindsight gap was 5 to 7 %, the learned winner beat a sampling planner by under two points (5.15 % against 6.97 % and 7.12 %) ([Baty et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2304.00789)), and a sampling method with no learning came within 0.3 to 0.8 % of it ([Lan et al. 2024](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2308.14476)). With a total ceiling of 1.2 %, the reachable fraction is the 0.1 to 0.2 % the group already measures.

**Congested charging: 1.6 % at most, and it comes from planning.** The established recipe is a good plan plus a dynamic response to the observed queue: fixed-route rollout policies that react to queues are certified within 10 % of a dual bound on industry instances ([Kullman et al. 2021](https://doi.org/10.1287/trsc.2020.1018)), and stochastic station queues have been handled by planning with expected waiting times that are tuned adaptively ([Keskin et al. 2021](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cor.2020.105060)). The one learned result is for a single truck without station queues, 4.8 % average energy saving over deterministic re-optimisation ([Basso et al. 2022](https://research.chalmers.se/publication/527406/file/527406_Fulltext.pdf)). No learned policy has been shown to add value over a queue-reactive planner for a multi-vehicle freight fleet, and the group's own split agrees: choosing among planners alone reaches 1.43 of the 1.60 points, the policy alone 0.46.

**Hours of service: 2 to 3.75 % of hours and 0.8 points of success are available, mostly through planner choice.** The literature is thin. The one stochastic treatment is an online rollout with tree search on a rule-based base policy, which beats that weak base by up to 83 % ([Alcaraz et al. 2022](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.tre.2022.102790)). No paper was found that combines hours-of-service rules, charging and stochastic queues with a learned policy, so the group's ceilings are the only guide.

**Drayage: the gain is real and in the published range, but it is an operating-point shift until shown otherwise.** In resource-constrained, highly dynamic problems the literature reports gains of this size: value approximations raise request acceptance by 21 to 27 % over a greedy policy while better routing alone is worth −0.4 to +2.8 % ([Zhang et al. 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2202.12983)), and a learned value function gains 12 to 21 % over myopic re-optimisation at high dynamism ([Joe and Lau 2020](https://cdn.aaai.org/ojs/6685/6685-40-9914-1-10-20200521.pdf)). The group's result, 1.28 to 1.39 times the rolling planner's on-time services, sits at the top of that range and replicates on fresh scenarios. It arrives with 11 to 12 % more operating hours and 5.6 to 6.7 late jobs per episode against 1.5 to 1.7. No published drayage paper reports a hindsight gap or a head-to-head between a learned dispatcher and a strong rolling-horizon optimiser, so the literature can neither confirm nor cap this gain. It does say what a reviewer will ask: whether a re-weighted planner reaches the same point.

## PPO fell below the planner for documented reasons, and the repairs only protect the clone

The group's pattern (a slightly worse copy after imitation, then one gain and several losses after PPO) is what theory and the published record predict. Imitation of an expert is bounded by expert cost plus horizon times classification error, an upper bound relative to the expert and not a route past it ([Ross and Bagnell 2014](https://arxiv.org/abs/1406.5979)). PPO carries no monotonic-improvement guarantee in practice: the trust-region theorem assumes exact advantages and a penalty the authors say "leads to prohibitively small steps" ([Schulman et al. 2015](https://arxiv.org/abs/1502.05477)). In a 2026 preprint on dynamic multi-depot routing with a similar recipe, PPO after cloning had little average effect on one architecture, helped another only with more variability across seeds, and the learned policies "did not outperform the strongest heuristic" ([Ardali and Knapp 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.13799)).

Four causes have direct experimental support. The first is an untrained critic driving the first actor updates: "naively finetuning the actor with a randomly-initialized critic leads to a rapid drop in performance", and in the ablation naive fine-tuning adds 1.6 points of success where a staged scheme adds 9.9 ([Ramrakhya et al. 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2301.07302)). The second is a constant imitation weight, which pins the student at the teacher's level (41.2 against a teacher at 39.7, where a decayed weight reaches about 59 to 61) ([Schmitt et al. 2018](https://arxiv.org/abs/1803.03835)). The third is forgetting on states the policy stops visiting: a retention loss computed on the student's own states "fails completely" to protect them, while cloning on stored expert states was the most effective remedy ([Wołczyk et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.02868)). A constant term should at least hold the starting level, so the group's run that ended below its start despite one points to the first or the third cause, neither of which that term addresses. The fourth is interference across problem families through return scale: "the saliency of a task for the agent increases with the scale of the returns observed in that task", and per-task normalisation lifted a multi-task agent's median score from 59.7 % to 110.7 % ([Hessel et al. 2019](https://arxiv.org/abs/1809.04474)). Drayage, where advantages are large, dominates a shared gradient over long haul, where they are tiny. Gradient-surgery methods are not the answer; a controlled study found that none "consistently outperforms" a tuned sum of losses ([Kurin et al. 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2201.04122)).

The repairs are known and cheap. Fit the critic to the frozen starting policy before any actor update; warm the actor up at a learning rate about ten times smaller with no entropy bonus, since removing either produced collapse in a large-scale study ([Hu et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.16578)); keep a decaying retention loss on planner-visited states; normalise advantages per category. Two further tools fit the group's simulator. Subtracting a baseline that depends on the exogenous input sequence is unbiased and cut policy-gradient variance fifty-fold in a load-balancing example ([Mao et al. 2019](https://arxiv.org/abs/1807.02264)); the planner's return on the same seeded scenario is such a baseline wherever requests and travel conditions do not react to the fleet, which excludes queues at shared chargers. No paper was found that trains PPO on return minus an external expert's return on the same scenario, so this would be new. A one-sided paired t-test on a fixed instance set, used to decide when a baseline policy is replaced ([Kool et al. 2019](https://arxiv.org/abs/1803.08475)), is a ready-made acceptance rule for checkpoints.

None of this meets the requirement. Every repair anchors the policy to the clone, and the clone is 0.3 to 1.2 points of hours and, under hours of service, 2.5 points of success behind the planner. Gains from reinforcement learning also shrink as the imitation policy improves (10.1 points of success at 20,000 demonstrations, 6.3 at 77,000) ([Ramrakhya et al. 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2301.07302)), and they concentrate where the expert is weak: in driving, reinforcement learning alone had a higher failure rate than cloning on all scenarios (6.66 % against 4.35 %) and a lower one only on the hardest tenth ([Lu et al. 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2212.11419)). Constrained formulations do not close the gap either. Success-rate constraints with learned multipliers exist, with absolute thresholds of 0.99 and 0.80 ([Roy et al. 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2112.12228)), but none has been set to a reference policy's own measured rate, and with failures in only a few percent of episodes the multiplier signal is noisy: separating 98.0 % from 95.5 % success with unpaired samples of 500 episodes gives a difference of only about 2.2 standard errors (arithmetic on the group's figures). Parity with the planner needs the planner's own action in the loop, not a better copy of it.

## Never-worse guarantees require the planner's own action in the loop

An exact "no worse than the base policy" statement exists for a small family. Rollout applies, at each decision, the action whose base-policy completion is cheapest; if the base policy is sequentially consistent or sequentially improving, the rollout costs no more than the base, and when that condition fails rollout can be worse. Fortified rollout, which deviates from the best trajectory found so far only when a completed alternative is no worse, is unconditional in deterministic problems ([Bertsekas 2020](https://arxiv.org/abs/2002.07407)). In stochastic problems the property holds for expected cost-to-go and is lost under truncation or approximation, where improvement holds only "within error bounds" ([Bertsekas 2019](https://arxiv.org/abs/1910.00120)). In dynamic vehicle routing the same result reads: "base policies composed of deterministic decision rules lead to rollout policies with performance at least as strong as that of their base policy" ([Ulmer et al. 2019](https://doi.org/10.1287/trsc.2017.0767)). A time-limited or randomised planner is not a deterministic decision rule unless it is seeded and budgeted deterministically. For several base policies, switching at each state to the one with the highest value is no worse than every member, with exact values; with Monte Carlo estimates the probability of choosing correctly approaches one exponentially in the number of sample paths ([Chang 2021](https://arxiv.org/abs/2112.02177)). The group's per-truck, event-driven decisions resemble the one-agent-at-a-time form of rollout, which keeps the improvement property at a cost linear in the number of agents but cannot strictly improve a base policy that is already agent-by-agent optimal ([Bertsekas 2019](https://arxiv.org/abs/1910.00120)).

The "follow the base policy unless deviating pays" rule has three published forms that train with ordinary reinforcement learning. A lazy-MDP adds a default action that defers to the base policy and charges a penalty η for any other action; the result is an ordinary MDP, η acts as the margin, and it was set by sweep ([Jacq et al. 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2203.08542)). A value-gated switch lets the base policy take over when the learner's action is worse by more than ε under the base policy's own value function, and proves that the mixed policy's return is at least the base policy's minus (1 − β)·ε/(1 − γ), with β the intervention rate (the paper's appendix states the bound without the (1 − β) factor); the thresholds were set so that intervention stays below 5 %, and an ensemble-variance term closes the gate where the critic is unsure ([Xue et al. 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.01728)). An advantage-based override by a backup policy with threshold η was trained with PPO ([Wagener et al. 2021](https://arxiv.org/abs/2106.09110)). The link to "no worse than the planner" is a performance-difference identity: a policy whose advantage under a baseline value function is non-negative at every state is at least as good as that baseline ([Cheng et al. 2020](https://arxiv.org/abs/2007.00795)). Putting the planner's value in that identity and the gate bound on top gives the design rule used below; this combination is a synthesis of the cited results, and no single paper states it for an operations-research planner. No paper was found that calibrates the margin from paired simulations in a routing or dispatching problem.

Everything else is weaker. Cost-to-go imitation and multi-oracle methods bound the learner by expert cost plus policy-class error plus regret ([Ross and Bagnell 2014](https://arxiv.org/abs/1406.5979)); batch safe policy improvement gives high-probability bounds that an audit found useful only with very large data ([Scholl et al. 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2208.00724)); residual learning, jump-start and Q-gated action choice give no guarantee, the residual's "never worse" start being an initialisation and not a theorem ([Silver et al. 2018](https://arxiv.org/abs/1812.06298)). The multi-oracle line is the one worth keeping in view: it computes PPO-style advantages against the maximum of several oracles' values and, in its latest form, adds the learner's own critic to that maximum so that the update imitates where an oracle is confidently better and reinforces where the learner is ([Liu et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.01737)). It has not been applied to routing. Max-value ensembles are also "sensitive to slight differences in the values between constituent policies", to the point that tie-breaking can flip the outcome, and the authors of that analysis could not obtain non-trivial return from one active-selection variant after tuning ([Hussing et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.16739)).

The empirical record says where to expect gains. Learned policies that clearly exceeded their teacher did so against weak teachers: a policy stopped before convergence ([Cheng et al. 2018](https://arxiv.org/abs/1805.10413)), hand controllers at 15 to 55 % success ([Silver et al. 2018](https://arxiv.org/abs/1812.06298)). Rollout is the exception: in backgammon it cut the error of neural base players by factors of 4.8 to 6.6, the larger figure flagged by its authors as possibly biased ([Tesauro and Galperin 1996](https://arxiv.org/abs/2501.05407)), and in routing it improved a good a priori solution by 1 to 4 % ([Secomandi 2001](https://doi.org/10.1287/opre.49.5.796.10608)). It is also unaffordable here. At mid-range values of the group's simulator costs (500 decisions per episode, 0.5 s per decision), one exact rollout decision over ten candidate actions costs about 21 minutes for a single sample per action (arithmetic on the group's figures, not a literature result). Published multi-vehicle experience confirms the problem: on fleets of 6 to 20 vehicles, rollouts with ten sample paths took 11 to 12 s per decision on average, up to 158 s, and "are frequently not effective enough to outperform their base policies" ([Zhang et al. 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2202.12983)). Distilling rollout into a network does not keep the property: under a demand shift an imitation network fell to 0.98 to 1.0 on a normalised wait-time scale while rollout held 0.60 to 0.62 ([Garces et al. 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2211.14983)).

## Strong planners are beaten from inside the planner, typically by one to three points

Where learned and classical methods met on neutral ground, the position of the learning decided the outcome. In the 2022 dynamic routing competition the top two entries put learned prizes or priorities inside the routing solver, the entry that trained a network to select requests directly ranked ninth of ten behind six entries with no learning, and the organisers' own learned baselines "did not outperform the greedy baseline"; the winner finished about 3 % ahead of the best entry without learning, computed from the final table ([Kool et al. 2023](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v220/kool23a/kool23a.pdf)). The field's position paper states the division of labour: solvers "search the action space effectively" and reinforcement learning methods "evaluate actions well with respect to the future" ([Hildebrandt et al. 2023](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2103.00507)). A graph policy that scores planner-proposed actions is on the right side of that line; a policy that re-derives the plan through per-truck choices is not. The same benchmark warns about architecture claims: replacing a small feed-forward network with a graph network moved the gap by 0.11 points, while the feature set moved it by up to 8.7 ([Baty et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2304.00789)).

The cheapest inside-the-planner method is a parametric cost function approximation: the deterministic planner with a few tunable parameters (buffers, reserves, penalties, bonuses for waiting loads), tuned in the simulator, at the same decision-time cost as the plain planner. The default planner is one parameter setting. Tuned policies beat the default by 20 to 50 % in an energy-storage study ([Powell and Ghadimi 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2201.00258)); in another, three parameterisations gained 13 to 26 % and a fourth returned exactly the default, 0 %, which is the fallback working ([Perkins and Powell 2017](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1703.04644)). The one routing precedent that keeps PPO in charge has the network output a single state-dependent planner parameter and improves on the best fixed value by almost 8 % ([Stein et al. 2024](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2409.01815)). The guarantee is narrow: no loss on the training scenarios if only paired improvements are accepted, nothing out of sample, and the tuning objective is non-convex and noisy.

Hindsight methods are the other inside route, and the group's own measurement argues against them. Hindsight learning replaces Monte Carlo returns in a policy-gradient method with the values a full-information planner assigns to each action, and converges to a surrogate policy whose regret is bounded by a problem-specific "hindsight bias"; cloning hindsight-optimal actions directly is biased because the clairvoyant never hedges ([Sinclair et al. 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2207.06272)). The competition winner's authors concluded: "The anticipative strategy might not be the best policy to imitate" ([Baty et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2304.00789)). In drayage the group's planner with all requests known adds one or two services per episode, far below the PPO policy, so a hindsight teacher built from that planner would pull the policy back toward 47 %. A useful teacher would need the adaptive large neighbourhood search or branch-and-price code solving the declared objective from mid-episode states, one solve per labelled action. That is a new entry point and a large labelling bill for the one category where PPO already works, so hindsight learning is not on the shortlist.

This literature also fixes what the paper must contain. The Transportation Science precedents share four elements: a re-optimisation or sampling planner as the main baseline, simple rules as a floor, a hindsight or dual bound as a ceiling, and scale-up or generalisation tests. The critique literature adds equal-compute baselines and paired per-instance tests, a one-tailed Wilcoxon signed-rank test with Bonferroni correction ([Accorsi et al. 2022](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2109.13983)). Reviewers at Transportation Research Parts C and E "show exceptional focus on methodology innovation" ([Wu et al. 2024](https://research.chalmers.se/publication/541243/file/541243_Fulltext.pdf)). Accepted papers with partial dominance present a regime map: a Networks paper reports that combining anticipation with re-optimisation "does not provide significant benefit" over the better of the two and that the right choice "is strongly connected to the degree of dynamism" ([Ulmer 2019](https://doi.org/10.1002/net.21861)). The search behind this report found no editorial guidance from Transportation Research Part C or E or IEEE Transactions on Intelligent Transportation Systems, so this standard is inferred from precedent in neighbouring journals.

## Five candidates on the same seven criteria

The five candidates below are compared on mechanism, guarantee relative to the planner, what has to be built, compute, main risks and fit with the paper's core contributions; the seventh criterion, expected gain per category, follows in one table across all five. The budget frames the comparison: at about 2,000 units per training run and 15 to 25 affordable runs, five seeds per arm leaves three to five trained arms in total (arithmetic on the stated budget), so anything that can be tested without a training run should be.

### A. Staged, constrained PPO fine-tuning

| Criterion | Assessment |
|---|---|
| Mechanism | Fit the critic to the frozen clone first; warm the actor up at a learning rate about ten times smaller, no entropy bonus; decaying retention loss on planner-visited states; per-category advantage normalisation; the planner's return on the same scenario as baseline; per-category success constraint with a learned multiplier; accept a checkpoint only if a paired test finds it no worse |
| Guarantee and assumptions | None relative to the planner. The acceptance test gives statistical non-inferiority to the clone, which is itself below the planner |
| What has to be built | Critic-only phase, loss schedule, normaliser, cache of planner returns per scenario, multiplier controller |
| Training and decision-time compute | One run per seed; decision time unchanged |
| Main risks | Ends at clone level at best; PPO raises variance across seeds; multipliers driven by failures in a few percent of episodes are noisy |
| Fit with core contributions | Complete, but it adds no new idea against the novelty objection |

### B. Plan-default action with gated deviation

| Criterion | Assessment |
|---|---|
| Mechanism | The action the current plan prescribes is one scored member of the variable action set and is executed exactly. The policy is initialised to choose it, so the critic's warm-up fits the planner's value. PPO then learns deviations under a penalty or margin on estimated advantage over the planner, with an ensemble-variance term that closes the gate where the critic is unsure. The margin is calibrated per category by a paired non-inferiority test |
| Guarantee and assumptions | Gate closed: identical to the planner, by construction. Exact planner values and threshold ε: return at least the planner's minus (1 − β)·ε/(1 − γ). Learned critic: holds only up to critic error, so the claim is a paired statistical one. Assumes every decision event has a feasible plan-prescribed action |
| What has to be built | Plan-prescribed action as a candidate; planner-initialised policy; planner-value ensemble; gate and margin sweep; paired per-category test; A's training recipe |
| Training and decision-time compute | One run per seed. A decision-time gate around an already trained policy, with the planner value regressed on logged planner episodes, needs no training run. Decision time unchanged: the plan is already computed and fed to the policy at every decision |
| Main risks | The gate opens only in drayage and the hybrid reads as "the planner plus a switch"; critic error on rare failures opens the gate wrongly under hours of service; published gating results are against learned, imperfect teachers in control and driving tasks, none against a near-optimal routing planner |
| Fit with core contributions | High: one more action in the variable set, PPO and the MDP unchanged; the policy becomes a learned evaluator of planner-proposed actions, the combination the routing position paper asks for |

### C. Portfolio switching over the three planners and the policy

| Criterion | Assessment |
|---|---|
| Mechanism | One value head per planner, fitted on episodes in which the learner rolls in and a planner finishes. Either a per-episode selector picks the planner with the highest predicted value, or each planner's prescribed action becomes a default action in B and advantages are computed against the maximum of the members' values, the learner's included |
| Guarantee and assumptions | Exact values: no worse than every member. Estimated values: within the estimation error of the best member; ties and small errors can flip choices, so the selector needs a tolerance and consistent tie-breaking |
| What has to be built | Per-planner value heads; mid-episode hand-over to a planner; selector; for the per-decision form, every planner callable from any state |
| Training and decision-time compute | Per-episode selector: regression on logged planner episodes, no training run, decision time unchanged. Per-decision form: about one extra episode per value sample, and up to three planner calls per decision |
| Main risks | No routing application exists; value estimates dilute as members are added; one independent attempt to reproduce an active-selection variant failed; applies only to the three long-haul categories |
| Fit with core contributions | High as an extension of B; low as an external selector, which is an algorithm-selection result and not a policy result |

### D. Policy-set planner parameters

| Criterion | Assessment |
|---|---|
| Mechanism | The planner keeps the decision. A fixed vector first, then a small policy head, sets a few parameters per replanning event: holding bonus per open request, lateness-penalty multiplier, charger-wait buffer, charge reserve, rest-time buffer, horizon. The default setting reproduces the planner |
| Guarantee and assumptions | Default is a special case. No worse on the training scenarios if only paired improvements are accepted; no published out-of-sample guarantee |
| What has to be built | Parameter interface in each planner; fixed-parameter sweep; extra action head for the state-dependent version |
| Training and decision-time compute | Fixed sweep: evaluation episodes only. State-dependent: one run per seed. Decision time unchanged |
| Main risks | The network becomes a parameteriser; tuning is non-convex and noisy; a tuned planner may reproduce the drayage gain without any learning, which would remove the policy's one clear win |
| Fit with core contributions | Medium to low as the method; essential as a baseline |

### E. Rollout-verified deviation

## Conclusion: the ranking, and the order in which to spend the budget

**B ranks first.** It is the only candidate that satisfies all three constraints together: parity by construction in the categories where the literature and the group's ceilings agree that nothing noticeable can be won, the drayage gain kept because the gate opens where deviations have positive advantage under the declared reward, and an unchanged decision maker. It also converts the earlier submission's weakness into its claim. The contribution stops being "a graph network trained by imitation and PPO", which the surveyed record suggests reviewers will read as a standard recipe, and becomes a calibrated rule for when a learned policy may overrule an operations-research planner, together with a map of where that rule opens. It also answers the generalisation objection in the one way the record supports: off the training distribution, where a network that amortises a better policy degrades, a closed gate returns the planner, and the held-out and heavy problems test exactly that. **C ranks second** because the largest measured long-haul ceilings (1.43 % on congested charging, 2.67 % and 0.8 points of success under hours of service) come from planner choice, and C reaches them with the value heads B already needs. **D ranks third** as a method and first as an experiment: its fixed-parameter form is the baseline reviewers will demand, and it decides what the drayage result means. **E ranks fourth**: it carries the strongest theorem and the strongest record against strong base policies, and it is unaffordable on this simulator except as an audit on sampled states of how often an opened gate was right. **A ranks last** as a stand-alone method because it cannot reach the planner, and it is the training recipe for B.

The order of work follows from the budget. Three experiments need no training run and should come first: a fixed-parameter sweep of the drayage planner's objective, reported as a frontier of on-time services against operating hours and late jobs with the PPO policy plotted on it; a per-episode planner selector for the three long-haul categories; and a decision-time gate wrapped around the five existing PPO policies. Their results set the claim before a single unit is spent on training. If no planner setting reaches the policy's drayage point, B carries a strict, defensible gain; if one does, the state-dependent form of D becomes the route to exceeding the planner and the paper's centre moves toward the planner. Then B is trained at five seeds, and the remaining two to four arms go to the ablations the rejection asked for: the gate removed (which is A), and the graph network replaced by a feed-forward network on the same features, with the published warning that the second may show the architecture contributes little to cost and must be defended on variable fleet size and transfer instead. Two things stay unknown and should be written as such: no published result shows a gated policy holding a gain of this size against a tuned near-optimal planner, and without an independent bound the statement "at the limit" in three categories rests on a saturated portfolio, which exact solutions on small instances can support but not prove at scale.
