# Headroom above planners, the learned-versus-classical record, and evaluation standards

Verification legend used on every finding: **[V]** = read in the primary text or abstract this session; **[S]** = taken from a search-result summary or indexed abstract, primary text not opened; **[M]** = bibliographic detail supplied from prior knowledge, not re-fetched. Numbers marked "computed" are my arithmetic on a cited table. Preprints are labelled as such. Scope: published work up to November 2026.

## 1. What does the literature say about the size of the gap between strong rolling-horizon re-optimisation and the perfect-information bound (dynamic pickup-and-delivery, same-day delivery, drayage, electric-vehicle routing with charging uncertainty)?

### Takeaway
Where a bound is reported, good dynamic policies sit roughly 5 to 12 % from a perfect-information (hindsight) reference and naive policies 20 to 75 % away, but the unpenalised hindsight bound is documented to be loose, so those figures are a ceiling on headroom, not the headroom. Inside that gap, the measured difference between a learned policy and a strong sampling or rolling-horizon planner is about 2 to 3 percentage points; I found no published hindsight gap at all for drayage and only one bounded result for electric routing with charging queues.

### Cited Findings

**Same-day delivery and dispatch waves**
- Klapp, Erera and Toriello, "The Dynamic Dispatch Waves Problem for Same-Day Delivery" (preprint dated 7 Oct 2016; journal version EJOR 2018 [M]): on 720 instances (25, 35, 50 orders), "On average, the AP policy's cost is 23.1% over the perfect information bound"; rolling the a priori plan out "cuts the average gap by 47.7%, with an average cost reduction of 9.1%", leaving a 12.1 % gap. [V] — [optimization-online preprint](https://optimization-online.org/wp-content/uploads/2016/10/5667.pdf)
- Same paper: the bound is a sample average of deterministic optimal costs over simulated realisations, and the gap grows with the number of orders, which "may be related to an increase in the problem's size and complexity, but also to the lower bound's tightness"; gaps also rise with arrival variability "possibly due to a deterioration of our lower bound". The authors therefore do not read the 12.1 % as true suboptimality. [V] — [optimization-online preprint](https://optimization-online.org/wp-content/uploads/2016/10/5667.pdf)
- Same paper: the dynamic policy is also less variable (gap standard deviation 3.7 % versus 7.4 % for the a priori policy), and the benefit of dynamic policies is concentrated in instances with more variability and less information known at the start. [V] — [optimization-online preprint](https://optimization-online.org/wp-content/uploads/2016/10/5667.pdf)
- Related work from the same group (request acceptance in same-day delivery; Klapp's dissertation) reports a rollout policy cutting the a-priori-to-perfect-information gap "by more than half" and a dynamic policy 21 % above the perfect-information bound, with an approximate-linear-programming bound as a second reference. [S] — [Request Acceptance in Same-Day Delivery](https://optimization-online.org/wp-content/uploads/2019/08/7339.pdf); [dissertation](https://repository.gatech.edu/server/api/core/bitstreams/3b3c3f1f-2d7b-4d74-8f16-ced04be3cbbf/content)

**Dynamic vehicle routing with time windows and dispatch epochs (closest public analogue to dynamic requests)**
- Baty, Jungel, Klein, Parmentier and Schiffer (2024), Transportation Science 58(4):708-725, DOI 10.1287/trsc.2023.0107, arXiv:2304.00789: gaps to an anticipative (full-information) baseline on 25 test instances with 20 seeds each: learned pipeline 5.15 %, Monte-Carlo sampling policy 6.97 %, rolling-horizon 7.12 %, greedy 20.99 %, random 36.29 %, lazy 74.05 %. [V] — [ar5iv full text](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2304.00789)
- Same paper: the anticipative reference is produced by a heuristic (hybrid genetic search, 3600 s) on the full-information instance, so it is a strong hindsight solution, not a proven bound; varying the quality of that target (best of 10 seeds 6.18 %, 3600 s 5.15 %, 900 s 4.79 %, 60 s 5.81 %) shows no clear trend. [V] — [ar5iv full text](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2304.00789)
- Kool et al. (2023), "The EURO Meets NeurIPS 2022 Vehicle Routing Competition", PMLR 220:35-49: final dynamic costs were 348,831.56 (1st, learned prizes inside a prize-collecting solver, trained to imitate the anticipative oracle), 358,161.36 (2nd, RL-trained priority networks inside the solver), 359,270.09 (3rd, scenario sampling with the static solver as oracle, no learning), 361,803.57 (4th, hand-defined penalties), down to 370,670.53 (9th, RL request selection). [V] — [PMLR paper](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v220/kool23a/kool23a.pdf)

**Electric-vehicle routing with public charging queues, and electric ride-hailing**
- Kullman, Goodson and Mendoza (2021), "Electric Vehicle Routing with Public Charging Stations", Transportation Science 55(3):637-659, DOI 10.1287/trsc.2020.1018: policies anticipate station queue dynamics (optimal static policy and fixed-route rollout policies); a decomposition yields dual bounds; on real industry instances the policies are within 10 % of a dual bound. Keywords include information relaxation and information penalties. [V abstract] — [RePEc record](https://ideas.repec.org/a/inm/ortrsc/v55y2021i3p637-659.html); [INFORMS page](https://pubsonline.informs.org/doi/fpi/10.1287/trsc.2020.1018)
- The earlier working-paper version reported the policies within 5 % of optimal in the majority of instances and within 11 % on average. [S] — [HAL record](https://hal-univ-tours.archives-ouvertes.fr/hal-01928730v1)
- Kullman, Cousineau, Goodson and Mendoza (2022), "Dynamic Ride-Hailing with Electric Vehicles", Transportation Science 56(3):775-794, DOI 10.1287/trsc.2021.1042: deep Q-learning policies are compared "against a reoptimization-based policy and against dual bounds on the value of an optimal policy, including the value of an optimal policy with perfect information" obtained with a Benders-based decomposition; the best learned policy outperforms re-optimisation across instance sizes and can be deployed on larger instances without retraining. [V abstract] — [RePEc record](https://ideas.repec.org/a/inm/ortrsc/v56y2022i3p775-794.html)
- A later preprint describes the Kullman et al. baseline as "a myopic reoptimization approach", which matters for how strong that win is. [V] — [Cordeiro and Pitombeira-Neto, arXiv:2307.07508](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2307.07508)
- Dai, Wu and Zhang (2025 preprint), "Atomic Proximal Policy Optimization for Electric Robo-Taxi Dispatch and Charger Allocation", arXiv:2502.13392: with 300 vehicles on Manhattan data the learned policy reaches 91 % of a fluid linear-programming upper bound, against 71 % for power-of-k dispatch and 43 % for the fluid policy; the authors state "This ratio is a lower bound of the optimality ratio achieved by Atomic-PPO algorithm." [V] — [arXiv html](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.13392v2)

**Truckload, pickup-and-delivery and drayage (value of information)**
- Zolfagharinia and Haughton (2014), "The benefit of advance load information for truckload carriers", Transportation Research Part E: access to second-day and third-day advance load information improves profit by 22 % and 6 % on average in a rolling-horizon setting; the effect depends on service radius and trip length and is statistically independent of load density and fleet size. [S] — [TRID abstract](https://trid.trb.org/view/1327051)
- Zhang, Smilowitz and Erera (2011), "Dynamic planning for urban drayage operations", Transportation Research Part E: sampling possible future task requests at each decision epoch finds better plans than a deterministic rolling horizon when a larger fraction of tasks is unknown at the start of the day; no percentage gap to a hindsight solution was visible. [S] — [ScienceDirect record](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S1366554511000202)
- Máhr, Srour, de Weerdt and Zuidwijk (2010), "Can agents measure up? A comparative study of an agent-based and on-line optimization approach for a drayage problem with uncertainty", Transportation Research Part C 18(1):99-119: the on-line optimisation (a mixed-integer program re-solved every 30 s) is competitive under job-arrival uncertainty and wins under extreme combined uncertainty; the agent-based method wins when service times are highly uncertain. [S] — [TU Delft repository](https://repository.tudelft.nl/record/uuid:f933560a-80a6-4370-a813-025cbf103ebf)
- Berbeglia, Cordeau and Laporte (2010), "Dynamic pickup and delivery problems", EJOR: value of information is defined as the relative gain from knowing all requests in advance, usually approximated by running the same heuristic on the dynamic and the static instance; competitive analysis is the worst-case alternative. [S] — [survey PDF (connection refused this session)](http://neumann.hec.ca/chairedistributique/common/surveyPDP.pdf); [ResearchGate record](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/46491804_Dynamic_pickup_and_delivery_problems)
- Srour, Agatz and Oppen (2018), Transportation Science 52(1):3-19, DOI 10.1287/trsc.2015.0658: a sample-scenario strategy exploits advance time information and "performs well on instances with both short time windows and limited advance confirmation"; only qualitative results were visible. [S] — [ERIM report](https://repub.eur.nl/pub/77187/ERS-2014-015_2-LIS.pdf)

**How loose the hindsight bound is, and how penalties fix it**
- Brown, Smith and Sun (2010), "Information Relaxations and Duality in Stochastic Dynamic Programs", Operations Research 58(4):785-801, DOI 10.1287/opre.1090.0796: relax non-anticipativity and charge a penalty for using future information; weak duality, strong duality and complementary slackness hold, with an ideal penalty built from the optimal value function. [S, M] — [INFORMS page](https://pubsonline.informs.org/doi/10.1287/opre.1090.0796)
- Brown and Smith (2022), review and tutorial, Foundations and Trends in Optimization 5(3):246-339, DOI 10.1561/2400000027: "Without any penalty for using this additional information, the resulting performance bound is often quite weak"; "perfect information bounds with no penalty are often quite weak". [V] — [author copy](https://jimsmith.host.dartmouth.edu/wp-content/uploads/2022/04/Information_Relaxation_Review_Paper.pdf)
- Same review, on Balseiro and Brown (2019): the penalised bound "is typically quite close (within a few percent)" to the policy value, and "with no penalty, the performance bounds are typically quite weak, sometimes a factor of two or more larger than the penalized information relaxation bound". [V] — [author copy](https://jimsmith.host.dartmouth.edu/wp-content/uploads/2022/04/Information_Relaxation_Review_Paper.pdf)
- Same review: routing applications of information relaxation are Goodson, Ohlmann and Thomas (2013, multivehicle routing with stochastic demand) and Kullman et al. (2021); other applications listed are ambulance dispatch, ride-sharing tree search and container repositioning. [V] — [author copy](https://jimsmith.host.dartmouth.edu/wp-content/uploads/2022/04/Information_Relaxation_Review_Paper.pdf)
- Chandrasekaran (July 2026 preprint, single author, not peer reviewed), truckload bid acceptance with hours-of-service: a Lagrangian-per-truck information relaxation is 20.7 % (tight capacity) and 39.3 % (scarce capacity) tighter than a simple linear-programming hindsight ceiling. [V abstract] — [arXiv:2607.07343](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.07343)

### Inferences
- Computed from the competition table: the non-learning scenario-sampling entry (3rd) is 2.99 % behind the learned winner and 0.31 % behind the RL-based runner-up; the other non-learning entries (ranks 4 to 8) are 3.7 to 6.0 % behind the winner; the pure RL request-selection entry is 6.26 % behind. With Baty et al. (5.15 % versus 6.97 to 7.12 %), the best public evidence puts the learned-over-strong-planner margin on a dynamic-request problem at roughly 2 to 3 %, and that margin is one third or less of the remaining hindsight gap.
- The hindsight gap is a necessary but not sufficient test for headroom. A category with a small hindsight gap (expected for long haul with little exogenous randomness) has no room for any policy; a category with a large hindsight gap (expected for dynamic requests and drayage) may still have little reachable room, because the unpenalised bound can be loose by tens of percent (Chandrasekaran) or by a factor of two (Balseiro and Brown as reported by Brown and Smith).
- A planner being 0.4 to 3 % from a best-known solution says nothing about its distance from the hindsight optimum when the best-known solution comes from non-anticipative methods. The two references answer different questions and both need to be in the paper.
- For shared charging queues the literature pattern (Kullman et al. 2021) is: static optimal policy, rollout around fixed routes, and a penalised dual bound, with a reported gap of about 10 %. That is the only precedent for claiming "near the limit" in that category.

### Gaps
- No source found reports the gap between rolling-horizon re-optimisation and a perfect-information solution for port drayage with dynamic requests; the drayage papers found compare methods with each other, not with a bound.
- Quantitative results of Kullman et al. (2022) (percentage over re-optimisation, gap to the perfect-information bound, fleet sizes) could not be read: the publisher page and the HAL copy blocked automated access. Only the abstract-level claims above are verified.
- The figures attributed to Mitrović-Minić, Krishnamurti and Laporte (2004, double-horizon heuristics, 0.64 to 10.36 % route-length improvement over a standard rolling horizon) appeared only in a search snippet and are unverified; the value-of-information tables of that paper were not read.
- No verified hindsight-gap figures were obtained for Yang, Jaillet and Mahmassani (2004) or Tjokroamidjojo, Kutanoglu and Taylor (2006).
- I found no paper that applies penalised information relaxation to trucks with driver hours-of-service rules other than the 2026 single-author preprint above.

## 2. When the baseline is within about 1 % of a best-known solution, what improvement is realistic, and how do authors establish that a result is not noise?

### Takeaway
On static or weakly stochastic problems where a strong solver is already within a fraction of a percent of best known, published learned methods do not improve on it at all, and the organisers of the largest joint competition said so explicitly; realistic gains over a strong planner are zero there and about 1 to 3 % (occasionally 6 % on average) on strongly dynamic problems. The accepted way to show a small gain is real is paired evaluation on common scenarios with a non-parametric paired test or bootstrap interval, multiple training seeds, and a bound that shows the gain is possible.

### Inferences
- Computed from the competition table: the ten static finalists are within 0.085 % of each other while the dynamic finalists (excluding one failed entry) span 6.3 %. A 1 % reference gap on a deterministic class therefore leaves well under 1 % to share among all methods, and a learned policy that "wins" by 0.1 to 0.3 % there needs hundreds of paired scenarios to be distinguishable from seed noise.
- Sample-size logic (standard statistics, not from a cited source): with paired per-scenario differences of standard deviation s, detecting a mean difference d at 5 % significance and 80 % power needs about 7.9·(s/d)² scenarios. If the paired standard deviation is 2 % of cost, a 0.5 % effect needs about 126 scenarios and a 0.2 % effect about 790; unpaired comparison needs several times more. This is why common random numbers are the default in the bounded-policy literature.
- Two variance sources must be separated and both reported: scenario noise (handled by pairing) and training-seed noise (handled by several independently trained policies). The Ardali and Knapp observation that PPO raises seed variability is a warning that a single trained policy cannot support a 1 % claim.
- A realistic target, given the record, is: parity within a stated equivalence margin on deterministic and mildly stochastic classes, and a paired, interval-backed gain of a few percent on the strongly dynamic class, with the bound showing the first is at the limit and the second is not.

### Gaps
- I found no transportation-journal source that prescribes common random numbers or a specific paired test for stochastic dynamic routing; the practice is visible in papers (Klapp et al.) rather than in guidelines.
- Classic computational-testing references (Barr et al. 1995; Rardin and Uzsoy 2001; Coffin and Saltzman 2000; Hooker 1995) were not fetched this session and are not cited as findings.
- The effect sizes in the NDS table and the Routing Arena table need checking against the original PDFs before being quoted.

## 3. Which conditions make learned policies win over planners, and which make planners win?

### Takeaway
Learned components win when the problem is strongly dynamic, future demand has learnable structure, decisions are time-limited, and the learning is placed inside or on top of an optimiser (prizes, value functions, priorities) rather than replacing it. Planners win on static and mildly stochastic problems, under distribution shift and scale-up, and against end-to-end RL over combinatorial action sets.

### Cited Findings

**Planner wins**
- Liu, Zhang, Tang and Yao (2023), IEEE Computational Intelligence Magazine, arXiv:2209.10913: across effectiveness, efficiency, stability, scalability and generalisation, learned solvers "still fall short of traditional solvers in nearly all these aspects"; the one potential advantage is time and energy efficiency on small instances when enough training instances exist. [V abstract] — [arXiv:2209.10913](https://arxiv.org/abs/2209.10913v2)
- Accorsi, Lodi and Vigo (2022): machine-learning routing papers use non-standard instances, weak baselines, unequal hardware and no statistical testing; OR-Tools is "erroneously considered by most ML papers to be among the best open-source VRP solvers"; the hybrid genetic search and FILO outperform LKH-3 on the standard X instances; many learned approaches handle only very small instances. [V] — [ar5iv full text](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2109.13983)
- Feng, Sun, Li, Talwalkar and Yang (2025 preprint, revised February 2026), "FrontierCO": a neural solver that reported a 0.72 % gap on the standard travelling-salesman benchmark shows a 10 % gap on easy and 77 % on hard real-world-structured instances, against 0.03 % for LKH-3 on the easy set; neural solvers ran out of memory on 4 of 8 problems; neural components do improve weak heuristics, and the paper leaves open whether that carries over to strong ones. [V] — [arXiv:2505.16952](https://arxiv.org/html/2505.16952v2)
- Routing Arena: "pure neural search approaches are not competitive" with one exception; neural solver performance declines as instance size grows; hybrid learned-plus-classical methods "deliver better any-time performance on average and in a more stable fashion". [V] — [arXiv:2310.04140](https://arxiv.org/html/2310.04140v1)
- Kool et al. (2023): the organisers' own supervised (oracle-imitation) and DQN request-selection baselines "were for illustrative purposes and did not outperform the greedy baseline"; the finalist that trained an RL network to select requests ranked 9th of 10 on the dynamic track, behind six entries that used no learning (ranks 3 to 8: scenario sampling, hand-defined penalties, scenario-based column generation, proximity rules, an analytic regret rule). [V] — [PMLR paper](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v220/kool23a/kool23a.pdf)
- Hildebrandt, Thomas and Ulmer (2023), Computers and Operations Research 150:106071, arXiv:2103.00507: the difficulty combines "searching an action in a complex, NP-hard mixed-integer program" with evaluating each action under uncertainty; prior work either restricts the action space and leaves routing to heuristics, or treats problems that reduce to resource allocation; no reviewed work handles a detailed action space with complex route constraints. [V] — [ar5iv full text](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2103.00507)
- Xia et al. (2024), ICML position paper: a simple non-learned distance-based heatmap matches or beats most learned heatmaps inside the same tree search for large travelling-salesman instances, questioning what the learned part contributes. Disputed: a comment by the author of one compared method argues the timing comparison and tuning were unfair. [S] — [PMLR 235](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v235/xia24f.html); contested by [arXiv:2406.09441](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.09441)
- Ardali and Knapp (2026 preprint): on dynamic multi-depot routing, learned policies kept millisecond decisions and transferred to 80 requests without retraining but "did not outperform the strongest heuristic"; rolling-horizon optimisation achieved the lowest waiting times and makespan at much higher computational cost. [V abstract] — [arXiv:2608.13799](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.13799)
- Bertsimas, Jaillet and Martin (2019), Operations Research 67(1):143-162, DOI 10.1287/opre.2018.1763: re-optimisation with a backbone algorithm scales to dispatching thousands of taxis serving more than 25,000 customers per hour and improves on existing heuristics, so "the planner cannot scale" is not a safe premise. [S] — [INFORMS page](https://pubsonline.informs.org/doi/10.1287/opre.2018.1763)

**Learned or hybrid wins**
- Kool et al. (2023), dynamic track: "All the best approaches used machine learning as a key component of their winning policies"; the top two entries embed learned prizes or priorities in the routing solver, with a 2-minute limit per decision epoch in the final. [V] — [PMLR paper](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v220/kool23a/kool23a.pdf)
- Baty et al. (2024): the learned pipeline is nearly insensitive to the decision-time limit (5.66 % gap at 30 s, 4.87 % at 240 s), generalises from about 10 training instances, and beats the sampling-based planners; the motivation given is that multistage stochastic methods are too expensive online while RL methods "often struggle when being applied to high-dimensional combinatorial problems". [V] — [ar5iv full text](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2304.00789)
- Kullman et al. (2022): learned value approximations beat re-optimisation for electric ride-hailing where charging and repositioning must anticipate future requests. [V abstract] — [RePEc record](https://ideas.repec.org/a/inm/ortrsc/v56y2022i3p775-794.html)
- Jungel et al. (2025), Sadeghi Eshkevari et al. (2022) and Azagirre et al. (2023): gains come from learned values or learned objective terms inside a matching or dispatch optimisation, not from replacing it. [S, V abstract] — [arXiv:2302.03963](https://arxiv.org/abs/2302.03963); [arXiv:2202.05118](https://arxiv.org/abs/2202.05118); [arXiv:2310.13810](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.13810)
- Máhr et al. (2010): the non-optimisation method wins under high service-time uncertainty, the optimisation under job-arrival uncertainty and under extreme combined uncertainty; a companion workshop paper states the agent approach is competitive when less than 50 % of jobs are known at the start of the day. The type of uncertainty, not only its amount, decides the winner. [S] — [TU Delft repository](https://repository.tudelft.nl/record/uuid:f933560a-80a6-4370-a813-025cbf103ebf); [companion paper](https://repository.tudelft.nl/record/uuid:959411d0-5f02-45d6-ab30-6cfccab86bd5)
- Hottung et al. (2025 preprint): the learned removal policy overtakes classical solvers only as instance size grows under a fixed time budget. [S] — [arXiv:2501.03715](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.03715v2)

### Inferences
- The consistent separator is where the learning sits. Entries that learn a parameter of the planner's objective (prizes, priorities, value-to-go) rank first and second; entries that learn the decision directly rank near the bottom or tie simple rules. A policy that reads the planner's plan and outputs actions is structurally closer to the second group unless the planner's objective itself is what gets adjusted.
- Imitating the non-anticipative planner can at best reproduce it; the winning imitation target in the dynamic competition was the anticipative (hindsight) solution, which carries information the planner lacks. A "slightly worse copy" is the expected outcome of cloning a planner and then fine-tuning with a high-variance policy-gradient method on a problem with little anticipation value.
- More on-time services together with more operating hours and more late jobs is a movement along a trade-off frontier, not necessarily a dominance. The literature that reports such results shows several outcome measures side by side (Klapp et al. report cost, penalty, fill rate and route duration; Ardali and Knapp conclude no method is best across all measures), so the comparison needs either the declared scalar objective or a frontier plot with the planner's weights swept.
- Tight decision time is a real but narrow advantage: it is credited in Liu et al. and in the 2026 preprints, but the dynamic competition allowed 2 minutes per epoch and learned-in-the-loop methods still won, so the stronger argument is anticipation, with speed as a secondary benefit.

### Gaps
- The findings of Ulmer (2019), "Anticipation versus reactive reoptimization for dynamic vehicle routing with stochastic requests", Networks 73(3):277-291, DOI 10.1002/net.21861, could not be retrieved; this is the most directly relevant paper on when anticipation beats re-optimisation and should be read in full.
- The winners and methods of the ICAPS 2021 dynamic pickup-and-delivery competition (152 teams, industrial data) could not be verified; only the benchmark description was found ([arXiv:2202.01256](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2202.01256)).
- I found no controlled study that varies fleet size from tens to a hundred vehicles and reports where a learned dispatcher overtakes re-optimisation.
- The comparison paper on RL policy classes for dynamic routing with stochastic requests attributed to Akkerman, Mes and van Jaarsveld could not be located and may be mis-remembered; it is not cited.

## 4. What baselines and bounds do reviewers of the top transportation journals expect in a paper that proposes an RL policy for dynamic electric fleet routing?

### Takeaway
The published Transportation Science precedents all contain the same four elements: a re-optimisation or sampling-based planner as the main baseline, simple rules as a floor, a perfect-information or penalised dual bound as a ceiling, and real-data instances with scale-up or generalisation tests; the critique literature adds state-of-the-art solvers at equal compute, paired tests and component ablations. I could not open any journal's official computational-testing rules, so this rests on precedent and on critique papers.

### Cited Findings
- Kullman et al. (2022, Transportation Science): baselines and references are a re-optimisation policy plus dual bounds including the perfect-information value by Benders decomposition; instances derived from real Manhattan data; several instance sizes; deployment on larger instances without retraining. [V abstract] — [RePEc record](https://ideas.repec.org/a/inm/ortrsc/v56y2022i3p775-794.html)
- Kullman et al. (2021, Transportation Science): the optimal static policy, rollout policies, the industry-standard strategy (depot charging) and a dual bound from information relaxation with penalties, on real industry instances. [V abstract] — [RePEc record](https://ideas.repec.org/a/inm/ortrsc/v55y2021i3p637-659.html)
- Baty et al. (2024, Transportation Science): anticipative reference; greedy, lazy and random floors; rolling-horizon and Monte-Carlo sampling planners; ablations over feature sets (complete 5.15 %, model-aware only 13.83 %, model-free only 6.78 %), training-set size, imitation-target quality and model class (linear 6.85 %, feed-forward network 5.15 %, sparsified graph network 5.04 %); sensitivity to the decision-time limit. [V] — [ar5iv full text](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2304.00789)
- Same paper: replacing the small feed-forward network by a graph network changes the gap by 0.11 points, while the feature set changes it by up to 8.7 points. [V] — [ar5iv full text](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2304.00789)
- Jungel et al. (2025, INFORMS Journal on Computing): greedy and model-predictive-control baselines, several fleet sizes and request densities on real data, code released in the journal repository. [S] — [INFORMS page](https://pubsonline.informs.org/doi/fpi/10.1287/ijoc.2024.0637)
- Accorsi, Lodi and Vigo (2022): "The comparison should still occur against the best performing algorithms"; run baselines single-threaded or disclose and normalise hardware (PassMark), report the share of time spent in inference, account for training time, analyse the effect of individual components, and test statistically. [V] — [ar5iv full text](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2109.13983)
- Routing Arena: evaluate under both a fixed budget and an any-time protocol; "Comparing total run-times, even on a per-instance level, to evaluate efficiency is not recommended." [V] — [arXiv:2310.04140](https://arxiv.org/html/2310.04140v1)
- Brown and Smith (2022): the purpose of the dual bound is to judge whether "the proposed policy is 'good enough' or if we should continue searching for a better policy, perhaps using more complex ADP techniques." [V] — [author copy](https://jimsmith.host.dartmouth.edu/wp-content/uploads/2022/04/Information_Relaxation_Review_Paper.pdf)
- Wu, Sanchez-Diaz, Yang and Qu (2024), "Why is your paper rejected? Lessons learned from over 5000 rejected transportation papers", Communications in Transportation Research 4:100129, DOI 10.1016/j.commtr.2024.100129: of 5,036 rejected papers, 16.4 % were rejected after review and the rest at the desk; 55 % of desk rejections "were attributed to issues related to topic and scope", which the authors say may mask other reasons such as contribution; topic modelling of reviewer criticism shows "TRB, TRC, and TRE show exceptional focus on methodology innovation". [V] — [Chalmers open copy](https://research.chalmers.se/publication/541243/file/541243_Fulltext.pdf)
- Same study, citing an editor's analysis of 1,000 rejections elsewhere: the main reasons are lack of novel contribution, importance of the findings, scope, "the lack of generalization", appropriateness and execution of the method, and writing. [V] — [Chalmers open copy](https://research.chalmers.se/publication/541243/file/541243_Fulltext.pdf)
- Example of the weak-baseline pattern: an event-based deep RL dispatching preprint compares only with heuristic policies used in practice and reports up to 50 % lower waiting times, with no optimisation baseline and no bound. [V] — [arXiv:2307.07508](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2307.07508)
- Hildebrandt et al. (2023) classify existing RL routing papers by whether they have complex route constraints, enumerate the full action space and use full state information, and find the combination essentially empty; this is the novelty axis a methods reviewer from that community will apply. [V] — [ar5iv full text](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2103.00507)

### Inferences
- Expected package for an RL paper on dynamic electric fleet routing, assembled from the precedents: (a) the rolling re-optimisation planner at the same decision-time budget, and at a generous budget to show its ceiling; (b) a sampling or scenario-based anticipatory planner, because the competition and Baty et al. show it is the real competitor, about 2 points better than nothing and about 2 points worse than the best learned pipeline; (c) myopic and rule-based floors; (d) a hindsight solution per scenario with its solver gap, plus a penalised bound where the plain one is loose; (e) ablations that hold everything fixed except one component: plan-as-input removed, graph network replaced by a small feed-forward network on the same features, imitation only versus imitation plus PPO, and PPO from scratch; (f) generalisation tables over fleet size, demand intensity and held-out problem classes with retraining disallowed; (g) paired statistics over scenarios and several training seeds.
- The earlier rejection reasons map one-to-one onto this literature: "weak baselines" is Accorsi et al.'s main criticism; "ablations that did not isolate the architecture" is answered by the Baty et al. design, which also warns that the architecture may contribute almost nothing (0.11 points); "weak generalisation evidence" is the scale-without-retraining test in Kullman et al. and the distribution-shift collapse in FrontierCO; "limited novelty" is the methodology-innovation focus that Wu et al. measure for Parts B, C and E.
- A graph network with a variable-size action set, imitation then PPO, is by now a standard recipe; on the evidence above the architecture is unlikely to be accepted as the contribution. The defensible contributions are a new way of coupling learning to the planner, a bound-backed map of where headroom exists across problem classes, or the benchmark itself.

## 5. How have successful papers framed "matches the planner on deterministic or mildly stochastic classes and beats it on strongly dynamic classes", and is a hybrid that provably never underperforms its planner regarded as a contribution?

### Takeaway
Accepted papers do not present parity as a result of the learned method; they present a regime map backed by a bound (the planner is at the limit here, there is room there, and the learned component captures part of it), or they move the claim to decision time or generality. Never-worse guarantees are publishable when they are formal and come with a regime of strict improvement, but the available guarantees are either deterministic-trajectory results or worst-case ratio bounds, and a safeguard alone would not answer a novelty objection.

### Cited Findings

**Framings used in accepted work**
- Regime map by uncertainty type (Transportation Research Part C): Máhr et al. (2010) state where each approach wins, including the regimes where the optimisation baseline wins. [S] — [TU Delft repository](https://repository.tudelft.nl/record/uuid:f933560a-80a6-4370-a813-025cbf103ebf)
- Regime statement by competition organisers: static instances "too small to permit improvements over the HGS baseline", dynamic variant showing "very significant progress beyond the baseline strategies". [V] — [PMLR paper](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v220/kool23a/kool23a.pdf)
- Learning enriches the optimiser (Transportation Science): Baty et al. (2024) position the method against both expensive multistage stochastic optimisation and RL that struggles with combinatorial actions, report that it ranked first in the competition, and state openly that the sampling planner is more robust across seeds while theirs "balances the trade-off between robustness and solution quality". [V] — [ar5iv full text](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2304.00789)
- Bound-anchored comparison (Transportation Science): Kullman et al. (2022) place the learned policies between a re-optimisation policy and dual bounds and add scale-up without retraining. [V abstract] — [RePEc record](https://ideas.repec.org/a/inm/ortrsc/v56y2022i3p775-794.html)
- Division of labour (Transportation Research Part B): Jia, Cui, Chen and Meng (2022), 166:237-258, dynamic container drayage with uncertain request arrival times: RL decides in real time whether a request is served immediately, integer programming periodically plans resources for accumulated requests; tested on data from a Singapore drayage operator. [S] — [RePEc record](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/transb/v166y2022icp237-258.html)
- Parity framed as generality (MSOM): Gijsbrechts et al. (2022) present "can match" state-of-the-art heuristics as valuable where tailored heuristics do not exist. [S] — [INFORMS page](https://pubsonline.informs.org/doi/fpi/10.1287/msom.2021.1064)
- Parity framed as speed: the 2026 bid-acceptance preprint claims statistical indistinguishability from the rollout teacher at 40 to 56 % of its latency; Liu et al. identify time and energy efficiency on small instances as the one potential advantage of learned solvers. [V abstract] — [arXiv:2607.07343](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.07343); [arXiv:2209.10913](https://arxiv.org/abs/2209.10913v2)
- Complete-information counterpart as the organising device: Lorenz, Otto and Gendreau (2024 preprint) compute complete-information optimal solutions to quantify "the improvements remaining for advanced anticipation mechanisms", extract which decision patterns the hindsight optimum uses (for example repositioning before 39 to 62 % of orders), and show that adding one such pattern to a myopic policy cuts its gap by 4.3 % on average and up to 14 %. [V abstract] — [arXiv:2410.14316](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.14316)

**Never-worse hybrids**
- Bertsekas (2020), fortified rollout: it follows the base heuristic's trajectory until it finds a better one, and "will generate a complete trajectory that is feasible and has cost that is no worse than the cost of the base trajectory" even when the base heuristic is not sequentially improving; patterned after Bertsekas, Tsitsiklis and Wu (1997). The statement is for deterministic trajectory problems. [S] — [arXiv:2002.07407](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2002.07407); [original 1997 paper](https://www.mit.edu/~jnt/Papers/J066-97-rollout.pdf)
- Goodson, Thomas and Ohlmann (2017), "A rollout algorithm framework for heuristic solutions to finite-horizon stochastic dynamic programs", EJOR 258(1):216-229, DOI 10.1016/j.ejor.2016.09.040: a formalisation of rollout variants with pre- and post-decision states was accepted as a methodological contribution; the companion Operations Research paper applies rollout to multivehicle routing with stochastic demand. [S] — [EJOR record](https://portal.mardi4nfdi.de/wiki/Publication:1698902); [Operations Research 61(1):138-154](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:inm:oropre:v:61:y:2013:i:1:p:138-154)
- Powell and Ghadimi (2022 preprint) and Perkins and Powell (2017 preprint), parametric cost function approximation: keep the deterministic lookahead, add tunable parameters (buffers, reserves, modified costs), and tune them in a simulator of the true stochastic model; the authors claim the parameterised deterministic model "may easily produce higher quality solutions" than stochastic programming at deterministic-model cost. Evidence is the authors' own; no independent head-to-head was found. [S] — [arXiv:2201.00258](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2201.00258); [arXiv:1703.04644](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1703.04644)
- Learning-augmented online algorithms: performance is judged by consistency (near-optimal when predictions are good) and robustness (never much worse than the best prediction-free algorithm); "Online TSP and Online Dial-a-Ride with Predictions" was published in INFORMS Journal on Computing (DOI 10.1287/ijoc.2023.0478), and a NeurIPS 2022 paper gives a robustness factor of at most 3 + 2/α for a trust parameter α. The guarantees are bounds on the competitive ratio, not per-instance dominance. [S] — [arXiv:2206.15364](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2206.15364); [arXiv:2205.12850](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2205.12850)
- Deployed systems use the same structure without a formal guarantee: a learned value term inside the production matching optimiser, evaluated by A/B or switchback experiments. [S, V abstract] — [arXiv:2202.05118](https://arxiv.org/abs/2202.05118); [arXiv:2310.13810](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.13810)

### Inferences
- The framing with the best precedent for a multi-category paper is a headroom map: for each category report the planner's gap to the hindsight or penalised bound, classify categories as "at the limit" or "open", and claim learned gains only in the open ones. Parity in the closed categories then becomes evidence that the bound is right and that the learned policy is safe, not a failed result.
- Three method changes have a published track record of beating a strong planner; all move the learning into the planner: (1) learn objective terms (prizes, penalties, value-to-go) that the planner optimises, trained by imitating hindsight solutions (first place, 5.15 % versus 7.12 %); (2) tune a parameterised version of the deterministic lookahead in the simulator (parametric cost function approximation), which contains the original planner as one parameter setting and so cannot be worse in-sample; (3) use the planner as the base policy of a rollout and let the learned policy propose alternatives that are accepted only when simulated continuation is better. Options 2 and 3 are the ones that carry a never-worse property.
- Limits of the never-worse claim that a reviewer will raise: the fortified-rollout guarantee is deterministic; in a stochastic problem the improvement holds in expectation and only when the comparison of continuations is exact, so with Monte-Carlo evaluation it becomes a statistical statement that needs common random numbers and an acceptance threshold. In-sample containment (option 2) does not guarantee out-of-sample dominance. The paper should state the guarantee in the form it actually holds.
- A safeguard that falls back to the planner makes the hybrid equal to the planner wherever the learned part adds nothing. On its own that reads as "limited novelty". It becomes a contribution when paired with a strict, bound-verified gain in at least one class and an analysis of when the gate opens (how often the learned action replaces the planner's, and in which states), in the style of the complete-information pattern analysis of Lorenz et al.
- The drayage result needs reframing before it can carry the claim: 11 to 39 % more on-time services with more operating hours and more late jobs is an operating-point shift. Showing that no re-weighting of the planner's objective reaches the same point (a frontier comparison), or that the gain survives under the planner's own scalar objective, is what converts it into the "beats the planner where anticipation matters" result that the framing requires.

### Gaps
- I did not find a transportation-journal paper whose headline contribution is a learned-plus-planner hybrid with a proven never-worse property in a stochastic dynamic routing problem; the guarantee literature (rollout, learning-augmented algorithms) and the empirical hybrid literature are largely separate.
- The exact statements of the rollout improvement property and its conditions in Goodson et al. (2017) were not read; the description above rests on the abstract and on Bertsekas's text.
- Numerical results of the Jia et al. (2022) drayage paper were not accessible.
- No examples were found from IEEE T-ITS or Transportation Research Part E that use the regime-map framing with a bound.

## 6. What are best practices for reporting when the best-known reference is itself produced by the baseline family being compared?

### Takeaway
No source addresses this bias head-on, but practice in the bounded-policy literature is clear: a best-known solution is used for primal gaps only when it comes from an independent community record or an exact method, and otherwise authors add a reference that does not depend on any compared method (hindsight optimum with a proven gap, penalised information relaxation, or a fluid bound) and say explicitly that the ratio to it is conservative.

### Cited Findings
- Accorsi, Lodi and Vigo (2022): gaps are computed against best-known solutions held in community repositories (CVRPLIB, VRP-REP) on standard instance sets, and an exact solver (VRPSolver, consistently solving capacitated instances up to about 200 customers) is recommended to obtain optimal references; for exact solvers report when the last improving solution was found and the primal integral. [V] — [ar5iv full text](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2109.13983)
- Routing Arena: best-known values are taken from the community library and stored with the algorithm that produced them; the suite "currently does not yet assess the optimality of a Best Known Solution"; performance is reported both relative to best known and as relative improvement over a fixed base solver. The paper does not discuss bias when the reference comes from a compared solver. [V] — [arXiv:2310.04140](https://arxiv.org/html/2310.04140v1)
- FrontierCO: because the primal gap is measured against best-known solutions, absolute gap values do not directly reflect instance difficulty. [V] — [arXiv:2505.16952](https://arxiv.org/html/2505.16952v2)
- Baty et al. (2024): the hindsight reference is produced by the same solver family that every compared policy uses for routing; the authors call it an anticipative baseline, not a bound, and test sensitivity to its quality. [V] — [ar5iv full text](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2304.00789)
- Kool et al. (2023): the "oracle" in the competition is the provided solver given future information, used to generate training examples and as a reference, not as a proven optimum. [V] — [PMLR paper](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v220/kool23a/kool23a.pdf)
- Klapp et al.: the perfect-information reference is the sample average of deterministic optimal costs, computed on the same realisations as all policies, and the authors flag bound tightness as a confound when interpreting gaps. [V] — [optimization-online preprint](https://optimization-online.org/wp-content/uploads/2016/10/5667.pdf)
- Kullman et al. (2021, 2022): the references are dual bounds obtained by decomposition (information relaxation with penalties; Benders-based perfect-information value), independent of the policies being compared. [V abstract] — [RePEc 2021](https://ideas.repec.org/a/inm/ortrsc/v55y2021i3p637-659.html); [RePEc 2022](https://ideas.repec.org/a/inm/ortrsc/v56y2022i3p775-794.html)
- Dai, Wu and Zhang (2025 preprint): performance is reported as a share of a fluid upper bound with the explicit caveat that the ratio "is a lower bound of the optimality ratio". [V] — [arXiv html](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.13392v2)
- Brown and Smith (2022): simulation of heuristic policies gives one side of the interval and information-relaxation bounds the other; in a portfolio example the penalised perfect-information bounds made it "clear that the best-performing feasible policies are nearly optimal in each case, a conclusion that is less clear" from a simpler model-based (frictionless) bound alone. [V] — [author copy](https://jimsmith.host.dartmouth.edu/wp-content/uploads/2022/04/Information_Relaxation_Review_Paper.pdf)
- Chandrasekaran (2026 preprint): two hindsight ceilings of different tightness are reported side by side so the reader can see how much of the apparent headroom is bound slack. [V abstract] — [arXiv:2607.07343](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.07343)

### Inferences
- A gap to a best-of-portfolio solution measures distance from the best method tried, and it is biased in favour of whichever family supplied most of the reference solutions: the planner cannot be more than 0 % from a reference it produced. Reporting practice that follows from the sources above (my synthesis, not a quoted rule):
  1. Call the reference "best known" and never "optimal"; state per category what fraction of reference solutions came from each method family, and how often the planner itself is the reference.
  2. Include every compared method, the learned policy too, in the portfolio that defines the reference, and report how many reference solutions each method newly sets.
  3. Report a leave-one-family-out reference as a sensitivity check: recompute the planner's gap against the best solution found by all other families.
  4. Add at least one reference that is independent of all compared methods: exact solutions on small instances; a hindsight mixed-integer solution with its proven bound and remaining solver gap; a penalised information-relaxation bound for the stochastic categories.
  5. Report both sides: primal gap to best known and gap to the bound, with the statement that the true optimality gap lies between zero and the latter.
  6. For dynamic categories, separate the two meanings of "best known": best non-anticipative policy value (what a policy can be compared with) and best hindsight solution (a ceiling no policy can reach).
- The statement "the planner is within 0.4 to 3 % of best known" should be presented as a saturation indicator for the portfolio, and the statement about being near the limit should rest only on the independent bound.

### Gaps
- I found no paper or guideline that explicitly analyses the bias from a reference solution generated by the baseline family in stochastic dynamic routing; items 1 to 6 are inferences from adjacent practice.
- Virtual-best-solver and portfolio-scoring conventions from solver competitions (for example the DIMACS routing challenge) were not researched this session and may contain a directly citable rule.
- One 2026 preprint on RL routing of electric trucks under operational uncertainty surfaced in a generic search ([arXiv:2604.26566](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.26566)); per the search summary it compares with heuristic and optimisation benchmarks and no hindsight bound, and it was not used as independent evidence.
