## 1. What are the experimentally established causes of performance collapse when PPO fine-tunes a behaviour-cloned policy, and which single fixes matter most?

### Takeaway
Five causes have direct experimental support: an untrained critic driving the first actor updates, step sizes and entropy bonuses tuned for training from scratch, forgetting of behaviour on states the fine-tuned policy stops visiting or visits only after small errors, unstructured action noise in long-horizon tasks, and a sparse rare-event reward that is noisier than the dense term it competes with. The fix with the largest measured effect is to fit the critic to the frozen imitation policy before any actor update; next come a learning rate about ten times smaller with warm-up, and a retention loss computed on the expert's states that is decayed, not held constant.

### Cited Findings

#### Untrained critic and destructive first updates
- (V) PIRLNav (Ramrakhya, Batra, Wijmans, Das; CVPR 2023; arXiv:2301.07302) states: "Naively finetuning the actor with a randomly-initialized critic leads to a rapid drop in performance", and that after the initial drop "the performance increases but the improvements on success are small." — [PIRLNav](https://arxiv.org/abs/2301.07302)
- (V) PIRLNav ablation, HM3D validation, 20k demonstrations, success / SPL: BC 52.0 / 20.6; naive BC then RL 53.6 / 28.6; plus critic learning 56.7 / 27.7; plus critic learning and critic learning-rate decay 59.4 / 26.9; plus critic learning and actor warm-up 58.2 / 26.7; full method 61.9 / 27.9. Naive fine-tuning adds 1.6 points of success; the staged scheme adds 9.9. — [PIRLNav](https://arxiv.org/abs/2301.07302)
- (V) FLaRe (Hu, Hendrix, Farhadi, Kembhavi, Martín-Martín, Stone, Zeng, Ehsani; arXiv:2409.16578, 2024; ICRA 2025 from memory) attributes failure of earlier BC-to-RL methods at scale to "the abrupt shift from BC to RL often results in destructive gradient updates", leading to "oscillations or even collapse during RL training". — [FLaRe](https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.16578)
- (V) FLaRe names four components (on-policy PPO, a small learning rate, entropy bonus set to 0, separate actor and critic networks). Removing any one of them gives "the success rate quickly collapsing to 0 on the fetch task"; the ablations were PPO replaced by SAC, learning rate ×10, entropy coefficient 0.2, shared actor-critic trunk. The paper gives curves only, no ablation table. — [FLaRe](https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.16578)
- (V) AWAC (Nair, Gupta, Dalal, Levine; arXiv:2006.09359, 2020) reports that SAC started from a behaviour-cloned actor shows "an initial decrease in performance, and performance similar to learning from scratch". — [AWAC](https://arxiv.org/abs/2006.09359)
- (V) Cal-QL (Nakamoto, Zhai, Singh, Mark, Ma, Finn, Kumar, Levine; NeurIPS 2023; arXiv:2303.05479) traces the initial dip ("unlearning") to the scale of the value estimates: the offline Q-values are "much smaller than their ground-truth value", and performance recovers only when the Q-range matches the true range. — [Cal-QL](https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.05479)
- (V) WSRL (Zhou, Peng, Li, Levine, Kumar; ICLR 2025; arXiv:2412.07762): "Distribution shift between offline and online data destroys Q-function fit"; IQL and CQL "unlearn right at the beginning of fine-tuning", with kitchen-partial success falling to "nearly a 0% success rate". — [WSRL](https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.07762)

#### Forgetting of states no longer visited
- (V) Wołczyk, Cupiał, Ostaszewski, Bortkiewicz, Zając, Pascanu, Kuciński, Miłoś (ICML 2024; arXiv:2402.02868) name the cause "forgetting of pre-trained capabilities" and give two cases. State coverage gap: the pre-trained agent is good on "Far" states but must first learn the "Close" states, and loses the Far behaviour meanwhile. Imperfect cloning gap: the agent is a slightly perturbed copy of a good policy, small errors shift visitation, and behaviour on the rest of the state space is forgotten. — [Wołczyk et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.02868)
- (V) Same paper, NetHack with APPO: knowledge retention raises the best score "from 5K to over 10K points" in the Human Monk scenario; kickstarting is best and behaviour cloning on stored expert data second; elastic weight consolidation "is consistently outperformed by the other approaches". — [Wołczyk et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.02868)
- (V) Same paper: kickstarting "fails completely" in the state-coverage-gap setting, because its loss is computed on the student's own states; behaviour cloning on stored expert states was the most effective method in RoboticSequence (all four stages solved 80% of the time). — [Wołczyk et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.02868)
- (V) VPT (Baker et al.; NeurIPS 2022; arXiv:2206.11795): without a KL loss to the frozen pretrained policy, RL fine-tuning stalls after 100,000 episodes and "subsequent skills like crafting a wooden pickaxe are lost due to catastrophic forgetting". With the loss, the agent crafts a diamond pickaxe in 2.5% of episodes. — [VPT](https://arxiv.org/abs/2206.11795)
- (V) DAPG (Rajeswaran, Kumar, Gupta, Vezzani, Schulman, Todorov, Levine; RSS 2018; arXiv:1709.10087): demonstration data "is lost when the data is used only for initialization". — [DAPG](https://arxiv.org/abs/1709.10087)

#### Exploration noise, long horizons and rare-event rewards
- (V) DPPO (Ren, Lidard, Ankile, Simeonov, Agrawal, Majumdar, Burchfiel, Dai, Simchowitz; ICLR 2025; arXiv:2409.00588): under PPO fine-tuning with medium initial-state randomness on Furniture-Bench, "Gaussian-MLP collapses to zero success rate in all three tasks". The authors attribute the difference to noise structure: a Gaussian policy adds unstructured noise to the final action, a diffusion policy adds noise through denoising steps that pull actions back toward the data manifold. — [DPPO](https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.00588)
- (V) Ankile, Simeonov, Shenfeld, Torne, Agrawal (arXiv:2407.16677, 2024): direct PPO fine-tuning of a chunked behaviour-cloned MLP "exhibits training instability and requires careful KL regularization to avoid collapse". Success in % on six assembly tasks (one_leg low/medium randomness, round_table low/medium, lamp low/medium, mug-rack, peg-in-hole, bimanual insert): cloned chunked MLP 45/10, 5/2, 8/1, 21, 2, 7; the same MLP after direct PPO fine-tuning 70/28, 38/6, 32/2, 23, 4, 30; cloned diffusion policy 54/26, 12/4, 7/2, 26, 5, 33; that diffusion policy frozen plus a PPO residual 98/76, 94/77, 97/70, 88, 99, 93. — [Ankile et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.16677)
- (V) Peng, Luo, Lu, Shen, Gulino, Seff, Fu (ECCV 2024; arXiv:2409.18343) fine-tune a cloned driving model with REINFORCE and a reward of displacement error plus a weighted collision indicator. Raising the collision weight from 2 to 10 lowers the collision score from 0.863 to 0.831 and worsens displacement error from 2.436 to 3.058; the authors' reason is that "displacement error is a very dense and rich reward signal, whereas collision is a more sparse and noisy signal." — [Peng et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.18343)
- (R) Engstrom et al. (ICLR 2020; arXiv:2005.12729) show that PPO's clipping does not by itself enforce a trust region; the KL step actually taken depends on code-level choices such as learning-rate annealing and reward or advantage normalisation. — [Engstrom et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2005.12729)
- (R) Rudner, Lu, Osborne, Gal, Teh (NeurIPS 2021; arXiv:2111.14629) show that a KL penalty to a parametric behaviour prior becomes pathological where the prior's predictive variance collapses away from the demonstration data. — [Rudner et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2111.14629)

#### Constant versus annealed imitation terms
- (V) Kickstarting (Schmitt et al.; arXiv:1803.03835, 2018), DMLab-30, score at 10 billion frames with a teacher at 39.7: constant distillation weight 1 gives 41.2 and weight 2 gives 40.8 (constant schedules "cause the agent to plateau near the teacher's final performance"); linear decay to zero gives about 59 to 61; weights tuned by population-based training give 56.9. The paper notes it is "important to reduce this weight quickly". — [Kickstarting](https://arxiv.org/abs/1803.03835)
- (V) DAPG weights the demonstration gradient by λ0·λ1^k·max A with λ0 = 0.1 and λ1 = 0.95, so the term decays geometrically per iteration. — [DAPG](https://arxiv.org/abs/1709.10087)
- (V) Reincarnating RL (Agarwal, Schwarzer, Castro, Courville, Bellemare; NeurIPS 2022; arXiv:2206.01626): in QDagger the distillation weight falls with the ratio of student return to teacher return (a linear decay worked comparably). Kickstarting "exhibits performance degradation" once the teacher is weaned off and DQfD "exhibits severe performance degradation when weaning off the teacher"; QDagger "outperforms the teacher in 75% of runs" within 10M frames on 10 Atari games. — [Reincarnating RL](https://arxiv.org/abs/2206.01626)
- (V) Wołczyk et al. used a kickstarting loss scaled 0.5 and decayed by 0.99998 per step, and a behaviour-cloning loss scaled 2.0 with no decay; they did not test whether the schedule matters. — [Wołczyk et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.02868)
- (V) Zhao, Boney, Ilin, Kannala, Pajarinen (arXiv:2210.13846, 2022): behaviour-cloning constraints reduce the collapse but "significantly slow down online fine-tuning by forcing the agent to stay close to the behavior policy"; they adapt the weight "based on the agent's performance and training stability". — [Zhao et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2210.13846)
- (V) SPACE (Yang, Rosca, Narasimhan, Ramadge; ICML 2021; arXiv:2006.11645): fixed-weight regularisation to a baseline policy fails through "persistent supervision from the baseline policies", and a decaying weight "is susceptible to a sub-optimal" baseline; a distance to the baseline that is adjusted during training gave 2.3 times more reward than a fixed distance on one task. — [SPACE](https://arxiv.org/abs/2006.11645)

#### Do offline-to-online lessons carry to on-policy PPO?
- (V) AWAC argues on-policy fine-tuning "is data inefficient as it does not reuse the prior data in the RL stage" and that DAPG, AWR and MARWIL "are an order of magnitude slower than off-policy actor-critic methods" on HalfCheetah. — [AWAC](https://arxiv.org/abs/2006.09359)
- (V) DPPO reports the opposite ordering on long-horizon sparse-reward manipulation: on Robomimic Can and Square "RLPD and Cal-QL fail to learn at all and IBRL saturates at lower success levels", while PPO-based fine-tuning reaches over 90% on Transport; RLPD, IBRL and Cal-QL are more sample-efficient on HalfCheetah. — [DPPO](https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.00588)
- (V) Uni-O4 (Lei, He, Lu, Hu, Gao, Xu; ICLR 2024; arXiv:2311.03351) runs the PPO objective in both the offline and the online phase; "The policy and value function obtained by offline training is directly used as initialization" for online PPO, with no added regulariser. It attributes "an initial performance drop or asymptotical suboptimality" in other methods to conservatism carried over from offline training. — [Uni-O4](https://arxiv.org/abs/2311.03351)
- (V) Cal-QL's remedy is a value function lower-bounded by the Monte Carlo value of a reference (behaviour) policy; aggregate improvement over the offline initialisation was +106.9% against +37.7% for IQL and +23.7% for AWAC, and mean regret 0.22 against 0.38 for IQL. — [Cal-QL](https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.05479)
- (V) WSRL's remedy is 5,000 warm-up steps of rollouts from the frozen pre-trained policy before any update; without warm-up it "performs worse or has much higher variance". — [WSRL](https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.07762)
- (R) RLPD (Ball, Smith, Kostrikov, Levine; ICML 2023; arXiv:2302.02948) samples half of every batch from the prior data and reports up to 2.5 times improvement over earlier methods. — [RLPD](https://arxiv.org/abs/2302.02948)

### Inferences
- Cal-QL's calibration and WSRL's warm-up are the off-policy forms of PIRLNav's critic-only phase: each makes the value function describe the starting policy's true returns before it is allowed to move the actor. A PPO critic regressed on Monte Carlo returns of the frozen imitation policy is calibrated by construction.
- A constant imitation term caps the gain (Kickstarting) but should also prevent a fall below the starting point. A policy that ends below its start despite a constant term therefore points to one of three causes the term does not address: the critic was not fitted before the actor moved; the term is computed on the student's own states, which Wołczyk et al. show does not protect states the student has stopped visiting; or sampling noise and entropy pressure dominate.
- The two-stage result in the context (better imitation checkpoints, less harm) matches the imperfect cloning gap: a closer copy shifts visitation less.
- Ranking of single fixes by measured effect: critic fitted first (PIRLNav +3.1 points from that step alone over naive fine-tuning, and it is a precondition for the other steps); learning-rate reduction and warm-up (PIRLNav +1.5 to +2.7 on top; FLaRe collapse without it); retention loss on expert states with decay (NetHack score doubled; VPT stall without it); no entropy bonus (FLaRe).

### Gaps
- No controlled study compares all the fixes on one benchmark. PIRLNav ablates the schedule; FLaRe reports collapse without numbers; Wołczyk et al. compare retention losses only.
- VPT's KL coefficient and decay schedule are in an appendix I did not read.
- FLaRe's learning rate is inconsistent in the paper: the text says it is reduced by an order of magnitude from 2e-4, a table lists 2e-4, and the ablation calls 2e-4 "10 times our original learning rate" (implying 2e-5).
- I found no study of PPO fine-tuning of a cloned graph-network policy for routing or dispatching; all evidence above is from navigation, manipulation, games and driving. One unread lead: SABR, behaviour-cloning pretraining plus PPO fine-tuning for adaptive bitrate control (arXiv:2509.10486).

## 2. What evidence exists for critic pretraining, frozen-actor phases, small learning rates or tight KL limits in avoiding the initial drop?

### Takeaway
Critic pretraining with the actor frozen has the clearest quantitative support (PIRLNav's ablation, a 2026 15-task study, and the NetHack setup of Wołczyk et al.). Small learning rates and KL anchors are supported mainly by collapse-versus-no-collapse reports; a smaller learning rate alone delays the drop but does not remove it.

### Cited Findings
- (V) PIRLNav phase 1: the cloned actor is frozen, rollouts sample actions instead of taking the argmax, and the critic is trained until its loss plateaus (8M steps sufficed). The critic's final linear layer is initialised near zero. — [PIRLNav](https://arxiv.org/abs/2301.07302)
- (V) PIRLNav phase 2: between 8M and 12M steps the critic learning rate decays from 2.5e-4 to 1.5e-5 and the actor learning rate warms up from 0 to 1.5e-5; both then stay at 1.5e-5. The visual encoder and non-visual embeddings stay frozen. PPO clip 0.2, 2 epochs, 2 mini-batches, max gradient norm 0.2, advantages not normalised, 300M steps in total. — [PIRLNav](https://arxiv.org/abs/2301.07302)
- (V) PIRLNav appendix: with only a smaller learning rate and no critic phase, "training performance drops significantly early on" and recovers as the critic improves. — [PIRLNav](https://arxiv.org/abs/2301.07302)
- (V) PIRLNav against a VPT-style KL anchor on the same task: 59.7 ± 0.70 success with the KL anchor, 61.9 ± 0.47 with the staged scheme. — [PIRLNav](https://arxiv.org/abs/2301.07302)
- (V) Kernbach, Elsheikh, Grupp, Nagel, Huber (arXiv:2602.23804, February 2026, preprint) pretrain the actor by behaviour cloning and "The critic is pretrained using returns obtained from rollouts of the pretrained policy", then run PPO. On 15 simulated manipulation and locomotion tasks, sample efficiency improves by 86.1% over no pretraining and by 30.9% over actor-only pretraining. I read the abstract only. — [Kernbach et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.23804)
- (V) Wołczyk et al., NetHack: because "BC does not include a critic", the value head is pre-trained for 500M environment steps with the rest of the model frozen before fine-tuning. — [Wołczyk et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.02868)
- (V) Peng et al. fine-tune a cloned driving model with learning rate 5e-6 and no KL or behaviour-cloning term; the only anchor is the displacement term in the reward. Composite realism rises from 0.490 to 0.597 (1M-parameter model) and from 0.549 to 0.608 (10M). — [Peng et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.18343)
- (V) Ankile et al.: PPO settings clip 0.2, target KL 0.1, discount 0.999; the residual is scaled by 0.1 and its last layer initialised with a small gain so that initial corrections are near zero; actor learning rate 3e-4 and critic 5e-3. No critic warm-up is described. — [Ankile et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.16677)
- (V) Liu, Gao, Wei, Chen, Liao, Wu, Yu, Wang (arXiv:2505.19789, 2025) fine-tune a vision-language-action policy with PPO: one PPO epoch per batch (more epochs gave no gain), a warm-up on 140 demonstrations cut environment steps by about 50%, and a shared actor-critic backbone was kept because a separate critic "trained 35% slower and consumed 83% more VRAM". — [Liu et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.19789)
- (V) Conflict: FLaRe reports collapse with a shared actor-critic trunk, Liu et al. prefer a shared backbone. — [FLaRe](https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.16578); contrasted with [Liu et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.19789)
- (V) Conflict: Li, Zhang, Ding, Wang, Yu (arXiv:2608.10473, August 2026, preprint) argue that reusing a critic trained offline hinders online fine-tuning and propose starting with a fresh critic; the abstract gives no numbers. — [Li et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.10473); contrasted with [Cal-QL](https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.05479)
- (R) Goecks, Gremillion, Lawhern, Valasek, Waytowich (AAMAS 2020; arXiv:1910.04281) pretrain both actor and critic from demonstrations and keep a behaviour-cloning loss to avoid the drop at the switch to RL. — [Goecks et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/1910.04281)

### Inferences
- The Li et al. objection concerns critics trained on offline data with pessimism, which are biased for the online distribution. A critic fitted to on-policy Monte Carlo returns of the frozen imitation policy does not have that bias, so the two results are compatible.
- The shared-versus-separate conflict is probably about gradient scale: FLaRe's value loss flows into a large pretrained trunk, Liu et al. train low-rank adapters with a small value head. For a pretrained policy the safe default is a separate critic, or a shared trunk with the value gradient stopped during the warm-up.
- Sampling actions during the critic phase matters: the critic must describe the stochastic policy PPO will update, and under rare failures the stochastic policy's failure rate may differ from the greedy one's.
- A tight KL target is supported only indirectly (Ankile et al.'s "careful KL regularization"; VPT's anchor); I found no sweep of clip range or target KL for fine-tuning.

### Gaps
- No paper reports the size of the initial drop as a function of clip range or target KL.
- Kernbach et al. is a preprint read at abstract level; whether it compares against a PIRLNav-style frozen-actor phase is unknown.
- How long the critic phase must be for long episodes with 1 to 5% failure rates is not addressed anywhere I read; PIRLNav's rule is "until the critic loss plateaus".

## 3. How do baseline-relative or paired-scenario rewards reduce variance, and has anyone trained a policy on "improvement over the expert's return on the same random scenario"?

### Takeaway
Subtracting the return of a reference rollout on the same instance removes instance difficulty and exogenous randomness from the learning signal, with large measured effects (a 50 times lower gradient variance in one queueing example; about a third of the optimality gap in routing). I found per-instance comparisons against the policy's own greedy rollout, its best past version or its own history, but no paper that uses an external expert's return on the same seeded scenario as the PPO reward baseline.

### Cited Findings
- (V) Kool, van Hoof, Welling (ICLR 2019; arXiv:1803.08475): the baseline is "the cost of a solution from a deterministic greedy rollout of the policy defined by the best model so far" on the same instance. The baseline policy is replaced only when the current policy is better on 10,000 instances by a one-sided paired t-test at α = 5%, and the evaluation instances are then resampled. — [Kool et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/1803.08475)
- (V) Same paper, 20-node travelling salesman problem, optimality gap: exponential-average baseline 0.95%, learned critic 0.96%, greedy rollout 0.34% (second seed 0.93%, 0.97%, 0.29%). The authors' reason: "the difficulty of an instance can (on average) be estimated by the performance of an algorithm applied to it". — [Kool et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/1803.08475)
- (R) Self-critical sequence training (Rennie, Marcheret, Mroueh, Ross, Goel; CVPR 2017; arXiv:1612.00563) subtracts the reward of the model's own greedy decode on the same input and reports CIDEr rising from 104.9 to 114.7. — [Rennie et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/1612.00563)
- (R) POMO (Kwon, Choo, Kim, Yoon, Gwon, Min; NeurIPS 2020; arXiv:2010.16011) uses the mean return of several rollouts of the same instance as a shared baseline. — [POMO](https://arxiv.org/abs/2010.16011)
- (V) Mao, Venkatakrishnan, Schwarzkopf, Alizadeh (ICLR 2019; arXiv:1807.02264) treat environments driven by an exogenous input process (job arrivals, bandwidth, wind). A state-only baseline fails because the future inputs, not the action, explain most of the return. Their baseline is "a function of both the state and the entire future input sequence", and Theorem 1 shows it "does not bias the policy gradient" when the input process is independent of states and actions. — [Mao et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/1807.02264)
- (V) Same paper, results: "a 50× lower policy gradient variance" and "a 33% higher test reward" in a load-balancing example; 25 to 33% better test reward with A2C; 25% to 3 times with TRPO; 42% to 3.5 times with PPO. Training replays the same input sequence for several rollouts; an LSTM baseline that only reads the inputs gave no significant gain. — [Mao et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/1807.02264)
- (V) Mandhane et al. (arXiv:2202.06626, 2022) keep, for every [video, target bitrate] pair, an exponential moving average of the agent's own past outcome and pay +1 or −1 for beating it; constraint overshoot is compared first and quality second, so the agent "learns to satisfy the constraint first". Result: 6.28% bitrate reduction at equal quality over the production controller. Their Lagrangian variant needed several hyperparameter sweeps and performed far worse; they note "there is no single multiplier that is optimal across videos and bitrates". — [Mandhane et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2202.06626)
- (V, search summary only) Ranked Reward (Laterre et al.; arXiv:1807.01672, 2018) binarises the reward against a percentile of the agent's recent performance; Pirnay, Göttl, Burger, Grimm (ICLR 2023; arXiv:2306.04403) let the planner compete against a historical policy on the same problem and beat single-player variants on travelling salesman and job-shop problems with half the simulation budget. — [Laterre et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/1807.01672); [Pirnay et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.04403)
- (V, abstract) Sakib (arXiv:2609.24144, September 2026, single-author preprint) studies rollouts that share an event-keyed noise schedule. Pairing "removes the between-schedule component of reward-contrast variance" but does not always lower gradient variance (a counterexample is given). Measured: covariance traces 40 to 63% lower under tool faults and 21 to 30% lower under grader noise; final success +5.1 points with three seeds; the pre-registered learning-speed criterion was missed. — [Sakib](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.24144)
- (R) TRPO's "vine" estimator uses common random numbers across the rollouts from one state (Schulman, Levine, Moritz, Jordan, Abbeel; ICML 2015; arXiv:1502.05477); PEGASUS fixes the simulator's random numbers so that policy comparison becomes deterministic (Ng and Jordan; UAI 2000; arXiv:1301.3878). — [TRPO](https://arxiv.org/abs/1502.05477); [PEGASUS](https://arxiv.org/abs/1301.3878)
- (R) Methods that use an expert's cost-to-go as the learning signal exist in learning-to-search: LOLS (Chang, Krishnamurthy, Agarwal, Daumé, Langford; ICML 2015; arXiv:1502.02206) and MAMBA (Cheng, Kolobov, Agarwal; NeurIPS 2020; arXiv:2007.00795), which baselines on the maximum of several oracles' values. — [LOLS](https://arxiv.org/abs/1502.02206); [MAMBA](https://arxiv.org/abs/2007.00795)

### Inferences
- The planner's return on the same seeded scenario is a function of the exogenous inputs only, so by Mao et al.'s Theorem 1 it can be subtracted without bias wherever the inputs (request arrivals, travel conditions) do not depend on the policy's actions. Queues at shared chargers are endogenous and are not covered by that argument.
- The variance removed is the between-scenario part. With 5 to 100 trucks and five problem families, scenario difficulty probably dominates return variance, which is the case where Kool et al. and Mao et al. see their largest gains.
- A per-scenario return difference also puts all families on one scale (improvement over the planner), which addresses the return-scale problem of question 4 at the same time. Mandhane et al. chose a per-instance comparison for a similar reason.
- Kool et al.'s paired t-test on a fixed instance set is a ready-made acceptance rule for "never end below the starting point": keep a checkpoint only if it is not significantly worse than the imitation policy per family on paired scenarios.
- Sakib's caution applies: pairing sharpens comparisons but a learning-speed gain was not shown at three seeds.

### Gaps
- I found no paper that trains with PPO on "return minus the external expert's return on the same random scenario". The nearest are the own-policy baselines of Kool et al. and Rennie et al., the own-history comparison of Mandhane et al., and the oracle-value baselines of LOLS and MAMBA.
- Mao et al. need the baseline as a function of time within the episode; how to build a per-decision baseline from a planner rollout whose state diverges from the learner's is not treated in what I read.
- The Laterre and Pirnay entries rest on search summaries; I did not read the papers.

## 4. In multi-task RL, which methods stop gains in one task family from costing another, and how large are the reported effects?

### Takeaway
Per-task return normalisation has the largest and best-replicated effect (median Atari score 59.7% to 110.7%). Gradient-surgery methods add 14 to 34 points of success on Meta-World over a naive shared policy in their own papers, but a later controlled study found none of them consistently beats a tuned sum of losses, and all shared policies stay below one agent per task.

### Cited Findings
- (V) PopArt (Hessel, Soyer, Espeholt, Czarnecki, Schmitt, van Hasselt; AAAI 2019; arXiv:1809.04474): "the saliency of a task for the agent increases with the scale of the returns observed in that task". The method keeps one value output and one mean and scale per task, and the policy gradient uses the per-task normalised advantage; only the value, not the policy, is conditioned on the task index. — [PopArt](https://arxiv.org/abs/1809.04474)
- (V) PopArt results: Atari-57 median human-normalised score 59.7% for IMPALA and 110.7% with PopArt (clipped rewards, random starts); with unclipped rewards 0.3% against 107.0%. DMLab-30 mean capped score 60.6% against 73.5% (train) and 58.4% against 72.8% (test). The paper's prose quotes 101% for unclipped, which differs from its table. — [PopArt](https://arxiv.org/abs/1809.04474)
- (V) Per-task value heads alone do not help: MultiHead-IMPALA without normalisation is slightly worse than plain IMPALA. — [PopArt](https://arxiv.org/abs/1809.04474)
- (V) AMAGO-2 (Grigsby, Sasek, Parajuli, Adebi, Zhang, Zhu; NeurIPS 2024; arXiv:2411.11188): standard actor and critic losses "directly depend on the scale of returns", so gradients "can favor small relative improvements in tasks with high absolute returns". Replacing value regression by classification and the actor loss by advantage-filtered imitation needs no task labels and gave "dramatic improvements in 8/10 games" on multi-game Atari with unclipped rewards, and more than double the earlier success rate on Meta-World ML45 at 100M steps. — [AMAGO-2](https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.11188)
- (V) Meta-World (Yu et al.; CoRL 2019; arXiv:1910.10897) notes that "varying reward scales or structures can make the tasks appear completely distinct for the learning algorithm" and designs all rewards to span 0 to 10. Success in the current arXiv version: multi-task PPO 30.5% (10 tasks) and 35.4% (50 tasks); multi-task SAC 68.3% and 38.5%. — [Meta-World](https://arxiv.org/abs/1910.10897)
- (V) PCGrad (Yu, Kumar, Gupta, Levine, Hausman, Finn; NeurIPS 2020; arXiv:2001.06782) attributes harm to conflicting gradients combined with high curvature and large differences in gradient magnitude, and projects each task gradient onto the normal plane of any conflicting one. With SAC it solves all 10 tasks of MT10 and about 70% of MT50, where a single SAC policy and a multi-head SAC are "unable to acquire half of the skills"; the paper claims "more than 30% absolute improvement". — [PCGrad](https://arxiv.org/abs/2001.06782)
- (V) CAGrad (Liu, Liu, Jin, Stone, Liu; NeurIPS 2021; arXiv:2110.14048), Meta-World success on MT10 / MT50: multi-task SAC 0.49 / 0.36; with task encoder 0.54 / 0.40; multi-headed SAC 0.61 / 0.45; PCGrad 0.72 / 0.50; soft modularisation 0.73 / 0.50; CAGrad 0.83 / 0.52; CARE 0.84 / 0.54; one SAC agent per task 0.90 / 0.74. — [CAGrad](https://arxiv.org/abs/2110.14048)
- (V) Kurin, De Palma, Kostrikov, Whiteson, Kumar (NeurIPS 2022; arXiv:2201.04122): "no SMTO consistently outperforms unitary scalarization across the considered settings"; on MT10 the plain sum "performs on par", on MT50 PCGrad's mean is slightly higher but not significantly. A plain run "takes roughly 15 hours, whereas PCGrad, MGDA and GradDrop require more than a week". The authors "postulate that SMTOs act as regularizers". — [Kurin et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2201.04122)
- (R) Xin, Ghorbani, Gilmer, Garg, Firat (NeurIPS 2022; arXiv:2209.11379) reach the same conclusion on supervised benchmarks: tuned static task weights match the specialised optimisers. — [Xin et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2209.11379)
- (V) Distillation from per-task teachers: a kickstarted multi-task agent with one expert teacher per task reaches 73.8 on DMLab-30 at 10 billion frames against 51.9 from scratch, and reaches a score of 50 in 0.85 billion frames against 8.14 billion. — [Kickstarting](https://arxiv.org/abs/1803.03835)
- (V) Sampling toward hard cases: Lu et al. (IROS 2023; arXiv:2212.11419) report that "all methods perform best when trained on Top10" (the hardest 10% of driving scenarios); for their combined method the failure rate on all scenarios falls from 3.35% to 2.81%. — [Lu et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2212.11419)
- (R) Related mechanisms: Distral regularises each task policy to a shared distilled policy (Teh et al.; NeurIPS 2017; arXiv:1707.04175); "ray interference" describes plateaus when one component's learning starves another under shared parameters and on-policy data (Schaul, Borsa, Modayil, Pascanu; arXiv:1904.11455); teacher-student curricula and prioritised level replay choose tasks by learning progress or value error (Matiisen et al., arXiv:1707.00183; Jiang, Grefenstette, Rocktäschel, ICML 2021, arXiv:2010.03934). — [Distral](https://arxiv.org/abs/1707.04175); [Schaul et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/1904.11455); [Matiisen et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/1707.00183); [Jiang et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2010.03934)

### Inferences
- Return normalisation should be tried before gradient surgery: it is the method with the largest effect, it costs nothing at run time, and Kurin et al. show surgery's gains do not survive tuning while costing roughly ten times the compute.
- PopArt's result that separate heads without normalisation do not help says the problem is the scale of the advantage that reaches the shared policy, not the value architecture.
- A family in which RL has room to improve (larger and more variable advantages) will dominate the shared policy gradient over a family where the imitation policy is already near the planner. That is the pattern in the context: gains in drayage, losses in long haul.
- The gap to one agent per task (0.84 against 0.90 on 10 tasks; 0.54 against 0.74 on 50) shows no method removes interference entirely; a per-family acceptance test or per-family adapters remain the only guarantees.
- Forgetting within one policy (question 1) and interference across families are the same mechanism seen at two scales; a retention loss on each family's expert states acts on both.

### Gaps
- I found no study of interference during RL fine-tuning of a multi-task imitation-pretrained policy; all numbers above are for training from scratch.
- PCGrad and Kurin et al. give Meta-World numbers in figures only; the CAGrad table is the one tabulated source.
- The original Meta-World conference table (which I recall listing a multi-head SAC at 88% on MT10) differs from the current arXiv version quoted above; I could not verify the older numbers.
- Evidence for task-sampling schemes in multi-task fine-tuning is limited to the Lu et al. result; the curriculum papers are cited from memory.

## 5. Which constrained-RL formulations have been used to keep a success or safety rate above a reference policy's level while optimising another objective?

### Takeaway
Three formulations fit: an indicator-cost constraint with a learned Lagrange multiplier ("success rate at least X", used with thresholds of 0.99 and 0.80), conservative exploration that requires return of at least (1 − α) times a baseline policy's and falls back to the baseline otherwise, and projection methods that keep the policy within an adaptive distance of a baseline. None was demonstrated with the threshold set to an imitation policy's measured success rate inside PPO fine-tuning.

### Cited Findings
- (V) Roy, Girgis, Romoff, Bacon, Pal (ICML 2022; arXiv:2112.12228) write each requirement as an indicator cost whose expected value is the event's rate, so thresholds are probabilities. They use a success constraint of at least 0.99 (Arena) and 0.80 (OpenWorld) together with "at most" constraints such as in-lava 0.01. The solver is SAC-Lagrangian (TD3 in an appendix), not PPO. — [Roy et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2112.12228)
- (V) Same paper, two changes to the Lagrangian method: multipliers are passed through a softmax so the reward weight is 1 − Σλ and update scale stays bounded (the unnormalised version diverges); and the success constraint's multiplier is reused as the weight of the dense task reward ("bootstrap constraint") so that many constraints do not squeeze the task weight to zero. With a hand-tuned penalty reward instead, only 2 of 49 weight settings gave good feasible policies for two constraints and 0 of 343 for three. — [Roy et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2112.12228)
- (V) RCPO (Tessler, Mankowitz, Mannor; ICLR 2019; arXiv:1805.11074) trains on r − λc with the critic on the fastest timescale, the actor on an intermediate one and λ on the slowest, and proves almost-sure convergence to a constraint-satisfying solution under stated assumptions. It handles mean-valued and probabilistic constraints and is invariant to reward scale; a fixed penalty "is not shared across domains". With PPO on MuJoCo and a 25% torque limit, RCPO ends at 24 to 26.7% torque in five of six domains, while a fixed penalty of 0.1 either violates the limit (Swimmer 32.8%, Humanoid 28.5%) or over-satisfies it at a cost in reward (HalfCheetah reward −0.4 against 1547.1). — [RCPO](https://arxiv.org/abs/1805.11074)
- (V) Stooke, Achiam, Abbeel (ICML 2020; arXiv:2007.03964): "the traditional Lagrange multiplier update... amounts to integral control on the constraint", which produces oscillation and overshoot ("intermediate iterates often violate constraints"). Adding proportional and derivative terms damps this with PPO on Safety Gym; scaling the reward gradient by the ratio of gradient norms made learning nearly identical when rewards were scaled by 0.1 and 10. Results are in figures. — [Stooke et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2007.03964)
- (R) CPO (Achiam, Held, Tamar, Abbeel; ICML 2017; arXiv:1705.10528) solves a trust-region problem with a linearised constraint and gives a bound on constraint violation at every iteration. — [CPO](https://arxiv.org/abs/1705.10528)
- (R) Ray, Achiam, Amodei (2019, OpenAI report) found on Safety Gym that Lagrangian PPO and TRPO enforced constraints more reliably than CPO. — [Ray et al.](https://cdn.openai.com/safexp-short.pdf)
- (V) Garcelon, Ghavamzadeh, Lazaric, Pirotta (AISTATS 2020; arXiv:2002.03218) require at every time that cumulative reward be at least (1 − α) times that of a known baseline policy. Their algorithm plays its exploratory policy only when a checkable version of the condition holds and "Otherwise, CUCRL2 executes the baseline"; regret stays of order √T, and in an inventory problem it "always satisfies the constraint for all values of α". The setting is tabular. — [Garcelon et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2002.03218)
- (V) SPACE (Yang et al.) alternates a reward step, a projection into a region around the baseline policy, and a projection onto the cost-constraint set; the baseline need not satisfy the constraint. It reports "10 times fewer constraint violations and 40% higher reward on average" against fixed-weight and decaying-weight regularisation toward the baseline. — [SPACE](https://arxiv.org/abs/2006.11645)
- (V) Mandhane et al. avoid multipliers by a lexicographic per-instance comparison (constraint first, objective second) against the agent's own history. — [Mandhane et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2202.06626)
- (V) A fixed penalty weight shows the trade-off the constrained forms are meant to remove: in Peng et al. a collision weight of 2 gives collision score 0.863 at displacement error 2.436, and a weight of 10 gives 0.831 at 3.058. — [Peng et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.18343)
- (R) Offline counterparts with a guarantee relative to a baseline: SPIBB keeps the baseline's action wherever data are insufficient (Laroche, Trichelair, Tachet des Combes; ICML 2019; arXiv:1712.06924); high-confidence policy improvement returns a new policy only if a lower confidence bound on its performance exceeds the baseline's (Thomas, Theocharous, Ghavamzadeh; ICML 2015). — [SPIBB](https://arxiv.org/abs/1712.06924); [Thomas et al.](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v37/thomas15.html)

### Inferences
- The formulation closest to "do not fall below the imitation policy's success rate" is Roy et al.'s success constraint with the threshold set to the starting policy's measured rate per family, one multiplier per family, and Stooke et al.'s proportional term to limit overshoot. Mandhane et al.'s remark that no single multiplier fits all instances supports per-family multipliers.
- Rare failures make the multiplier signal noisy. My arithmetic: a failure rate of 2% estimated to a standard error of 0.5 points needs about 780 episodes; separating 98.0% from 95.5% success with unpaired samples of 500 episodes each gives a difference of only 2.2 standard errors. Paired scenarios (question 3) reduce this, and the multiplier should be updated from a running estimate over many batches.
- The roles of the two signals can be split as in Lu et al. (question 6): an imitation term carries nominal behaviour, the RL reward carries only the rare-event objective. Garcelon et al.'s rule "play the baseline unless the condition holds" has a direct deep-RL analogue in checkpoint acceptance tests and in the per-state gating of question 6.

### Gaps
- No paper I found sets the constraint threshold to a reference policy's own measured success rate during PPO fine-tuning of an imitation policy. Roy et al. use absolute thresholds with SAC; Garcelon et al. are tabular; SPACE constrains distance to the baseline, not its success rate.
- Quantitative Safety Gym results of Stooke et al. are in figures only; CPO and the Ray et al. comparison are cited from memory.
- Mandhane et al.'s Lagrangian baseline number extracted from the appendix (a 256.37% bitrate change) looked implausible and should be checked before quoting.
- I did not cover multi-objective policy-gradient methods in depth; MO-MPO (Abdolmaleki et al.; ICML 2020; arXiv:2005.07513), which encodes preferences as per-objective KL budgets that do not depend on reward scale, is cited from memory only. — [MO-MPO](https://arxiv.org/abs/2005.07513)

## 6. Are there reports of RL fine-tuning helping only where the expert is weak, and how do authors handle the categories where the expert is near-optimal?

### Takeaway
Yes, consistently: gains from RL shrink as the imitation policy improves, concentrate in rare or dynamic cases where the expert is not optimal, and pure RL is worse than imitation on nominal cases. Authors protect the near-optimal categories by keeping the imitation policy frozen and choosing per state (or adding a small residual), by keeping an imitation loss whose weight depends on whether the expert's action is still better, or by training mostly on the hard cases.

### Cited Findings
- (V) PIRLNav: the gain from RL falls as imitation data grow: "at 20k BC demonstrations, the BC→RL improvement is 10.1% success", against 6.3% at 77k (70.4% final success). The kind of expert matters: after RL, policies cloned from human demonstrations reach 66.1% validation success, from frontier exploration 51.3%, from shortest paths 43.6%. — [PIRLNav](https://arxiv.org/abs/2301.07302)
- (V) Lu, Fu, Tucker, Pan, Bronstein, Roelofs, Sapp, White, Faust, Whiteson, Anguelov, Levine (IROS 2023; arXiv:2212.11419): "imitation can perform well in low-difficulty scenarios that are well-covered by the demonstration data". Failure rates for BC / SAC alone / BC plus SAC, all trained on all scenarios: hardest 10% of scenarios 6.72 / 4.64 / 2.88%; all scenarios 4.35 / 6.66 / 3.35%. SAC alone also completes less of the route on all scenarios (77.82% against 99.00% for BC). — [Lu et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2212.11419)
- (V) Same paper, design: the RL reward contains only collision and off-road terms, an imitation term is kept throughout, and one imitation update is made per eight RL updates. The abstract's headline is "over 38% reduction in failures" on the most challenging scenarios. — [Lu et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2212.11419)
- (V) Hoppe, Baty, Bouvier, Parmentier, Schiffer (arXiv:2505.19053, 2025, preprint), combinatorial decision problems: on static problems the RL method "matches the performance" of imitation of the optimal expert; on dynamic vehicle scheduling it is comparable; it beats imitation by 8% on dynamic assortment and by 78% on a gridworld shortest-path problem. Imitation's "performance is bounded by that of the expert policy". PPO "consistently underperforms across all environments" with variance up to 80 times higher. RL was trained from scratch, not from the imitation policy. — [Hoppe et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.19053)
- (V) Reincarnating RL: "a higher performing teacher results in a better performing student", and on humanoid-run the student and a from-scratch agent "converge to similar performance". — [Reincarnating RL](https://arxiv.org/abs/2206.01626)
- (V) Peng et al.: after RL fine-tuning of a cloned driving model, "most of the gains come from improved safety-critical metrics such as collision and offroad". — [Peng et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.18343)
- (V) IBRL (Hu, Mirchandani, Sadigh; RSS 2024; arXiv:2311.02198) keeps the imitation policy unchanged; at each state the imitation action and the RL action are both proposed and the one with the higher Q-value is executed and used in the bootstrap target. It "does not require picking hyper-parameters nor annealing schedules for the BC regularization weight". Real-robot success, IBRL / RLPD / regularised fine-tuning / BC: Lift 100 / 95 / 90 / 50%; Drawer 95 / 85 / 50 / 55%; Hang 85 / 15 / 35 / 65%. On Hang, both RL baselines end below the imitation policy. — [IBRL](https://arxiv.org/abs/2311.02198)
- (V) IBRL's regularised baseline scales its imitation weight by the share of demonstration states where the pretrained policy still has the higher Q-value, and "requires additional tuning" of the base weight (0.1 after a sweep). — [IBRL](https://arxiv.org/abs/2311.02198)
- (R) The Q-filter of Nair, McGrew, Andrychowicz, Zaremba, Abbeel (ICRA 2018; arXiv:1709.10089) applies the imitation loss only on demonstration samples where the critic rates the demonstrated action above the policy's. — [Nair et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/1709.10089)
- (V) Frozen base plus residual: Ankile et al. raise success from 2 to 54% (frozen diffusion base, across nine task settings) to 70 to 99% with a residual scaled by 0.1, where direct PPO fine-tuning of a cloned MLP gained 1 to 33 points and ended at 2 to 70%. — [Ankile et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.16677)
- (R) Related designs: policy expansion keeps the pretrained policy frozen and adds a second policy chosen adaptively (Zhang, Xu, Yu; ICLR 2023; arXiv:2302.00935); Policy Decorator learns a bounded residual on a frozen imitation model with a gradual exploration schedule (Yuan et al.; ICLR 2025; arXiv:2412.13630); jump-start RL lets a guide policy control the early part of each episode (Uchendu et al.; ICML 2023; arXiv:2204.02372). — [PEX](https://arxiv.org/abs/2302.00935); [Policy Decorator](https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.13630); [JSRL](https://arxiv.org/abs/2204.02372)
- (R) Theory for surpassing a suboptimal expert: LOLS gives low regret against both the reference policy and local deviations (arXiv:1502.02206); LOKI switches from imitation to RL after a randomised number of steps and guarantees to outperform a suboptimal expert (Cheng, Yan, Wagener, Boots; UAI 2018; arXiv:1805.10413). — [LOLS](https://arxiv.org/abs/1502.02206); [LOKI](https://arxiv.org/abs/1805.10413)
- (R) AlphaGo's policy-gradient fine-tuned network won more than 80% of games against the supervised network it started from, yet the supervised network worked better inside the search (Silver et al.; Nature 2016; doi:10.1038/nature16961). AlphaStar kept a KL term to the supervised policy throughout RL (Vinyals et al.; Nature 2019; doi:10.1038/s41586-019-1724-z). — [AlphaGo](https://doi.org/10.1038/nature16961); [AlphaStar](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-019-1724-z)

### Inferences
- The context's pattern (RL helps in drayage with dynamic requests, harms long haul where the planner is near-optimal) is the published norm, not an anomaly: Lu et al., Hoppe et al. and PIRLNav all show gains where the expert is weak or the problem is dynamic and none, or losses, elsewhere.
- For a policy that already receives the planner's plan as input, the frozen-base designs are cheap: "follow the plan" is an available action at every decision, so a learned deviation with a per-state gate (IBRL, Q-filter) or a residual initialised at zero gives the planner's behaviour by construction in categories where the critic sees no advantage.
- Per-state gating needs a critic that is accurate on rare failures; with 1 to 5% failure rates a gate that defaults to the expert action unless the estimated advantage clears a margin is the conservative reading of IBRL and of Garcelon et al.'s fallback rule.
- Lu et al.'s result that training on the hardest 10% of scenarios improved all scenarios suggests weighting simulation toward the families and scenarios where the imitation policy fails, while the imitation term protects the rest.

### Gaps
- I found no paper that sets a per-category imitation weight or per-category gate for one multi-family policy, and none on RL fine-tuning from an imitation policy in electric-fleet routing or drayage.
- Hoppe et al. train RL from scratch, so their comparison says where RL can beat imitation, not what fine-tuning does to an imitation policy.
- Lu et al.'s headline 38% is ambiguous against the table rows I read (policies trained on all scenarios, evaluated on the hardest 10%): the combined method has 38% fewer failures than SAC alone (4.64 to 2.88) and 57% fewer than BC (6.72 to 2.88). Check the paper's wording before quoting the 38% as a reduction relative to imitation.
- IBRL and the residual methods use off-policy critics or small PPO residuals; whether per-state gating works with an on-policy value function and a variable-size action set is untested in what I read.
