# Learning through the solver: hybrids in which machine learning steers, parameterises or feeds a planner, and policies trained on hindsight solutions (dynamic vehicle routing and fleet control)

How to read these notes. Numbers are taken from the paper version named in each link (mostly the arXiv version; journal versions can differ slightly). A venue or detail marked "(from memory)" was not confirmed on a page I opened. Arithmetic I did myself on reported figures is in the Inferences blocks, never in Cited Findings. Abbreviations: CO = combinatorial optimisation; FY = Fenchel-Young; HGS = hybrid genetic search; ALNS = adaptive large neighbourhood search; CFA = cost function approximation; HL = hindsight learning; Exo-MDP = Markov decision process with exogenous inputs; MPC = model predictive control; PPO = proximal policy optimisation; "anticipative", "hindsight" and "full-information" all mean a solution computed with the whole future known.

## 1. By what mechanism does each hybrid exceed its underlying solver, and what improvement over a rolling-horizon or greedy baseline is reported?

### Takeaway
Hybrids beat their solver in five distinct ways: (1) they change the objective the solver sees (learned prizes, arc weights, penalties, buffers) so that a deterministic solve anticipates the future; (2) they choose what the solver works on (which requests to release now, which subproblem to re-optimise); (3) they steer the search inside the solver so it finds better solutions in the same time; (4) they give a short-horizon planner a learned value for the end of its horizon; (5) they use the full-information solver only during training, as a low-variance teacher. Reported gains over greedy or myopic baselines are mostly 6 to 17 %. Against a well-built rolling-horizon or scenario-sampling baseline, the best-documented dynamic routing benchmark (EURO Meets NeurIPS 2022) shows gains of only about 0.3 to 2 percentage points; in ride-hailing fleet control the margin over a sampling-based planner is larger, because that planner often did no better than greedy.

### Cited Findings

#### A. Learned objective for a combinatorial optimisation layer (Parmentier and co-authors)
- General idea (Parmentier 2022, Operations Research 70(1):606-623, DOI 10.1287/opre.2020.2094): a machine learning predictor turns an instance of a hard problem variant into an instance of a well-known problem, an existing algorithm solves that instance, and the solution is mapped back; demonstrated on two stochastic vehicle scheduling problems. — [INFORMS](https://pubsonline.informs.org/doi/fpi/10.1287/opre.2020.2094)
- Training tools (Dalle, Baty, Bouvier and Parmentier 2022, arXiv:2207.13513, no journal reference on the arXiv page): a CO layer is piecewise constant in its objective parameters, so ordinary back-propagation gives zero gradients; a probabilistic view of the layer gives approximate derivatives and structured losses; implemented in the Julia package InferOpt.jl. — [arXiv:2207.13513](https://arxiv.org/abs/2207.13513)
- Perturbation (Berthet, Blondel, Teboul, Cuturi, Vert and Bach 2020, arXiv:2002.08676; NeurIPS 2020 from memory): adding random noise to the solver's input makes the solver's expected output differentiable and never locally constant; the method "can be used readily together with existing solvers" and connects to a family of structured-prediction losses. — [arXiv:2002.08676](https://arxiv.org/abs/2002.08676)
- Fenchel-Young losses (Blondel, Martins and Niculae 2020, Journal of Machine Learning Research 21, arXiv:1901.02324): a generic way to build a convex loss from a regularised prediction function; many classical losses are special cases. — [arXiv:1901.02324](https://arxiv.org/abs/1901.02324)

#### B. EURO Meets NeurIPS 2022, dynamic vehicle routing with time windows: winner and other entries
- Competition setting (Kool, Bliek, Numeroso, Zhang, Catshoek, Tierney, Vidal and Gromicho 2023, PMLR 220:35-49): requests arrive in one-hour epochs; at each epoch the solver chooses which requests to dispatch now (with feasible routes) and which to postpone; "must-go" requests cannot wait. Final-phase time limit was 2 minutes per epoch; instances have 200 to 900 customers; 100 hidden test instances. Baselines: greedy (dispatch everything now), lazy (dispatch only must-go), random (dispatch each with probability 50 %). — [PMLR report](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v220/kool23a/kool23a.pdf)
- The organisers' own two learning baselines, a supervised network trained to predict which requests an oracle dispatches and a deep Q-network that selects requests, "did not outperform the greedy baseline". — [PMLR report](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v220/kool23a/kool23a.pdf)
- Final dynamic-variant costs (lower is better): Kleopatra 348,831.56 (rank 1); Team SB 358,161.36 (2); OptiML 359,270.09 (3); HustSmart 361,803.57 (4); ORberto Hood and the Barrymen 362,481.13 (5); UPB 367,007.49 (6); Miles To Go Before We Sleep 369,098.13 (7); HowToRoute 369,797.03 (8). The report states "All the best approaches used machine learning as a key component of their winning policies". — [PMLR report](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v220/kool23a/kool23a.pdf)
- Winner, Kleopatra (Baty, Jungel, Klein, Parmentier and Schiffer 2024, Transportation Science 58(4):708-725, DOI 10.1287/trsc.2023.0107, arXiv:2304.00789). Core idea: a neural network predicts one prize per open request; a prize-collecting vehicle routing problem with time windows is solved by a prize-collecting variant of HGS, which jointly picks the requests to dispatch and their routes. Why it can exceed the plain solver: the solver stays a deterministic single-epoch solve, but the prizes carry the value of dispatching now versus waiting. — [arXiv:2304.00789](https://arxiv.org/abs/2304.00789); [Transportation Science](https://pubsonline.informs.org/doi/fpi/10.1287/trsc.2023.0107)
- Baty et al. training: supervised imitation of an anticipative solution with a perturbed Fenchel-Young loss; the gradient is estimated by sampling Gaussian perturbations of the prizes and calling the solver on each. Default set-up: 15 training instances, 50 requests sampled per epoch, anticipative targets from HGS with a 3,600 s limit, a feed-forward network with four hidden layers of ten neurons. The theory assumes an optimal solver; the authors report that a metaheuristic layer works in practice. — [arXiv:2304.00789](https://arxiv.org/abs/2304.00789)
- Baty et al. results, as average cost gap to the anticipative baseline: lazy 74.05 %, random 36.29 %, greedy 20.99 %, single-scenario rolling horizon (600 s per epoch) 7.12 %, nine-scenario Monte Carlo majority vote (5,400 s per epoch) 6.97 %, the learned pipeline (90 s per epoch) 5.15 %. The paper summarises this as 13.18 % better than greedy and 1.57 % better than Monte Carlo in travel time. — [arXiv:2304.00789](https://arxiv.org/abs/2304.00789)
- Baty et al. ablations (gap to the anticipative baseline): linear model 6.85 %, small neural network 5.15 %, sparsified graph neural network 5.04 %; 1 / 5 / 10 / 15 / 25 training instances give 10.19 / 7.26 / 5.89 / 5.15 / 4.50 %; training on instances with 10 / 25 / 50 / 75 / 100 requests per epoch and testing on 100 gives 8.71 / 6.31 / 5.15 / 7.41 / 13.08 %; features computable from the current state alone give 6.78 %. — [arXiv:2304.00789](https://arxiv.org/abs/2304.00789)
- Team SB (rank 2 on the dynamic variant): two neural networks trained by reinforcement learning, one predicting request priorities and one selecting must-dispatch requests, used inside HGS with a modified objective that weighs priorities against added distance. — [PMLR report](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v220/kool23a/kool23a.pdf)
- OptiML (rank 3): simulate several scenarios of future requests, solve each with the static solver, mark postponable requests that are frequently routed with must-go requests as must-go, repeat, then dispatch routes containing at least one original must-go request. — [PMLR report](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v220/kool23a/kool23a.pdf)
- HustSmart (rank 4): a hand-defined penalty score per request; OR-Tools solves the routing problem with visits droppable at that penalty; HGS then polishes. ORberto Hood (rank 5): one integrated problem over sampled future scenarios solved by column generation. UPB (rank 6): an analytically computed regret value per postponable request with a dispatch threshold. Miles To Go and HowToRoute: dispatch postponable requests that are spatially close to must-go requests. Kirchhoffslaw: a network trained by reinforcement learning to select requests, rewarded against the greedy baseline's cost. — [PMLR report](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v220/kool23a/kool23a.pdf)
- Follow-up on the OptiML line (Lan, van Doorn, Wouda, Rijal and Bhulai 2024, Transportation Science 58(4):726-740, DOI 10.1287/trsc.2023.0111, arXiv:2308.14476): "iterative conditional dispatch" solves sampled scenarios, then fixes requests as dispatch (score at least 0.5) or postpone (score below 0.2) and repeats on the undecided ones; 3 iterations of 30 scenarios at 1 s each plus 30 s for the final plan. Gap to hindsight with 600 expected requests: single-scenario rolling horizon 9.58 %, single-threshold consensus 8.76 %, the two-threshold variant 8.05 %. With no learning it came within 0.8 % of the best competition result at 120 s per epoch, 0.6 % at 180 s and 0.3 % at 600 s. — [ar5iv 2308.14476](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2308.14476)

#### C. Learning-based online optimisation for fleet control
- Jungel, Parmentier, Schiffer and Vidal (2025, INFORMS Journal on Computing, DOI 10.1287/ijoc.2024.0637, arXiv:2302.03963). Core idea: each minute, dispatching and rebalancing of a ride-hailing fleet is a k-disjoint shortest paths problem on a graph of vehicles, requests and artificial rebalancing nodes; a linear model predicts the arc weights; a polynomial-time algorithm solves the layer. Two variants: sample-based (sampled future requests added as nodes) and cell-based (rebalancing cells, no request distribution needed). — [arXiv:2302.03963](https://arxiv.org/abs/2302.03963); [INFORMS](https://pubsonline.informs.org/doi/fpi/10.1287/ijoc.2024.0637)
- Jungel et al. training: Fenchel-Young loss with Gaussian perturbation; the gradient is the expected perturbed solution minus the full-information target; one training example per one-minute period of each historical instance; 5 working days of one-hour data for training and validation, 20 days for testing; training stopped after 70 iterations or 70 hours. — [arXiv:2302.03963](https://arxiv.org/abs/2302.03963)
- Jungel et al. results (profit): up to 17.1 % and on average 6.3 % above a greedy rolling re-optimisation; base scenario (2,000 vehicles) +4.6 % sample-based and +3.8 % cell-based, while a sampling-based receding-horizon policy only matched greedy and was "often performing worse than the greedy policy" elsewhere; fleets of 500 to 5,000 vehicles. The paper adds that state-of-the-art deep reinforcement learning methods "only outperform greedy policies by around 2-5 %". — [arXiv:2302.03963](https://arxiv.org/abs/2302.03963)
- Greif, Bouvier, Flath, Parmentier, Rohmer and Vidal (2024, arXiv:2402.04463): the same pattern for dynamic inventory routing; the network predicts prizes for a capacitated prize-collecting travelling salesman problem; the learned policy costs 23.43 % more than the anticipative baseline against 32.55 % for the best rolling-horizon benchmark, with inference an order of magnitude faster. — [arXiv:2402.04463](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.04463)
- Hoppe, Baty, Bouvier, Parmentier and Schiffer (2025, NeurIPS 2025, arXiv:2505.19053), "structured reinforcement learning": an actor-critic method in which the actor is a network plus CO layer; the score vector is perturbed, each perturbed score is passed through the solver to get candidate actions, a critic scores them, and a softmax-weighted target action is imitated with a Fenchel-Young loss. No expert is needed. On a dynamic vehicle routing environment it matches structured imitation of anticipative solutions and beats PPO applied to the same pipeline by 16 %; on two other dynamic problems it beats structured imitation by 8 % and 78 % and PPO by 77 % and 92 %. — [arXiv:2505.19053](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.19053)
- Enders, Harrison, Pavone and Schiffer (2023, L4DC 2023, arXiv:2212.07313): multi-agent Soft Actor-Critic produces per-vehicle evaluations and a weighted bipartite matching makes the globally coordinated assignment; the abstract reports outperforming benchmarks but gives no percentage. — [arXiv:2212.07313](https://arxiv.org/abs/2212.07313)

#### D. Parametric cost function approximation (detail in section 4)
- Powell and Ghadimi (2022, arXiv:2201.00258): a deterministic lookahead with tunable parameters in the objective or constraints, tuned in a stochastic simulator; in an energy storage problem with rolling forecasts the tuned policy beat the same lookahead with all parameters equal to 1 by 20 to 50 % for most starting points ("roughly 30 percent"), at no extra cost at decision time. — [arXiv:2201.00258](https://arxiv.org/abs/2201.00258)
- Stein, Hildebrandt, Thomas and Ulmer (2024, arXiv:2409.01815): for multi-day technician routing, PPO outputs one state-dependent parameter in [0, 1] that balances urgency against routing efficiency in the score used to build tours; the state-dependent version improves on the best fixed parameter by almost 8 %. — [ar5iv 2409.01815](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2409.01815)

#### E. Reinforcement learning that controls a metaheuristic or delegates to a solver
- Reijnen, Zhang, Lau and Bukhsh (2024, arXiv:2211.00759; ICAPS 2024 from memory), DR-ALNS: PPO (two hidden layers of 64 units) chooses, at every ALNS iteration, the destroy operator, the repair operator, the destroy severity (10 levels) and the simulated-annealing temperature, from problem-agnostic search-state features. Average best collected prize on a stochastic orienteering problem with time windows, sizes 20 / 50 / 100: vanilla ALNS 5.52 / 8.27 / 11.26; ALNS tuned by Bayesian optimisation 5.55 / 8.23 / 11.36; DR-ALNS 5.63 / 8.44 / 11.75. Ablation at size 50: operator selection only 8.28; plus severity 8.31; plus acceptance 8.31; all three 8.44. On the capacitated vehicle routing problem it reached better solutions "with 10x fewer search iterations". — [arXiv:2211.00759](https://arxiv.org/abs/2211.00759)
- Kallestad, Hasibi, Hemmati and Sörensen (2023, European Journal of Operational Research 309(1):446-468): a deep reinforcement learning agent replaces the adaptive weight layer of ALNS for operator selection and selects better than ALNS's own layer and uniform random selection; quantitative gaps were not retrieved. Reijnen et al. report that this method, trained 10 times longer, was on average 1.0 % worse than DR-ALNS on their size-20 instances. — [RePEc record](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/ejores/v309y2023i1p446-468.html); [arXiv:2211.00759](https://arxiv.org/abs/2211.00759)
- Johnn, Darvariu, Handl and Kalcsics (2023, OLA 2023, arXiv:2302.14678): operator selection in ALNS by deep reinforcement learning with a graph neural network on the current solution; outperforms the classic adaptive layer (no figures in the abstract). — [arXiv:2302.14678](https://arxiv.org/abs/2302.14678)
- Hottung and Tierney (2020, ECAI 2020, pp. 443-450, arXiv:1911.09539), neural large neighbourhood search: the repair operator is a learned attention network trained with REINFORCE and a critic baseline; destroy operators and the search loop are hand-made. Gap to the best solver (unified HGS) 0.04 to 3.10 %, against 0.89 to 12.14 % for a hand-made ruin-and-recreate search at the same budget; parity with LKH3; training takes hours to 4 days. — [ar5iv 1911.09539](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1911.09539)
- Li, Yan and Wu (2021, arXiv:2107.04139; NeurIPS 2021 from memory), learning to delegate: a Transformer, trained by regression, picks which spatially local subproblem to hand to a black-box solver; speeds up state-of-the-art solvers 10 to 100 times on 500 to 3,000 cities, and 1.5 to 2 times relative to heuristic or random subproblem selection. — [arXiv:2107.04139](https://arxiv.org/abs/2107.04139)
- Xin, Song, Cao and Zhang (2021, NeurIPS 2021, arXiv:2110.07983), NeuroLKH: a sparse graph network outputs edge scores (supervised) and node penalties (unsupervised) that build the candidate edge set and transform distances for the Lin-Kernighan-Helsgaun heuristic; claimed to significantly outperform LKH and to extend to capacitated, pickup-and-delivery and time-window problems (no figures in the abstract). — [arXiv:2110.07983](https://arxiv.org/abs/2110.07983)

#### F. Learned value function as the terminal cost of a short-horizon planner
- Lowrey, Rajeswaran, Kakade, Todorov and Mordatch (2019, ICLR 2019, arXiv:1811.01848), POLO: local trajectory optimisation corrects errors of an approximate value function, and the value function lets the planner use a shorter horizon and reach better policies than local planning alone; shown on simulated humanoid and manipulation tasks. — [arXiv:1811.01848](https://arxiv.org/abs/1811.01848)
- Bhardwaj, Choudhury and Boots (2021, ICLR 2021, arXiv:2012.05909): treat the planner as a series of local Q-function approximations and blend it with a learned value through a parameter analogous to the trace decay in TD(lambda), lowering reliance on the planner as the value improves; matches a planner with the true model despite a biased model. — [arXiv:2012.05909](https://arxiv.org/abs/2012.05909)
- Shah, Lowalekar and Varakantham (2020, AAAI 2020, arXiv:1911.08842): a neural value function learned from integer-programme assignments for ride-pooling, where existing real-time methods are myopic; "outperforms past approaches" by up to 16 % on real data. — [arXiv:1911.08842](https://arxiv.org/abs/1911.08842)
- Joe and Lau (2020, ICAPS 2020): a neural value function of the post-decision state (total cost of remaining routes and time) evaluates re-routing moves generated by simulated annealing; about 10 % better than a myopic policy on average, and 9.6 % against 1.7 to 2.2 % for a multiple-scenario approach on 100 test scenarios; sampling methods are cited as needing on the order of 100 s per decision. — [ICAPS paper](https://cdn.aaai.org/ojs/6685/6685-40-9914-1-10-20200521.pdf)
- Delarue, Anderson and Tjandraatmadja (2020, arXiv:2010.12001; NeurIPS 2020 from memory): a neural value function is embedded in a mixed-integer programme that chooses the next route; average gap 1.7 % to state-of-the-art solvers on medium library instances. — [arXiv:2010.12001](https://arxiv.org/abs/2010.12001)
- Ulmer, Goodson, Mattfeld and Hennig (2019, Transportation Science 53(1):185-202, DOI 10.1287/trsc.2017.0767): an offline value function approximation is used inside online rollout for single-vehicle routing with stochastic requests; the combination "enhances the anticipation" of the value-function policy. — [EconBiz record](https://www.econbiz.de/Record/offline-online-approximate-dynamic-programming-for-dynamic-vehicle-routing-with-stochastic-requests-ulmer-marlin-wolf/10011999335)
- Cheng, Kolobov and Swaminathan (2021, arXiv:2106.02757; NeurIPS 2021 from memory): a heuristic value function turns the learning task into a provably equivalent shorter-horizon one; an "improvable heuristic" lets the agent exceed the prior knowledge it was given. — [arXiv:2106.02757](https://arxiv.org/abs/2106.02757)

#### G. Learned prizes, acceptance and postponement
- Ma, Hao, Hao, Lu, Liu, Xialiang, Yuan, Li, Tang and Meng (2021, NeurIPS 2021): for dynamic pickup and delivery, an upper-level deep Q-network decides at each of 144 daily decision points whether to release the buffered orders to the solver or to wait for more; a lower-level policy (graph network, REINFORCE) selects improvement operators on the released static problem. Objective improvement over a fixed 10-minute batching with greedy insertion: 14.27 %, 17.16 % and 13.04 % on three data sets, against 1.24 %, 7.08 % and 4.57 % for fixed 10-minute batching with ALNS. — [NeurIPS abstract](https://papers.neurips.cc/paper_files/paper/2021/hash/c6a01432c8138d46ba39957a8250e027-Abstract.html); [paper PDF](https://keg.cs.tsinghua.edu.cn/jietang/publications/NeurIPS21-Ma-et-al-RL4Pickup.pdf)
- Chen, Ulmer and Thomas (2022, European Journal of Operational Research 298(3):939-952, DOI 10.1016/j.ejor.2021.06.021): deep Q-learning values assigning each new same-day customer to a drone, a vehicle, or no service; outperforms the earlier location-only rule. — [RePEc record](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/ejores/v298y2022i3p939-952.html)
- Akkerman, Dieter and Mes (2025, Transportation Science 59(2):250-278, DOI 10.1287/trsc.2023.0434): a learned pipeline for selecting and pricing out-of-home delivery options saves 7 percentage points in cost against a static policy and 3.8 against a demand-management benchmark; PPO is used there as a benchmark, not as the method. — [INFORMS](https://pubsonline.informs.org/doi/fpi/10.1287/trsc.2023.0434)
- Hildebrandt, Thomas and Ulmer (2023, Computers and Operations Research 150:106071, DOI 10.1016/j.cor.2022.106071): position paper arguing that solvers search the action space well and reinforcement learning evaluates actions well with respect to the future, and that existing methods usually do one and not both. — [arXiv:2103.00507](https://arxiv.org/abs/2103.00507v1)

### Inferences
- Derived from Baty et al.'s reported gaps: the learned pipeline is about 1.8 % cheaper than the single-scenario rolling horizon ((1.0712 - 1.0515) / 1.0712) and about 1.7 % cheaper than the nine-scenario vote. Derived from the competition table: the winner's dynamic cost is 2.6 % below Team SB and 2.9 % below OptiML. Together with Lan et al. (0.3 to 0.8 % behind the winner with no learning), the evidence is that on this benchmark most of the gain comes from anticipating at all; the learned prizes add roughly one to two points over careful sampling, at far lower decision-time cost.
- Derived from Reijnen et al.'s table: DR-ALNS is 2.0 / 2.1 / 4.4 % above vanilla ALNS and 1.4 / 2.6 / 3.4 % above the Bayesian-tuned ALNS at sizes 20 / 50 / 100. Search-control hybrids (family E) improve the solution found within a time budget; they cannot exceed the solver's converged quality. For a planner already within 0.4 to 3 % of best-known solutions, that range is the ceiling for this family.
- Families A to D and G change which problem the solver solves, so they can exceed a rolling planner by more than the planner's optimality gap. The gain exists only where the future is uncertain and deferral, acceptance or positioning matters. This matches the project pattern: large PPO gain on drayage with dynamic requests, none on deterministic long haul.
- Combination with a graph neural network policy trained by PPO over a variable-size discrete action set, by family:

| Family | What the network would output | Who takes the final decision | Fit with the existing policy |
|---|---|---|---|
| A, B, C (CO layer) | One score per request or per arc; a graph network gives variable-size outputs natively | The solver | Reuses the graph encoder; replaces the categorical action head by a score head. PPO is possible by treating the perturbed score vector as the action, but Hoppe et al. found that weaker than their Fenchel-Young actor-critic |
| D (parametric CFA) | A few planner parameters (discretised levels or a Gaussian mean), per replanning event | The planner, with modified costs or constraints | Smallest change; PPO stays as is with a small extra head; the default parameter value reproduces the planner |
| E (search control) | Operator, severity, temperature for the ALNS inside the planner | The metaheuristic | Separate small policy; only useful where planner quality within its time limit is the bottleneck |
| F (terminal value) | The PPO critic's state value | The planner, with the critic as end-of-horizon cost | No new network; lets the planner horizon shrink, which also cuts simulation cost |
| G (release or defer) | A binary or per-request "serve now / hold" action | The policy for the release decision, the planner for routing | Adds a small number of discrete actions to the existing variable action set |
| Hindsight learning (section 3) | Unchanged action distribution | The policy | Changes only the training signal: planner-computed hindsight values replace Monte Carlo returns |

## 2. How are anticipative (hindsight) solutions turned into per-decision training targets for a dynamic policy, and what are the documented pitfalls of imitating a clairvoyant expert?

### Takeaway
Three recipes exist: replay the full-information solution along its own trajectory and cut it into per-epoch decisions (Baty, Jungel); re-solve a full-information problem from every state the learner visits and keep the first decision (Greif's anticipative DAgger, optionally with several sampled futures voting); or label every available action with a hindsight-planner value and train on values, not actions (Sinclair). The pitfalls are well documented: copying hindsight-optimal actions is biased because the expert never hedges; the learner cannot reproduce an expert that sees more; better hindsight targets do not give better policies; and naive per-request classification of oracle decisions failed to beat greedy in the 2022 competition.

### Cited Findings
- Replay and cut (Baty et al.): solve the static problem with all requests known, each request carrying a release time equal to its reveal time; a route is dispatched in the first epoch by which all its requests are revealed; the per-epoch target is the set of routes dispatched then. States are those met along the anticipative trajectory. — [arXiv:2304.00789](https://arxiv.org/abs/2304.00789)
- Replay and cut (Jungel et al.): solve the full-information problem for the whole horizon; for each one-minute period rebuild the graph of the state at that time and attach the part of the full-information solution that falls in it. — [arXiv:2302.03963](https://arxiv.org/abs/2302.03963)
- Re-solve from learner states (Greif et al., "Anticipative-DAgger"): roll in with a mixture of the anticipative policy and the learner, shifting weight to the learner over iterations; at each visited state solve the anticipative problem and add only the first-period decision. A "voting" variant samples M futures per state and adds one target per future; it is more expensive in training and gives a better policy. — [arXiv:2402.04463](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.04463)
- Value labels (Sinclair et al.): sample an exogenous trace, roll out the current policy, and label each visited state with the hindsight value of every action (reward now plus the hindsight planner's optimum from the next state on that trace); train by a policy loss weighted by these values or by regressing a critic on them. The authors frame this as AggreVaTe-style online imitation with expert values. — [arXiv:2207.06272](https://arxiv.org/abs/2207.06272)
- Pitfall, action cloning is biased: in Sinclair et al.'s two-route sailing example, the hindsight-optimal route is route 1 in 51 % of traces, yet route 2 is better in expectation by 48 time units; a behaviour-cloned policy converges to the 51 / 49 mixture or its mode. They call this value-agnostic approach "uncontrollably biased". — [arXiv:2207.06272](https://arxiv.org/abs/2207.06272)
- Pitfall, optimism: hindsight planners give estimates of the optimal action value that are "overly optimistic"; the information-relaxation literature corrects this with penalties for using future information (Brown, Smith and Sun 2010, Operations Research 58(4):785-801, DOI 10.1287/opre.1090.0796), but how to construct such penalties in practice for arbitrary exogenous processes is described as unknown. — [arXiv:2207.06272](https://arxiv.org/abs/2207.06272); [Brown, Smith and Sun](https://pubsonline.informs.org/doi/10.1287/opre.1090.0796)
- Pitfall, anticipatory gap: Mercier and Van Hentenryck (2007, IJCAI-07, pp. 1979-1984) split the error of online anticipatory algorithms into a sampling error, negligible with a logarithmic number of scenarios, and an anticipativity error, which they show to be low for the applications then studied. — [IJCAI](https://www.ijcai.org/Abstract/07/319)
- Pitfall, privileged information: when the teacher decides with information the student lacks, that information is "marginalized" during imitation and performance can be poor (Weihs et al. 2021, NeurIPS 2021, arXiv:2007.12173); such experts are "impossibly good", "no learning algorithm will be able to reproduce their behavior", and environment reward must be brought in when conditions on the expert fail (Walsman, Zhang, Choudhury, Fox and Farhadi 2023, ICLR 2023). — [arXiv:2007.12173](https://arxiv.org/abs/2007.12173); [ICLR 2023](https://mlanthology.org/iclr/2023/walsman2023iclr-impossibly)
- Counter-example where it works: Choudhury et al. (2017, arXiv:1711.06391) train planning policies by imitating a clairvoyant oracle with full map knowledge, with performance guarantees, and beat state-of-the-art planners. — [arXiv:1711.06391](https://arxiv.org/abs/1711.06391)
- Pitfall acknowledged by the authors who use it: Baty et al. write that the "general consensus in literature" is that imitating an anticipative strategy does not generalise well, and call their positive result surprising; Greif et al. write that anticipative policies "are known to be often sub-optimal". — [arXiv:2304.00789](https://arxiv.org/abs/2304.00789); [arXiv:2402.04463](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.04463)
- Pitfall, target quality is not monotone: Baty et al. trained on anticipative targets computed with 60 s to 3,600 s and best-of-ten-runs; policy gaps were 5.81 % (60 s), 5.33 % (120 s), 7.61 % (300 s), 4.79 % (900 s), 5.15 % (3,600 s), 6.18 % (best of ten). Their Result 6: "The anticipative strategy might not be the best policy to imitate". — [arXiv:2304.00789](https://arxiv.org/abs/2304.00789)
- Pitfall, end of horizon: in inventory routing, cutting a whole anticipative trajectory into targets makes the policy deliver too little, because the full-information solution runs stock down before the horizon ends; Greif et al. therefore keep only the first-period decision. — [arXiv:2402.04463](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.04463)
- Pitfall, degenerate loss: without perturbation the natural imitation loss is minimised by all-zero scores, which let the solver return anything; the Gaussian perturbation removes this. — [arXiv:2304.00789](https://arxiv.org/abs/2304.00789)
- Pitfall, expert bound and no exploration: Hoppe et al. name two limits of structured imitation, performance "bounded by that of the expert policy" and no exploration to escape poor states; on one dynamic problem their reinforcement method beat structured imitation by 78 %. — [arXiv:2505.19053](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.19053)
- Pitfall, naive supervised targets: the competition's supervised baseline, a network predicting which requests the oracle dispatches, did not beat greedy, whereas the winner imitated the same kind of oracle through a solver layer. — [PMLR report](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v220/kool23a/kool23a.pdf)

### Inferences
- The difference between the failed supervised baseline and the winning entry, both imitating an oracle, suggests the loss matters more than the teacher: the winner compares whole solver outputs with the target (a decision-level loss), while per-request classification scores each request independently. For a per-truck categorical policy, cross-entropy on hindsight actions is the per-item classification case and inherits Sinclair's bias; value labels or a solver layer avoid it.
- Baty et al. build the data set once from expert trajectories (no DAgger step is described); Greif et al. changed this to learner-visited states. A project that already runs DAgger from a planner can swap the planner for a full-information solver at the labelling step, but each label then needs a full-information solve from a mid-episode state.
- In a deterministic problem category (no exogenous randomness) the hindsight solution is simply the optimal solution, so imitation carries no hindsight bias; the only bias left is that the target is one of many near-optimal plans.

### Gaps
- No paper was found that measures hindsight bias for dynamic pickup-and-delivery or drayage with time windows.
- Walsman et al.'s method details and Weihs et al.'s numbers were not read beyond the abstracts.
- Whether the journal version of Baty et al. adds a DAgger-style step was not checked.

## 3. What does hindsight learning for exogenous-input MDPs guarantee, and which problem features (exogenous request arrivals, exogenous queue or travel-time noise) make it applicable?

### Takeaway
Hindsight learning guarantees that the learned policy converges to the "hindsight surrogate" policy (act greedily on the average of hindsight-planner values over sampled futures), whose regret is bounded by a problem-specific "hindsight bias"; it does not guarantee optimality, and some problems have regret linear in the horizon. It applies when all randomness is exogenous (independent of actions), the dynamics and reward are known functions of state, action and exogenous input, traces of the exogenous input are available, and a full-information planner, exact or approximate, exists.

### Cited Findings
- Source: Sinclair, Frujeri, Cheng, Marshall, Barbalho, Li, Neville, Menache and Swaminathan (2023, arXiv:2207.06272; ICML 2023 as named in the assignment). — [arXiv:2207.06272](https://arxiv.org/abs/2207.06272)
- Setting: the only uncertainty comes from exogenous variables outside the decision maker's control; dynamics and reward functions are known; the exogenous distribution is unknown and does not depend on actions, so "action exploration is not needed" and any policy can be evaluated on any recorded trace. — [arXiv:2207.06272](https://arxiv.org/abs/2207.06272)
- Surrogate policy: act greedily on the expectation over futures of (reward now plus hindsight optimum from the next state). Running it online needs planner calls at every state, so it is distilled into a network offline; "the planning is not needed online during test time". — [arXiv:2207.06272](https://arxiv.org/abs/2207.06272)
- Relation to policy-gradient methods: the authors state that algorithms which estimate action values or advantages by Monte Carlo, citing PPO among them, can adopt hindsight learning "by replacing the Monte Carlo estimates" with the hindsight values. — [arXiv:2207.06272](https://arxiv.org/abs/2207.06272)
- Hindsight bias (Definition 1): the planner's over-estimation at the surrogate's action minus its over-estimation at the optimal action; it can be small even when over-estimation is large, and is zero when both rank actions alike. Theorem 2: the surrogate's regret is at most the sum over time of the expected hindsight bias on the states the surrogate visits. — [arXiv:2207.06272](https://arxiv.org/abs/2207.06272)
- Theorem 3: with N traces and a no-regret optimiser, the learned policy's regret is at most a term of order T times the square root of log(policy-class size)/N, plus the summed empirical hindsight bias; the result "is not asymptotically consistent due to the hindsight bias". Theorem 4: there are exogenous-input problems where the surrogate's regret grows linearly in the horizon. — [arXiv:2207.06272](https://arxiv.org/abs/2207.06272)
- Where the bias is small: stochastic online bin packing with independent arrivals (constant regret), multi-secretary and airline revenue management. For virtual-machine allocation with non-independent arrivals and departures "the bound on hindsight bias does not apply" and the bias is unknown. — [arXiv:2207.06272](https://arxiv.org/abs/2207.06272)
- Comparison with forecasting then planning: for predict-then-optimise the guarantees "scale quadratically in T" and depend on the complexity of the exogenous distribution, while the hindsight learning bound scales linearly in the horizon. — [arXiv:2207.06272](https://arxiv.org/abs/2207.06272)
- Results: virtual-machine allocation on 80 machines, machines saved relative to Best Fit: oracle bound 4.96; hindsight actor 4.33; hindsight critic 3.71; PPO -0.50; deep Q-network -0.64; policy gradient with a hindsight baseline -0.057. On five real clusters (horizon about 100,000 steps, six-layer graph network) gains were 0.1 to 0.7 % over Best Fit and 0.1 to 1.6 % over reinforcement learning, with a hand-made hindsight heuristic because even the linear relaxation was intractable. Tabular tests: within 2.1 to 2.6 % of optimal on multi-secretary where Q-learning was up to 25 % below. — [arXiv:2207.06272](https://arxiv.org/abs/2207.06272)
- Approximate planners are allowed: the algorithms rely only on the planner's objective value, so linear or fluid relaxations and heuristics can be used; the authors note that "using a heuristic for hindsight learning is inherently sub-optimal". — [arXiv:2207.06272](https://arxiv.org/abs/2207.06272)
- Related work: Wan, Sinclair, Shah and Wainwright (2024, arXiv:2409.14557) define the class by a state split into exogenous components that evolve independently of actions and endogenous components that evolve deterministically, name inventory control, resource management and ride-sharing as applications, and prove regret bounds that do not depend on the size of the action space or endogenous state space. — [arXiv:2409.14557](https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.14557)
- Related work: Mao, Venkatakrishnan, Schwarzkopf and Alizadeh (2019, arXiv:1807.02264; ICLR 2019 from memory) show that in input-driven environments a baseline that depends on the input sequence is unbiased and reduces policy-gradient variance; Sinclair et al. report that this kind of control variate "is not enough" in their experiments. — [arXiv:1807.02264](https://arxiv.org/abs/1807.02264); [arXiv:2207.06272](https://arxiv.org/abs/2207.06272)
- Suggested remedy for large bias: Sinclair et al. point to combining hindsight learning with reward shaping (Cheng et al. 2021) to trade hindsight bias against variance. — [arXiv:2207.06272](https://arxiv.org/abs/2207.06272)

### Inferences
- Request arrivals that do not react to fleet decisions are exogenous in exactly this sense, so same-day pickup-and-delivery and drayage with dynamic requests fit. Travel-time noise and the arrivals of other operators' vehicles at public chargers also fit if they do not depend on the fleet's own choices; the queue length itself may depend on the fleet's actions and still be allowed, provided it is a known function of state, action and the exogenous arrivals.
- The existing event-driven simulator supplies the known dynamics and reward; the adaptive large neighbourhood search or branch-and-price code supplies the hindsight planner. The missing piece is a planner entry point that starts from an arbitrary mid-episode state with one candidate first action fixed.
- With a variable-size action set, labelling a state costs one full-information solve per available action. Sinclair et al. sum over all actions only "because the action spaces are relatively small", so labelling the sampled action plus a few alternatives is consistent with the paper.
- For PPO specifically, the least invasive use is to compute the advantage as hindsight value of the taken action minus the mean hindsight value at that state on the same trace. This is a low-variance, biased replacement for the generalised advantage estimate. It keeps the policy-gradient-trained network as the decision maker.
- The bias is likely largest where the right action is to hedge against a future the planner would exploit if it knew it, for example holding a truck back for a request that may not come. That is the regime in which drayage gains were found, so the bias should be measured there (for instance by comparing the surrogate policy with PPO on small instances) before relying on hindsight learning.

### Gaps
- No guarantee covers approximate planners with unknown gaps, or non-independent arrivals; the bounded-bias results are for independent arrivals in packing-type problems.
- No source was found that applies hindsight learning to vehicle routing or fleet control; its transfer to routing is an inference.
- The ICML 2023 proceedings entry was not opened; venue taken from the assignment.

## 4. How is a parametric cost function approximation tuned, what parameters are typically exposed (buffers, penalties, prizes, horizon), and can the default planner be kept as a special case so that the tuned policy cannot be worse in training?

### Takeaway
A parametric CFA is a deterministic planner with a small vector of tunable parameters in its costs or constraints, tuned by simulation: stochastic gradient where the planner is a linear programme, finite-difference or simultaneous-perturbation methods otherwise, plain enumeration for one or two parameters, or a neural network trained by PPO when the parameters depend on the state. The default planner is a special case by construction (multipliers equal to 1, shifts equal to 0), and the published comparisons use exactly that case as the benchmark and starting point; "cannot be worse in training" then holds only if the tuner keeps the default whenever it does not win on the same training scenarios, because the tuning objective is noisy and non-convex.

### Cited Findings
- Framework: Powell (2019, European Journal of Operational Research, DOI 10.1016/j.ejor.2018.07.014) identifies four classes of policies: policy function approximations, cost function approximations, policies based on value function approximations, and direct lookaheads; hybrids are allowed. — [Powell, EJOR preprint](https://castle.princeton.edu/wp-content/uploads/2018/08/Powell-EJOR-Unified-Framework-for-Stoch-Opt-Aug-16-2018.pdf); [Princeton record](https://collaborate.princeton.edu/en/publications/a-unified-framework-for-stochastic-optimization/)
- Definition and motivation (Powell and Ghadimi 2022, arXiv:2201.00258): industry routinely solves a deterministic model with ad hoc parameters added for robustness; the paper treats this as a policy class, a hybrid of deterministic lookahead and CFA, whose parameters must be tuned in a simulator of the real stochastic problem. Stated advantages: the simulator need not assume that exogenous information is independent of decisions, and the policy "will generally have the same computational demands" as the plain deterministic lookahead. — [arXiv:2201.00258](https://arxiv.org/abs/2201.00258)
- Parameters exposed in the paper's templates: a linear correction to the objective (weights times features of state and decision); multipliers and shifts on constraint right-hand sides; reduced upper bounds and raised lower bounds (buffers, reserves); schedule slack entered through the constraint matrix; a bonus proportional to how long a load has waited in a driver-to-load assignment; forecast multipliers indexed by how far ahead the forecast looks. — [arXiv:2201.00258](https://arxiv.org/abs/2201.00258)
- Scaling: CFA coefficients "tend to be scaled by the structure of the deterministic optimization model"; in the energy storage example the optimal coefficients are all 1.0 under perfect forecasts and lie in about [0, 2] otherwise. — [arXiv:2201.00258](https://arxiv.org/abs/2201.00258)
- Default as special case and benchmark: the tuned policy is compared "against a base policy with theta = 1", one search is started from theta = 1, and most runs improve by 20 to 50 %. The paper warns that the objective "is nonconvex in theta" and that simulating policies "can also be quite noisy". — [arXiv:2201.00258](https://arxiv.org/abs/2201.00258)
- Gradient-based tuning (Perkins and Powell 2017, arXiv:1703.04644): for a linear lookahead with parameters only on right-hand sides, the gradient of simulated cost follows from the optimal basis of each linear programme and a chain rule along the trajectory; ADAGRAD step sizes. Improvement over the plain deterministic lookahead on a 24-period storage problem: constant multiplier 13 to 17 %, lookup table by lookahead period 20 to 25 %, exponential form 14 to 26 %. A storage-bound parameterisation gave 0 %: the search returned the unparameterised policy. — [ar5iv 1703.04644](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1703.04644)
- Derivative-free tuning (Ghadimi, Perkins and Powell 2020, arXiv:2001.00831; Ghadimi and Powell 2022, arXiv:2204.07317): because unbiased gradients need restrictive assumptions, a simulation-based stochastic approximation with numerical derivatives is used, with variance reduction to control the bias of finite differences from noisy simulations and a finite-time convergence rate; Powell and Ghadimi describe the algorithm as based on simultaneous perturbation stochastic approximation. — [arXiv:2001.00831](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2001.00831); [arXiv:2204.07317](https://arxiv.org/abs/2204.07317); [arXiv:2201.00258](https://arxiv.org/abs/2201.00258)
- State-dependent parameters from a neural network (Stein et al. 2024, arXiv:2409.01815): PPO; the network outputs the mean of a Gaussian from which the parameter is sampled in training, the mean is used in evaluation; the fixed-parameter baseline is found by enumeration; state-dependent beats fixed by almost 8 %, and a variant that also learns the exploration rate by about 10 % at roughly 100,000 training iterations instead of 15,000. The authors frame the scored assignment as a CFA in Powell's sense. — [ar5iv 2409.01815](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2409.01815)
- Differentiating through the planner (Agrawal, Barratt, Boyd and Stellato 2019, arXiv:1912.09529; L4DC 2020 from memory): when the policy is a convex optimisation problem, its parameters can be tuned along an approximate gradient of a performance metric obtained by differentiating the solution map. — [arXiv:1912.09529](https://arxiv.org/abs/1912.09529)
- Use in dynamic pickup and delivery (Horváth, Kis and Győrgyi 2024, arXiv:2405.01915, related DOI 10.1016/j.multra.2025.100194): at each decision epoch a variable neighbourhood search solves the routing problem with a perturbed objective that penalises waiting times and idle vehicles, so that routes stay adaptable to new requests; the problem has limited docking ports that force vehicles to wait; the abstract says the method "significantly surpasses" state-of-the-art methods on a common benchmark, without figures. — [arXiv:2405.01915](https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.01915)
- Use in on-demand delivery (Liu and Luo 2021, arXiv:2107.13058): the cost-to-go is approximated through a parametrised dispatching and routing policy; 36.53 % shorter delivery time on average than the company's current dispatching policy on real data. — [arXiv:2107.13058](https://arxiv.org/abs/2107.13058)
- Residual analogue (Silver, Allen, Tenenbaum and Kaelbling 2018, arXiv:1812.06298): a learned correction on top of an existing, possibly non-differentiable controller "consistently and substantially improves on the initial controllers" in six simulated control tasks. — [arXiv:1812.06298](https://arxiv.org/abs/1812.06298)

### Inferences
- The request prizes of Baty et al. and Greif et al., the penalty scores of HustSmart and the regret threshold of UPB are all CFA parameters in Powell's vocabulary: a deterministic single-epoch solver with a modified objective. What the Parmentier line adds is a per-request, state-dependent parameter vector and a way to fit it from hindsight solutions instead of by simulation search.
- A no-worse-in-training construction: (i) parameterise so that a known value reproduces the default planner exactly; (ii) initialise the network's output layer to emit that value in every state (zero-initialised residual around the default), so the first policy is the planner itself, not a learned copy of it; (iii) evaluate candidates against the default on the same exogenous traces (common random numbers) and accept only improvements. This guarantees no loss on the training scenarios, not on new ones. Perkins and Powell's 0 % case shows the fallback working.
- This construction addresses the reported "slightly worse copy of the planner" result: a policy that must reproduce the plan through per-truck action choices accumulates imitation error, whereas a policy that emits planner parameters equals the planner at its default output.
- Parameters natural for the described problem set, each with a neutral default: a deferral prize or holding bonus per open request; a lateness-penalty multiplier; a time buffer added to expected charger waiting time; a minimum state-of-charge reserve; a driving-time buffer before a mandatory rest; the planning horizon length. Horizon is a discrete choice and fits a categorical head.
- PPO with a low-dimensional parameter action needs one planner solve per step, the same cost as now, but has far fewer action dimensions to explore than per-truck actions. Finite-difference tuning needs two full episodes per gradient sample; with 100 to 1,400 decisions of 0.05 to 2 s each, a fixed (state-independent) parameter vector tuned by enumeration or Bayesian search over a handful of parameters is the cheapest first experiment, as in Stein et al.'s baseline.

### Gaps
- No published guarantee was found that a tuned CFA is no worse than its default out of sample.
- A web search also surfaced CFAs for a stochastic dynamic travelling salesman problem, local delivery (two parameters in a set-packing model) and crowd-shipping (one parameter tuned by offline simulation); these papers were not opened, and how Horváth et al. set their penalty weights is not stated in their abstract.
- Journal details of Ghadimi and Powell (2022) and the volume and pages of Powell (2019) were not confirmed.
- The claim that residual policies start exactly at the base controller through zero initialisation is from memory of Silver et al., not from the abstract I read.
- Whether sequentially improving rollout provably cannot be worse than its base policy (Goodson, Thomas and Ohlmann 2017, European Journal of Operational Research 258(1):216-229, DOI 10.1016/j.ejor.2016.09.040) could not be confirmed from the pages retrieved; the record is [here](https://iro.uiowa.edu/esploro/outputs/journalArticle/A-rollout-algorithm-framework-for-heuristic/9984380376102771).

## 5. Which of these approaches keep a policy-gradient-trained neural policy as the decision maker, and which replace it with a solver call?

### Takeaway
Most of the strongest dynamic-routing hybrids make the solver the decision maker and reduce the network to a parameteriser trained by imitation, not by policy gradient. A policy-gradient-trained network remains the decision maker in three places: state-dependent planner parameters (Stein et al., PPO), search control inside a metaheuristic (DR-ALNS with PPO, neural large neighbourhood search with REINFORCE), and release-or-wait decisions above a solver; hindsight learning keeps the network as sole decision maker and changes only its training signal.

### Cited Findings
- Solver decides, network parameterises, trained by supervised imitation with Fenchel-Young loss (no policy gradient): Baty et al.; Jungel et al.; Greif et al. — [arXiv:2304.00789](https://arxiv.org/abs/2304.00789); [arXiv:2302.03963](https://arxiv.org/abs/2302.03963); [arXiv:2402.04463](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.04463)
- Solver decides, network trained by reinforcement learning: Hoppe et al. train the score network with a critic and a Fenchel-Young actor update; their PPO baseline on the same pipeline samples a Gaussian-perturbed score vector as the action and was the weakest and highest-variance method (variance up to 80 times that of their method). — [arXiv:2505.19053](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.19053)
- Solver decides, networks trained by reinforcement learning to supply priorities: Team SB. — [PMLR report](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v220/kool23a/kool23a.pdf)
- Planner decides, PPO-trained network sets its parameter per state: Stein et al. — [ar5iv 2409.01815](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2409.01815)
- Policy-gradient network decides the search control, the metaheuristic produces the solution: DR-ALNS (PPO); neural large neighbourhood search (REINFORCE); Ma et al.'s lower level (REINFORCE). — [arXiv:2211.00759](https://arxiv.org/abs/2211.00759); [ar5iv 1911.09539](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1911.09539); [paper PDF](https://keg.cs.tsinghua.edu.cn/jietang/publications/NeurIPS21-Ma-et-al-RL4Pickup.pdf)
- Value-based network decides a coarse action, a solver or heuristic does the routing: Ma et al.'s upper level (deep Q-network, release or wait); Chen et al. (deep Q-network, vehicle, drone or no service). — [paper PDF](https://keg.cs.tsinghua.edu.cn/jietang/publications/NeurIPS21-Ma-et-al-RL4Pickup.pdf); [RePEc record](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/ejores/v298y2022i3p939-952.html)
- Planner or search decides, a learned value function scores its candidates (no policy network): POLO; Shah et al.; Joe and Lau; Delarue et al.; Ulmer et al. — [arXiv:1811.01848](https://arxiv.org/abs/1811.01848); [arXiv:1911.08842](https://arxiv.org/abs/1911.08842); [ICAPS paper](https://cdn.aaai.org/ojs/6685/6685-40-9914-1-10-20200521.pdf); [arXiv:2010.12001](https://arxiv.org/abs/2010.12001)
- Network alone decides at run time, solver used only in training: hindsight learning, in an actor form and a critic form. — [arXiv:2207.06272](https://arxiv.org/abs/2207.06272)
- No network at decision time: sampling and consensus methods (OptiML, Lan et al., ORberto Hood) and fixed-parameter CFAs. — [ar5iv 2308.14476](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2308.14476); [arXiv:2201.00258](https://arxiv.org/abs/2201.00258)
- Position of the field: Hildebrandt et al. argue that neither pure solver search nor pure reinforcement learning suffices and that methods should search the action space with optimisation while evaluating actions with learning. — [arXiv:2103.00507](https://arxiv.org/abs/2103.00507v1)

### Inferences
- To keep the event-driven MDP and the variable-action graph PPO policy as the core contribution, the options that leave PPO in charge are: (a) hindsight values as the PPO training signal; (b) extra action heads that set planner parameters or issue release-or-hold decisions, with the planner executing; (c) the PPO critic reused as the planner's terminal value. Option (b) is the only one with a direct published precedent using PPO in routing (Stein et al.).
- A CO-layer design in the Baty style would move the contribution away from PPO: the published evidence is that such pipelines train well by imitation and by a dedicated actor-critic, and poorly by PPO over perturbed scores.
- A mixed design is consistent with all sources: the policy keeps its per-truck discrete actions and gains one additional meta-action per decision, "execute the planner's proposal under parameter setting k". With k = default this is the current "follow the plan" behaviour, now exact by construction.

### Gaps
- No source compares, on one dynamic routing benchmark, a per-vehicle policy-gradient policy against a solver-layer policy and a CFA-parameter policy; the ranking between them is not established.
- No published PPO method with a variable-size discrete action set over planner parameters was found.

## 6. What are the compute costs at training and at decision time?

### Takeaway
Decision-time cost is essentially one solver call in every hybrid (seconds for polynomial layers and CFA lookaheads, tens of seconds for metaheuristic layers), and zero solver calls for hindsight learning and value-only methods. Training cost is where the families differ: solver-layer imitation needs full-information solves for targets plus several solver calls per gradient sample; hindsight learning needs a full-information solve per labelled action; simulation-tuned CFAs and PPO-set parameters need full episodes per evaluation; search-control policies train in hours.

### Cited Findings
- Baty et al.: targets from HGS at 3,600 s per training instance (15 instances by default; quality saturates near 10); decision time 90 s per epoch, with gaps of 5.66 % at 30 s, 5.18 % at 60 s, 5.15 % at 90 s and 4.87 % at 240 s; the compared rolling-horizon and Monte Carlo baselines were given 600 s and 5,400 s per epoch. — [arXiv:2304.00789](https://arxiv.org/abs/2304.00789)
- Jungel et al.: training capped at 70 iterations or 70 hours; average online decision time over scenario sizes 0.069 to 3.04 s (sample-based) and 0.093 to 4.17 s (cell-based), against 0.012 to 1.38 s for greedy. — [arXiv:2302.03963](https://arxiv.org/abs/2302.03963)
- Hoppe et al.: about 30 minutes of training per environment; per update the solver layer is called twice by PPO, 20 times by structured imitation and up to 61 times by their method; "computational cost scales with CO-layer complexity". — [arXiv:2505.19053](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.19053)
- Greif et al.: the voting variant is "computationally expensive during training"; inference is an order of magnitude faster than rolling-horizon benchmarks. — [arXiv:2402.04463](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.04463)
- Lan et al. (no training): 90 scenario solves of 1 s plus 30 s per epoch; longer scenario solves help with diminishing returns (0.25 s to 1 s cuts gaps by about 2.5 to 4 points). — [ar5iv 2308.14476](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2308.14476)
- Sinclair et al.: each labelled state needs hindsight values for the actions, hence planner calls during training only; on real clusters the exact planner and its linear relaxation were intractable and a heuristic planner was used. — [arXiv:2207.06272](https://arxiv.org/abs/2207.06272)
- Powell and Ghadimi: no added cost in the field; all cost is offline tuning in the simulator. — [arXiv:2201.00258](https://arxiv.org/abs/2201.00258)
- Stein et al.: PPO converged after about 15,000 iterations (about 100,000 for the variant with learned exploration); no wall-clock time in the part read. — [ar5iv 2409.01815](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2409.01815)
- Reijnen et al.: training 0.5, 2.5 and 10 hours for sizes 20, 50 and 100; solve time 5 s, 30 s and 3.5 min against 4 s, 12 s and 2 min for plain ALNS. — [arXiv:2211.00759](https://arxiv.org/abs/2211.00759)
- Hottung and Tierney: training from several hours to 4 days per operator; search time comparable to the hand-made search. — [ar5iv 1911.09539](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1911.09539)
- Ma et al.: the lower-level solver runs for at most 10 minutes per released batch; both levels are trained simultaneously. — [paper PDF](https://keg.cs.tsinghua.edu.cn/jietang/publications/NeurIPS21-Ma-et-al-RL4Pickup.pdf)
- Joe and Lau: decisions "almost instantaneously" after training on thousands of episodes, against about 100 s per decision reported for sampling-based methods. — [ICAPS paper](https://cdn.aaai.org/ojs/6685/6685-40-9914-1-10-20200521.pdf)
- POLO: the learned value shortens the planning horizon the optimiser needs. — [arXiv:1811.01848](https://arxiv.org/abs/1811.01848)

### Inferences
- With simulation at 0.05 to 2 s per decision and 100 to 1,400 decisions per episode, one episode costs roughly 5 s to 47 minutes. Methods whose training unit is a whole episode (PPO, finite-difference CFA tuning) are the expensive ones here; methods whose training unit is a labelled state (solver-layer imitation, hindsight learning) can reuse a fixed bank of states and traces and parallelise the solver calls.
- Solver-layer imitation in the Baty style needs few instances (saturation near 10 to 15), which suits a costly simulator, but each gradient sample needs several perturbed solver calls; with a planner call of 0.05 to 2 s this is affordable, with a 90 s metaheuristic it is not.
- Hindsight learning's cost is (states labelled) x (actions labelled per state) x (full-information solve time from mid-episode). Fast, approximate solves are acceptable in the method as published, but their sub-optimality feeds directly into the labels.
- A terminal value from the existing critic is the only option that can reduce decision-time cost, by shortening the planner horizon.

### Gaps
- Baty et al.'s total training wall-clock time and number of solver calls per gradient step were not captured from the text read.
- No source reports compute costs for these methods on event-driven simulators with second-scale decision cost; the estimates above are extrapolations.
