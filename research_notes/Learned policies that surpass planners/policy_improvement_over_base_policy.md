# Policy improvement over a base policy: how a learned policy can match and then exceed the expert or planner it started from

Verification conventions used in these notes:
- Every bullet under "Cited Findings" was read in this session from the linked page (arXiv/ar5iv full text unless marked **[abstract only]** or **[search snippet]**).
- **[venue: prior knowledge]** means the fetched page did not print the venue; the venue is from my memory and was not re-checked.
- Exact numbers are quoted only where the source text printed them. Many papers report results only in figures; that is said explicitly.
- "Base policy" = the expert, heuristic or planner the learner starts from.

## 1. Which methods give a formal "no worse than the base policy" guarantee, and under which assumptions (exact values, sequential consistency, enough samples)?

### Takeaway
An exact "no worse than the base policy" statement exists only for a small family: rollout / one-step policy improvement on exact base-policy values (plus fortified rollout, which is unconditional in deterministic problems), policy switching over several base policies with exact values, the Gumbel improvement operator with exact action values, and conservative / trust-region mixtures with exact advantages. Everything else is weaker: cost-to-go imitation and multi-oracle methods give "expert cost + policy-class error + regret" bounds; batch safe policy improvement gives high-probability bounds that need very large data; residual learning, jump-start, Lazy-MDPs, kickstarting and Q-gated action selection have no guarantee relative to the base policy at all.

### Cited Findings

#### Rollout on a base heuristic (Bertsekas)
- Definition (deterministic case): at partial trajectory y_k, for every feasible control u_k the base heuristic is run from the next state to completion; the control whose completed trajectory has the lowest cost is applied. The Q-factor of a candidate is the cost of that completed trajectory. D. P. Bertsekas, "Constrained Multiagent Rollout and Multidimensional Assignment with the Auction Algorithm", arXiv:2002.07407, 2020 — [Source](https://arxiv.org/abs/2002.07407)
- Sequential consistency (Def. 2.1): if the heuristic started at y_k generates the tail (x_k, u_k, …, x_N), then started at the next state on that tail it generates the rest of the same tail. Greedy heuristics and any feedback policy have this property. — [Source](https://arxiv.org/abs/2002.07407)
- Sequential improvement (Def. 2.2): the heuristic's completed cost from y_k is at least the minimum over u_k of the completed cost after taking u_k: G(y_k ∪ R(y_k)) ≥ min_u G(T_k(y_k, u)). Sequential consistency implies sequential improvement. — [Source](https://arxiv.org/abs/2002.07407)
- Cost improvement (Prop. 2.1): if the heuristic is sequentially improving and its trajectory from the start is feasible, the rollout's completed-trajectory costs are non-increasing along the rollout, so the final rollout trajectory is feasible and costs no more than the heuristic's. When the condition fails, rollout **can produce a worse solution than the heuristic**. — [Source](https://arxiv.org/abs/2002.07407)
- Fortified rollout (Sec. 3): keep a tentative best complete trajectory; at each stage consider only controls whose completed trajectory costs no more than the tentative best; if none exists, follow the tentative best trajectory's next control. **Even without sequential improvement the final trajectory is feasible and no worse than the base heuristic's trajectory**; with sequential improvement it coincides with plain rollout. Requires a deterministic problem and a feasible base trajectory. — [Source](https://arxiv.org/abs/2002.07407)
- The original 1997 paper already separates the two properties: a sequentially consistent heuristic is sequentially improving but not conversely, and "the algorithm using a sequentially improving heuristic is rollout improving"; it also allows the base heuristic to build several paths with different heuristics and keep the cheapest. D. P. Bertsekas, J. N. Tsitsiklis, C. Wu, "Rollout Algorithms for Combinatorial Optimization", Journal of Heuristics 3:245–262, 1997 **[search snippet; journal details from prior knowledge]** — [Source](https://www.mit.edu/~jnt/Papers/J066-97-rollout.pdf)
- Stochastic case: the rollout policy's cost-to-go is at most the base policy's from every state and stage (Eq. 1.3); policy iteration "can be viewed as repeated or perpetual rollout". D. P. Bertsekas, "Multiagent Rollout Algorithms and Reinforcement Learning", arXiv:1910.00120, 2019 (rev. 2020) — [Source](https://arxiv.org/abs/1910.00120)
- Rollout is "a single iteration of the fundamental DP method of policy iteration", and the rollout policy μ̃ is improved "in the sense that" J_μ̃(x) ≤ J_μ(x) for all x. Approximation in value space with one-step lookahead "amounts to a step of Newton's method for solving Bellman's equation", which gives a superlinear relation between the error of the off-line approximation and the performance error of the lookahead policy. D. P. Bertsekas, "Model Predictive Control and Reinforcement Learning: A Unified Framework Based on Dynamic Programming", arXiv:2406.00592, 2024 — [Source](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.00592)
- Loss of the guarantee under approximation: for multistep, truncated or otherwise approximate variants, strict cost improvement "need not hold"; it holds only "within error bounds" (the paper refers to Bertsekas 2019, Sec. 5.1, and does not print the bounds). — [Source](https://arxiv.org/abs/1910.00120)
- One-agent-at-a-time (multiagent) rollout keeps the cost improvement property (Prop. 2.1) while reducing the work per stage from O(s^m) to O(s·m) base-policy evaluations for m agents with s choices each. Caveats: if the base policy is already agent-by-agent optimal the one-at-a-time version cannot strictly improve it; and if agents decide without knowing their predecessors' choices the result can be **worse than the base policy** (Example 3.1: base cost 1 per stage, uncoordinated rollout 2, coordinated rollout 0). — [Source](https://arxiv.org/abs/1910.00120)
- Value-function-based rollout in dynamic vehicle routing: "base policies composed of deterministic decision rules lead to rollout policies with performance at least as strong as that of their base policy." M. W. Ulmer, J. C. Goodson, D. C. Mattfeld, M. Hennig, "Offline–Online Approximate Dynamic Programming for Dynamic Vehicle Routing with Stochastic Requests", Transportation Science 53(1):185–202, 2019, doi:10.1287/trsc.2017.0767 **[abstract / search snippet]** — [Source](https://pubsonline.informs.org/doi/10.1287/trsc.2017.0767)
- Tesauro and Galperin's Monte-Carlo policy improvement (the same one-step rollout with sampled Q-values) gives "no guarantee on how much improvement" is obtained and requires a simulator. G. Tesauro, G. R. Galperin, "On-line Policy Improvement using Monte-Carlo Search", NIPS 9, 1996, pp. 1068–1074 (arXiv:2501.05407) — [Source](https://arxiv.org/abs/2501.05407)

#### Several base policies: policy switching and parallel rollout
- Policy switching over a set Δ of base policies: π_ps(x) = φ_x(x) with φ_x ∈ argmax_{π∈Δ} V^π(x). Result attributed to Chang, Givan and Chong (2004): V^{π_ps(Δ)} ≥ V^φ for all φ ∈ Δ (restated as Theorem II.1). Setting: finite state and action sets, definitions in terms of exact value functions; with Monte-Carlo estimates the probability of picking a maximising policy "approaches one exponentially fast" in the number of sample paths. H. S. Chang, "On-line Policy Iteration with Policy Switching for Markov Decision Processes", arXiv:2112.02177, 2021, restating H. S. Chang, R. Givan, E. K. P. Chong, "Parallel rollout for online solution of partially observable Markov decision processes", Discrete Event Dynamic Systems 14:309–341, 2004 — [Source](https://arxiv.org/abs/2112.02177)
- Parallel rollout (one-step lookahead on the max of the base policies' values) has "a related result"; the exact formula and theorem were not printed on the page read. — [Source](https://arxiv.org/abs/2112.02177)

#### AlphaZero family and the Gumbel improvement operator
- "AlphaZero can fail to improve its policy network, if not visiting all actions at the root of a search tree." I. Danihelka, A. Guez, J. Schrittwieser, D. Silver, "Policy improvement by planning with Gumbel", ICLR 2022 (OpenReview bERaNdoegnO) — [Source](https://iclr.cc/virtual/2022/poster/6418)
- Counter-example in the paper: with q = (0, 0, 1) and prior π = (0.5, 0.3, 0.2), "pick the best of the top-2 most probable actions" scores 0 while the prior itself scores 0.2. — [Source](https://object.cloud.sdsc.edu/v1/AUTH_da4962d3368042ac8337e2dfdd3e7bf3/ml-papers-txt/ICLR/2022/policy_improvement_by_planning_with_gumbel__15d5fc26.txt)
- Guarantee: sample n actions without replacement with the Gumbel-Top-k trick (top n of g + logits), evaluate them, and return argmax_a [g(a) + logits(a) + σ(q(a))] with σ monotonically increasing and the same Gumbel noise g. Then E[q(A_{n+1})] ≥ E_{a∼π}[q(a)] (Eqs. 6–7, proof in Appendix B). **Assumption: the action values are evaluated correctly.** — [Source](https://object.cloud.sdsc.edu/v1/AUTH_da4962d3368042ac8337e2dfdd3e7bf3/ml-papers-txt/ICLR/2022/policy_improvement_by_planning_with_gumbel__15d5fc26.txt)
- Policy target: π′ = softmax(logits + σ(completedQ)), where unvisited actions receive the value estimate v_π = Σ_a π(a) q(a) (i.e. zero advantage); Appendix C proves this update is a policy improvement; the network is trained with KL(π′, π). — [Source](https://object.cloud.sdsc.edu/v1/AUTH_da4962d3368042ac8337e2dfdd3e7bf3/ml-papers-txt/ICLR/2022/policy_improvement_by_planning_with_gumbel__15d5fc26.txt)
- Expert Iteration has no formal convergence or performance guarantee; removing the expert-improvement step reduces it to DAgger, and using Monte-Carlo search as the expert recovers a form of policy iteration. T. Anthony, Z. Tian, D. Barber, "Thinking Fast and Slow with Deep Learning and Tree Search", arXiv:1705.08439, 2017 **[venue: prior knowledge, NeurIPS 2017]** — [Source](https://arxiv.org/abs/1705.08439)

#### Cost-to-go imitation (AggreVaTe family)
- AggreVaTe: roll in with the learner to a random time t, take an exploratory action, let the expert finish and record the expert's cost-to-go; train a cost-sensitive classifier on aggregated data. Theorem 2.1: J(π̂) ≤ J(π̄) ≤ J(π*) + T·[ε_class + ε_regret] + O(Q_max·T·log T/(αN)); in the limit J(π̄) ≤ J(π*) + T·ε_class, where π* is the expert, ε_class the best cost-sensitive classification regret in the policy class, ε_regret the online regret. This is an upper bound relative to the expert, **not** a proof of exceeding it. S. Ross, J. A. Bagnell, "Reinforcement and Imitation Learning via Interactive No-Regret Learning", arXiv:1406.5979, 2014 — [Source](https://arxiv.org/abs/1406.5979)
- Stated failure mode: AggreVaTe can fail when the expert is much better than anything in the policy class (it may favour a short but unsafe route); each cost-to-go sample may need a full trajectory, which the paper calls impractical in many settings. — [Source](https://arxiv.org/abs/1406.5979)
- AggreVaTeD (policy-gradient form using the expert's advantage Q* − V*): the guarantee holds for strongly convex losses with the expert in the policy class and "does not hold for non-linear function approximators". Separation result: on a binary-tree MDP any RL algorithm has regret Ω(√(SN)) while AggreVaTe with exact Q* has O(ln S). W. Sun, A. Venkatraman, G. J. Gordon, B. Boots, J. A. Bagnell, "Deeply AggreVaTeD: Differentiable Imitation Learning for Sequential Prediction", arXiv:1703.01030, 2017 **[venue: prior knowledge, ICML 2017]** — [Source](https://arxiv.org/abs/1703.01030)
- LOLS (learned roll-in, roll-out by the reference with probability β and by the learner otherwise). Theorem 3: β·(J(π̄) − J(π_ref)) + (1−β)·Σ_t (J(π̄) − min_{π∈Π} E_{s∼d^t_π̄}[Q^π̄(s, π)]) ≤ T·δ_N — simultaneous regret against the reference and against the learner's own one-step deviations (local optimality). Roll-in with the reference is "inconsistent" (Thm. 1); learned roll-in with pure reference roll-out is "not locally optimal" when the reference is sub-optimal (Thm. 2); learned roll-in with learned roll-out is plain RL. K.-W. Chang, A. Krishnamurthy, A. Agarwal, H. Daumé III, J. Langford, "Learning to Search Better than Your Teacher", arXiv:1502.02206, 2015 **[venue: prior knowledge, ICML 2015]** — [Source](https://arxiv.org/abs/1502.02206)
- THOR (reward shaping with an oracle value estimate V̂^e as potential, then optimise over a k-step truncated horizon). Thm. 3.1: with |V̂^e − V*| = ε there is an MDP where the one-step greedy policy is Ω(γε/(1−γ)) worse than optimal. Thm. 3.2: a policy minimising the k-step shaped objective at every state satisfies J(π̂) − J(π*) ≤ O(γ^k ε/(1−γ^k)). k = 1 recovers AggreVaTe(D), k = ∞ is pure RL. The summary I obtained states that for k > 1 the learner beats the oracle-induced greedy policy by at least (γ/(1−γ) − γ^k/(1−γ^k))·ε; I read this as a comparison of the two bounds on the worst-case instance, not a general theorem. W. Sun, J. A. Bagnell, B. Boots, "Truncated Horizon Policy Search: Combining Reinforcement Learning & Imitation Learning", arXiv:1805.11240, 2018 **[venue: prior knowledge, ICLR 2018]** — [Source](https://arxiv.org/abs/1805.11240)
- LOKI (K steps of online imitation by mirror descent, K random, then policy gradient). Thm. 1: E[J(π_K)] ≤ J(π*) + Δ with Δ = C_π*/(1−γ)·(ε_class^w + 2^{−d}σ̂D_R + G²C_{N_M}/(σ̂N_M)); Thm. 2 adds policy-gradient terms for the final policy. Needs online queries of the expert's action, **not** the expert's value function; assumes strongly convex per-round losses and bounded gradients. Exceeding the expert is "not guaranteed in general". C.-A. Cheng, X. Yan, N. Wagener, B. Boots, "Fast Policy Learning through Imitation and Reinforcement", arXiv:1805.10413, 2018 **[venue: prior knowledge, UAI 2018]** — [Source](https://arxiv.org/abs/1805.10413)

#### Trust-region and conservative updates
- Conservative policy iteration (Kakade and Langford 2002, as restated in the TRPO paper): mixture update π_new = (1−α)π_old + απ′ with bound η(π_new) ≥ L_{π_old}(π_new) − (2εγ/(1−γ)²)·α², ε = max_s |E_{a∼π′}[A_π(s,a)]|. — [Source](https://arxiv.org/abs/1502.05477)
- TRPO Theorem 1 for general stochastic policies: η(π_new) ≥ L_{π_old}(π_new) − (4εγ/(1−γ)²)·α² with α the maximum total-variation distance, ε = max_{s,a}|A_π(s,a)|; the penalised algorithm "is guaranteed to generate a monotonically improving sequence of policies". **Assumes exact advantage values.** The practical algorithm replaces the penalty by a hard constraint because the theoretical coefficient "leads to prohibitively small steps", uses average instead of maximum KL, and uses sampled estimates — so the guarantee does not carry to practical TRPO or PPO. J. Schulman, S. Levine, P. Moritz, M. Jordan, P. Abbeel, "Trust Region Policy Optimization", arXiv:1502.05477 **[venue: prior knowledge, ICML 2015]** — [Source](https://arxiv.org/abs/1502.05477)

#### Batch safe policy improvement
- SPIBB: state-action pairs seen fewer than N∧ times in the batch form a "bootstrapped" set on which the new policy copies the baseline's probabilities; elsewhere it optimises the estimated MDP. Theorem 2: with probability 1−δ the Π_b-SPIBB policy is a ζ-approximate safe improvement over the baseline, ζ = (4V_max/(1−γ))·√((2/N∧)·log(2|X||A|2^{|X|}/δ)) − ρ(π_SPIBB, M̂) + ρ(π_b, M̂). Assumes a finite MDP, a known baseline that generated the data, bounded rewards. The more effective Π_≤b variant and the deep SPIBB-DQN (pseudo-counts) have **no** guarantee. R. Laroche, P. Trichelair, R. Tachet des Combes, "Safe Policy Improvement with Baseline Bootstrapping", arXiv:1712.06924 **[venue: prior knowledge, ICML 2019]** — [Source](https://arxiv.org/abs/1712.06924)
- A later audit argues that Soft-SPIBB's claim of provable safety does not hold, proposes provably safe "Adv-Soft-SPIBB" variants and a heuristic "Lower-Approx-Soft-SPIBB" (best empirically among SPIBB variants on two benchmarks), and finds that the safety bounds of the provably safe methods become practically useful only with very large amounts of data. P. Scholl, F. Dietrich, C. Otte, S. Udluft, "Safe Policy Improvement Approaches and their Limitations", arXiv:2208.00724, 2022 **[abstract only]** — [Source](https://arxiv.org/abs/2208.00724)
- High-confidence policy improvement: a batch algorithm where the user picks a performance lower bound and a confidence level; the probability of returning a policy below the lower bound is at most the chosen level; an incremental version repeats the improvement step; tested on a 4×4 gridworld, mountain car and a digital-marketing data set. P. Thomas, G. Theocharous, M. Ghavamzadeh, "High Confidence Policy Improvement", ICML 2015, PMLR 37:2380–2388 **[abstract only]** — [Source](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v37/thomas15.html)
- The same line of work compares three safety tests (concentration inequality, Student's t-test, bootstrap confidence intervals); bootstrap lower bounds "may have error rates larger than δ" but are a practical alternative to guaranteed bounds that are too loose. **[search snippet from a follow-up paper]** — [Source](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2212.08302)

#### Methods with no guarantee relative to the base policy
- Residual policy learning: π_θ(s) = π(s) + f_θ(s), last layer of f initialised to zero so the initial policy equals the base policy; the property "never make a good initial policy worse" is an initialisation choice, not a theorem; no formal guarantees are stated. T. Silver, K. Allen, J. Tenenbaum, L. Kaelbling, "Residual Policy Learning", arXiv:1812.06298, 2018 — [Source](https://arxiv.org/abs/1812.06298)
- Jump-start RL: bounds are on sub-optimality relative to the optimal policy under a state-coverage assumption on the guide (Assumption 4.2, Theorem 4.3); there is no guarantee that the final policy is no worse than the guide. I. Uchendu et al., "Jump-Start Reinforcement Learning", arXiv:2204.02372 **[venue: prior knowledge, ICML 2023]** — [Source](https://arxiv.org/abs/2204.02372)
- Lazy-MDPs: optimality results are relative to the augmented (lazy) MDP; no formal guarantee that the lazy policy outperforms the default policy. A. Jacq, J. Ferret, O. Pietquin, M. Geist, "Lazy-MDPs: Towards Interpretable Reinforcement Learning by Learning When to Act", arXiv:2203.08542, 2022 **[venue: prior knowledge, AAMAS 2022]** — [Source](https://arxiv.org/abs/2203.08542)
- Imitation-bootstrapped RL (choose between the imitation policy's and the RL policy's proposed action by Q-value): no formal guarantee. H. Hu, S. Mirchandani, D. Sadigh, "Imitation Bootstrapped Reinforcement Learning", arXiv:2311.02198, 2023 **[venue: prior knowledge, RSS 2024]** — [Source](https://arxiv.org/abs/2311.02198)

### Inferences
- The guarantees fall into three strengths. (a) Exact, pointwise: rollout with exact Q-values of the base policy; fortified rollout in deterministic problems (no assumption on the heuristic); policy switching with exact values; the Gumbel operator with exact q. (b) Additive-error: AggreVaTe/AggreVaTeD/LOLS/LOKI/THOR/MAMBA — "expert cost + horizon × (policy-class error + online regret)"; these become vacuous if the network cannot represent the one-step-improved policy or the cost-to-go estimates are biased. (c) High-probability from finite batch data: SPIBB and high-confidence policy improvement — sound, but the bounds are loose and data-hungry.
- For a planner that is a deterministic function of the simulator state (a deterministic plan re-optimised in rolling fashion is a feedback policy), sequential consistency holds in a deterministic problem, so plain one-step rollout on it is guaranteed no worse; for the stochastic categories (dynamic requests, congested charging) the guarantee is in expectation and requires enough Monte-Carlo samples per candidate action. A planner with internal randomness or time limits (non-reproducible output) breaks sequential consistency; fortified rollout is then the safe form.
- A pure policy-gradient step (PPO) carries none of these guarantees in practice: the TRPO theorem needs exact advantages and a step size that the paper itself calls prohibitively small. A degradation after PPO fine-tuning of an imitation-initialised policy is therefore consistent with theory, not an anomaly.
- How each guaranteed mechanism maps onto a PPO actor-critic with a discrete, variable-size action set (my synthesis): (i) rollout and Gumbel act on whatever actions are available at the state, so variable action sets need no change — they produce either a decision-time override or an improved target distribution π′ over the available actions that can be added to the PPO loss as a KL/cross-entropy term; (ii) AggreVaTeD/THOR/MAMBA only change the baseline/advantage used in the policy-gradient estimator (base-policy value in place of, or alongside, the learner's critic), so they are drop-in changes to generalised advantage estimation; (iii) SPIBB-style constraints translate to "copy the base policy's action wherever the critic has little data", which needs a count or uncertainty proxy.

## 2. Which of them have been shown to exceed a strong sub-optimal expert in practice, and by how much?

### Takeaway
Decision-time rollout has the most consistent record of beating its base policy, including strong ones (error reduced 2.5 to 6.6 times in backgammon even with a strong neural base player; 1 to 4 % in stochastic vehicle routing). Learned policies that exceed their teacher are well documented, but almost always against deliberately weak teachers (early-stopped policies, hand controllers with 15 to 55 % success); against strong teachers the reported gains shrink or vanish.

### Cited Findings

#### Rollout and tree-search improvement
- Backgammon, full games against a fixed strong opponent (points per game): linear base players −0.52, −0.65, −0.32 become −0.01, −0.02, +0.04 with Monte-Carlo rollout. Per-move equity loss on 800 positions: random player 0.330 → 0.131 (ratio 2.5); three linear players 0.0400 → 0.0124 (3.2), 0.0665 → 0.0175 (3.8), 0.0291 → 0.00749 (3.9). Truncated rollouts on multi-layer network base players: 0.0152 → 0.00318 (ratio 4.8, 11-step) and 0.0120 → 0.00181 (ratio 6.6, 7-step; flagged by the authors as possibly biased). The abstract summarises "as much as a factor of 5 or more". The error-reduction ratio appeared to grow with base-player strength. — [Source](https://arxiv.org/abs/2501.05407)
- Vehicle routing with stochastic demands: the rollout policy obtains 1 % to 4 % average improvement over the a priori approach, depending on the quality of the initial solution. N. Secomandi, "A Rollout Policy for the Vehicle Routing Problem with Stochastic Demands", Operations Research 49(5):796–802, 2001 **[search snippet of abstract]** — [Source](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:inm:oropre:v:49:y:2001:i:5:p:796-802)
- Autonomous taxi routing and pickup (normalised wait time, 0 = oracle, 1 = worst policy; low/medium/high demand): greedy base policy 0.94/0.99/1.0; instantaneous assignment 1.0/0.92/0.88; one-agent-at-a-time rollout on the greedy base 0.62/0.65/0.58; graph network trained to imitate the rollout policy 0.58/0.68/0.65; one-step lookahead on top of that network ("online play") 0.57/0.62/0.50. D. Garces, S. Bhattacharya, S. Gil, D. Bertsekas, "Multiagent Reinforcement Learning for Autonomous Routing and Pickup Problem with Adaptation to Variable Demand", arXiv:2211.14983, 2022 **[venue: prior knowledge, ICRA 2023]** — [Source](https://arxiv.org/abs/2211.14983)
- Same paper, distribution shift: the imitation network alone degrades to 0.98–1.0 when demand moves away from the training distribution, while rollout stays near 0.60–0.62 and online play degrades less (0.68–0.73). — [Source](https://arxiv.org/abs/2211.14983)
- Expert Iteration (Hex 9×9): after DAgger-style rounds the apprentice network roughly matched its Monte-Carlo tree search expert (87 wins of 162); search guided by the network won 97 % of games against the plain search expert; the final system won 75.3 % against MoHex at equal iterations. 10,000 simulations per move. — [Source](https://arxiv.org/abs/1705.08439)
- Gumbel MuZero learned reliably with 2 simulations per move in 9×9 Go where MuZero failed to learn with 16 or fewer; on Atari MuZero failed at 4 or fewer simulations while Gumbel MuZero still learned at 2; large-scale runs matched or exceeded MuZero (19×19 Go) and AlphaZero (chess). No Elo values were printed in the text read. — [Source](https://object.cloud.sdsc.edu/v1/AUTH_da4962d3368042ac8337e2dfdd3e7bf3/ml-papers-txt/ICLR/2022/policy_improvement_by_planning_with_gumbel__15d5fc26.txt)

#### Cost-to-go imitation
- AggreVaTeD with natural gradient exceeded its expert by 25 % (CartPole), 5.8 % (Acrobot) and 5.4 % (Walker), reached 97 % of the expert on Hopper and 92 % on partially observed Acrobot (where plain RL made no progress); 97 % of the expert on dependency parsing. — [Source](https://arxiv.org/abs/1703.01030)
- LOLS, dependency parsing (unlabelled attachment score): 90.5 with an optimal reference, 90.2 with a sub-optimal reference, 89.4 with a bad reference, against 84.0 / 81.1 / 63.4 for Searn. Cost-sensitive multiclass with a bad reference: average cost 0.266 for LOLS against 1.670 for copying the reference with reference roll-outs and 0.282 for Searn. On part-of-speech tagging LOLS (94.13) did not beat Searn (94.88), which conflicts with the paper's text claim of outperforming on all tasks. — [Source](https://arxiv.org/abs/1502.02206)
- THOR: results are in figures only; k > 1 clearly beats AggreVaTeD (k = 1) on Acrobot; on continuous sparse-reward tasks good performance needed k of 20–30 % of the horizon and AggreVaTeD "learned very little"; the oracle value was fitted by temporal-difference learning from demonstrations and is called "just a coarse estimator". — [Source](https://arxiv.org/abs/1805.11240)
- LOKI: the expert was a TRPO policy stopped before convergence; DAgger reaches the expert quickly and does not exceed it; LOKI keeps improving after the switch and matches policy gradient started from the expert; THOR and a LOLS variant fell below TRPO on Hopper and the Walkers, attributed to inaccurate estimated expert value functions. No numerical tables. — [Source](https://arxiv.org/abs/1805.10413)

#### Distillation plus RL, residuals, gating
- Kickstarting (teacher cross-entropy added to the RL loss, weight decayed): a large student passed a small teacher by 43.4 % (56.9 against 39.7); with one expert teacher per task it reached the from-scratch score in about 9.6 times fewer frames and finished 42.2 % above from-scratch (73.8 against 51.9). **A constant distillation weight tied the student to the teacher's level** (40.0 at 1 billion frames, 41.2 at 10 billion); linear decay to zero at 1 billion frames gave 59.4, decay at 4 billion gave 53.1; pure distillation without the RL loss underperformed. S. Schmitt et al., "Kickstarting Deep Reinforcement Learning", arXiv:1803.03835, 2018 — [Source](https://arxiv.org/abs/1803.03835)
- Behaviour cloning then RL fine-tuning for object navigation: 65.0 % success (+5.0 % absolute over the previous best); **as the cloning data grows and cloning accuracy gets high, the gain from RL fine-tuning becomes smaller**; the released code uses a critic-only learning phase that gradually transitions to training actor and critic. R. Ramrakhya et al., "PIRLNav: Pretraining with Imitation and RL Finetuning for ObjectNav", CVPR 2023, arXiv:2301.07302 **[search snippet]** — [Source](https://arxiv.org/abs/2301.07302)
- Pretraining the critic as well as the actor before PPO (actor by behaviour cloning, critic on returns from rollouts of the cloned policy) improved sample efficiency by 86.1 % on average over no pretraining and 30.9 % over actor-only pretraining on 15 simulated robot tasks. A. Kernbach et al., "Actor-Critic Pretraining for Proximal Policy Optimization", arXiv:2602.23804, 2026 (preprint) **[abstract only]** — [Source](https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.23804)

Continuous actions only. — [Source](https://arxiv.org/abs/1812.06298)

- Lazy-MDPs on Atari with a random default: nearly 100 % of DQN's score on Gravitar while controlling under 10 % of states; about 80 % on Zaxxon with under 30 % control; over 100 % on PrivateEye with about 20 % control. - Teacher-student shared control (value-gated intervention): the student ends with higher return than an imperfect teacher on held-out driving scenarios for teachers of high, medium and low quality, whereas action-similarity gating stays bounded by medium and low teachers; numbers appear only in figures (4 seeds). Z. Xue, Z. Peng, Q. Li, Z. Liu, B. Zhou, "Guarded Policy Optimization with Imperfect Online Demonstrations", ICLR 2023, arXiv:2303.01728 — [Source](https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.01728)
- Jump-start RL (measured against other learners, not against the guide): antmaze-medium-play with 100k transitions 86.7 against 32.8 for the same offline-RL method without the guide curriculum; robot grasping with 20 demonstrations 0.54 against 0.29, and 0.91 against 0.00; an adversarial or poor guide can make learning slower than random exploration. — [Source](https://arxiv.org/abs/2204.02372)

#### Multiple oracles
- MAMBA: oracle returns CartPole 9–87, double inverted pendulum 2440–4244, HalfCheetah 1395–1726, Ant 776–1051; MAMBA with λ = 0.9 outperforms plain policy gradient with generalised advantage estimation and AggreVaTeD, also with a single oracle; no precise final numbers in the text. - MAPS beats MAMBA, PPO and the best oracle on three of four control tasks (not Walker-walk, attributed to high-quality oracles and a limited buffer). X. Liu, T. Yoneda, C. Wang, M. R. Walter, Y. ### Inferences
- The teachers that were clearly exceeded by a learned (search-free) policy were weak: early-stopped TRPO (LOKI), half-trained DQN (Lazy-MDPs), controllers at 15–55 % success (residual learning), checkpoints from different training stages (MAMBA/MAPS/RPI oracles). Where the teacher was strong — a high-accuracy cloned policy (PIRLNav), strong constituent policies (max-following on control tasks), a good a priori routing solution (Secomandi) — reported gains were small (1–4 %) or zero. For a planner within 0.4–3 % of best-known solutions, the realistic headroom from any of these methods on the planner's own objective is of that order, and the estimation noise of advantages must be smaller than that to capture it.
- Rollout is the exception: the backgammon ratios did not shrink as the base player became stronger. This suggests decision-time (or training-time) one-step improvement on the planner's own cost-to-go is the mechanism most likely to add value on top of a strong planner, with learning used to amortise it.
- The reported pattern "imitation gives a slightly worse copy; PPO helps one category and hurts another" matches three documented effects: (i) a constant imitation weight pins the student at teacher level (Kickstarting); (ii) starting actor updates with a mismatched or untrained critic causes an early drop (residual policy learning, imitation-bootstrapped RL, actor-critic pretraining); (iii) practical policy-gradient steps have no monotonic-improvement guarantee (TRPO paper). The gains on drayage at the cost of other metrics indicate that RL optimised a different trade-off than the planner; none of the surveyed methods addresses multi-metric non-inferiority except high-confidence tests applied per metric.

### Gaps
- No paper was found that reports a learned policy (without decision-time search) exceeding a near-optimal operations-research planner by a verified margin in dynamic fleet routing; the routing evidence is for rollout or for weak base policies.
- Numerical results for THOR, LOKI, MAMBA, MAPS, RPI and the teacher-student shared control method are only in figures; no exact margins over the oracle can be quoted.
- The Lazy-MDP statement about "no asymptotic gain" with a pretrained default was returned by an automated summary and is ambiguous (over the default, or over DQN); it should be checked in the paper before being quoted.

## 3. How is a "follow the base policy unless the estimated advantage of deviating exceeds a margin" rule formalised and trained in the literature, and how is the margin calibrated?

### Takeaway
The rule appears in five forms: a default ("lazy") action with a per-deviation penalty η; a value-gated switch between two policies with threshold ε, which carries the bound J(mixture) ≥ J(base) − (1−β)ε/(1−γ); an advantage-based override with threshold η relative to a backup policy's Q-function; an advantage or Q filter on the imitation loss; and a statistical test before deviating (fortified rollout, confidence-interval pruning, high-confidence tests). Margins are calibrated by sweeping between analytically derived bounds, by targeting an intervention rate (for example below 5 %), by a confidence level δ, or by a count threshold; no paper derives the margin from the base policy's estimated optimality gap.

### Cited Findings

#### Default action with a deviation penalty
- A lazy-MDP is (M, ā, π̄, η): the action set is A ∪ {ā}; choosing the lazy action ā defers to the default policy π̄ (reward and transition are those under π̄); choosing any other action costs an extra −η. The result is an ordinary MDP, so standard RL applies unchanged (the paper uses DQN). — [Source](https://arxiv.org/abs/2203.08542)
- Lazy-gap G_Q(s) = max_a Q(s,a) − E_{a∼π̄}[Q(s,a)]; the greedy rule (Eq. 19) takes the lazy action when G_Q(s) ≤ η and otherwise the best non-lazy action. The modified Bellman operator is a γ-contraction (Thm. 2). — [Source](https://arxiv.org/abs/2203.08542)
- Calibration: η_max = max_s G_{Q^π̄}(s) — above it the agent always defers (needs the default policy's action values); η_min is a minimax expression in Q* (Thm. 4) — below it the agent never defers. In experiments η was swept (0.005 to 0.2 with a random default; 0.05 with a pretrained default) and the best value per game selected; automated selection is left to future work. Too high η: never acts; too low: always acts; penalising control can also discourage exploration. Discrete actions only. — [Source](https://arxiv.org/abs/2203.08542)

#### Value-gated switch between teacher and student
- Theoretical rule (Eq. 5): the teacher takes over when V^{π_t}(s) − E_{a∼π_s}[Q^{π_t}(s,a)] > ε, i.e. when the student's action is worse than the teacher's by more than ε **under the teacher's own value function**. Practical rule (Eq. 9): an ensemble of 10 Q-networks trained on the teacher; intervene if the ensemble-mean gap exceeds ε₁ or the ensemble variance of the student's action value exceeds ε₂. — [Source](https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.01728)
- Bound (Thm. 3.4): J(π_b) ≥ J(π_t) − (1−β)·ε/(1−γ) for the behaviour (mixed) policy π_b, where β is the intervention rate; the action-similarity rule gives a looser O(1/(1−γ)²) bound (Thm. 3.3). Only a lower bound is proved; exceeding the teacher is empirical. The appendix version of the theorem drops the (1−β) factor (an inconsistency in the paper). — [Source](https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.01728)
- Calibration: ε₁ = 1.2 and ε₂ = 2.5 were chosen so that the average intervention rate stays below 5 %; the ablation says the threshold values have little effect, while the ensemble and the intervention cost matter more and the action-similarity variant performs poorly. Requires the environment reward to fit the teacher's value function. — [Source](https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.01728)

#### Advantage-based override by a backup policy
- Rule (Q̄, μ, η): with Ā(s,a) = Q̄(s,a) − Q̄(s,μ) for a cost-type value Q̄ of the backup policy μ, the backup overrides the learner's sampled action whenever Ā(s,a) > η. If the rule is σ-admissible, the shielded policy's unsafe cost is at most Q̄(d₀, μ) + min{σ+η, 2γ}/(1−γ); the learner's return is within (2/(1−γ))·P(π* would be intervened) + ε of any comparator π*. N. Wagener, B. Boots, C.-A. Cheng, "Safe Reinforcement Learning Using Advantage-Based Intervention", arXiv:2106.09110 **[venue: prior knowledge, ICML 2021]** — [Source](https://arxiv.org/abs/2106.09110)
- Training: a surrogate MDP with an absorbing state; an intervened state-action pair transitions there with reward −1; trajectories are truncated at the first intervention; the learner is trained with an unconstrained algorithm (**PPO in the experiments**). Q̄ can come from rollouts of the backup policy, value iteration, short-horizon model-predictive control, a pointwise minimum of several rules, or a fitted network with error δ (costing (1+γ)δ in admissibility). η = 0 gives the cleanest bound; larger η intervenes less and loosens the bound. Stated limitation: intervention limits exploration. — [Source](https://arxiv.org/abs/2106.09110)

#### Deviate within a tolerance of the best action
- Shared autonomy: execute the action closest to the pilot's suggestion among actions with Q′(s,a) ≥ (1−α)·Q′(s,a*), where Q′ subtracts the minimum Q-value. α was set by sweep and user study (0.6 for one task, 1 for another); in simulation α ≈ 0.5 was best for noisy and laggy pilots and α = 0 for a pilot with missing sensing. S. Reddy, A. D. Dragan, S. Levine, "Shared Autonomy via Deep Reinforcement Learning", arXiv:1802.01744 **[venue: prior knowledge, RSS 2018]** — [Source](https://arxiv.org/abs/1802.01744)
- Imitation-bootstrapped RL: at each step both the frozen imitation policy and the RL policy propose an action and the one with the higher target-Q is executed; the same maximum is used in the bootstrap target; a soft variant samples in proportion to exp(βQ). — [Source](https://arxiv.org/abs/2311.02198)
- Policy expansion keeps the offline-trained policy as one member of a policy set, adds a new learnable policy, and composes them adaptively during interaction, so that early online updates do not destroy the offline behaviour. H. Zhang, W. Xu, H. Yu, "Policy Expansion for Bridging Offline-to-Online Reinforcement Learning", ICLR 2023, arXiv:2302.00935 **[abstract only; the selection rule was not on the page read]** — [Source](https://arxiv.org/abs/2302.00935)

#### Advantage or Q filters on the imitation loss
- Q-filter: L_BC = Σ_i ‖π(s_i) − a_i‖²·1[Q(s_i, a_i) > Q(s_i, π(s_i))] — the cloning loss is applied only where the critic rates the demonstrator's action above the policy's own, to account for sub-optimal demonstrations. The filter accelerated learning but gave "mixed results on the longer horizon tasks". A. Nair, B. McGrew, M. Andrychowicz, W. Zaremba, P. Abbeel, "Overcoming Exploration in Reinforcement Learning with Demonstrations", arXiv:1709.10089 **[venue: prior knowledge, ICRA 2018]** — [Source](https://arxiv.org/abs/1709.10089)
- Critic-regularised regression filters the imitation term by f = 1[A(s,a) > 0] or exp(A(s,a)/λ), with the advantage estimated against the mean or the maximum of Q over sampled policy actions. Z. Wang et al., "Critic Regularized Regression", NeurIPS 2020, arXiv:2006.15134 **[search snippet]** — [Source](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2006.15134)

#### Count-based and statistical gates
- SPIBB's gate is on data support, not on advantage: copy the baseline wherever the state-action count is below N∧; larger N∧ is safer and more conservative; the guarantee degrades as 1/√N∧. — [Source](https://arxiv.org/abs/1712.06924)
- High-confidence policy improvement gates on a statistical test: a candidate replaces the current policy only if a lower confidence bound on its performance exceeds the user's threshold at confidence level δ; "no hyper-parameter that needs expert tuning" beyond the bound and the level. — [Source](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v37/thomas15.html)
- Fortified rollout is the deterministic analogue: deviate from the tentative best (initially the base heuristic's) trajectory only when a completed alternative costs no more. — [Source](https://arxiv.org/abs/2002.07407)
- Monte-Carlo rollout with statistical pruning: candidates are dropped once they are unlikely to be best at a user-specified confidence level, or once their value is close enough to the current best that the choice would not matter; this cuts the need from hundreds of thousands of trials per move to several tens of thousands. — [Source](https://arxiv.org/abs/2501.05407)
- Conservative policy iteration is a soft version of the rule: mix a fraction α of the greedy policy into the current one; the improvement bound L − (2εγ/(1−γ)²)α² is positive only for small α. — [Source](https://arxiv.org/abs/1502.05477)

#### Learning to defer / switch in sequential settings
- Switching control between a machine and a human in a finite-horizon MDP: with a known human policy the optimal switching policy follows from backward induction; with an unknown one, upper confidence bounds give regret sub-linear in the number of learning steps. V. Balazadeh Meresht, A. De, A. Singla, M. Gomez-Rodriguez, "Learning to Switch Among Agents in a Team via 2-Layer Markov Decision Processes", arXiv:2002.04258 **[search snippet]** — [Source](https://arxiv.org/abs/2002.04258)
- "Reinforcement Learning Under Algorithmic Triage" (Straitouri et al., arXiv:2109.11328) frames deferral through options with a two-stage (offline, then on-policy) method; sequential learning-to-defer (Joshi, Parbhoo, Doshi-Velez, arXiv:2109.06312) defers when the model-based estimate of long-term benefit is uncertain. **[search snippets]** — [Source](https://arxiv.org/abs/2109.11328); [Source](https://arxiv.org/abs/2109.06312)
- Post-posed shielding monitors the learner's action and corrects it "if and only if the chosen action is unsafe", from a shield synthesised from a safety specification and an abstraction of the environment; it is designed for minimum interference; a later paper notes the correction ignores which safe action is better for the task. M. Alshiekh et al., "Safe Reinforcement Learning via Shielding", AAAI 2018, arXiv:1708.08611 **[search snippet]** — [Source](https://arxiv.org/abs/1708.08611)
- Budget-aware interactive imitation hands control to a supervisor in states that are novel or risky, under a user-specified intervention budget. R. Hoque et al., "ThriftyDAgger: Budget-Aware Novelty and Risk Gating for Interactive Imitation Learning", CoRL 2021, arXiv:2109.08273 **[abstract only]** — [Source](https://arxiv.org/abs/2109.08273)

### Inferences
- The cleanest formalisation for "no worse than the planner" combines two cited results. The performance-difference identity used by MAMBA (Lemma 1: V^π(d₀) − f(d₀) = T·E_{s∼d^π}[A^f(s,π)]; Corollary 1: non-negative advantage with respect to f at every state implies V^π ≥ f) with f = V^planner says a policy that only ever deviates to actions with non-negative advantage under the planner's Q-function is no worse than the planner. The teacher-student bound says that gating with a threshold ε on that advantage loses at most (1−β)ε/(1−γ). So: fit Q^planner (or V^planner plus one-step simulation), deviate only when Â^planner(s,a) > m, and choose m at least as large as the advantage-estimation error; the guarantee then holds up to that estimation error. This is a synthesis; no single paper states it for an operations-research planner.
- Two training styles exist and both fit a variable-size discrete action set. (1) Make "follow the plan" an explicit extra action (Lazy-MDP) with a deviation penalty η; for a graph policy this is one more scored candidate, and PPO trains it unchanged. η plays the role of the margin and can be annealed from above η_max (pure planner) downward, stopping when validation performance stops improving. (2) Keep a separate gate at decision time (value-gated switch, Q-argmax between proposals); the learned policy is trained freely but its action is executed only when the gate passes.
- Margin calibration options that the literature supports: a target deviation rate (teacher-student shared control used < 5 % intervention); the analytic range [η_min, η_max] from the base policy's action values (Lazy-MDP); a confidence level on a paired comparison (high-confidence policy improvement; Monte-Carlo pruning). With a simulator that can replay the same random events, a paired test of "policy with gate at margin m" against the planner on common scenarios gives a direct non-inferiority check per problem category; this is my suggestion, consistent with the high-confidence approach.
- A value-gated rule is only as good as Q^planner. Documented symptoms of a poor critic: falsely high values for learner actions early on (imitation-bootstrapped RL), early performance drops (residual policy learning), and failures of THOR/LOLS variants attributed to inaccurate expert value estimates (LOKI paper). An ensemble-variance term in the gate (as in teacher-student shared control and RPI) is the standard mitigation.

### Gaps
- No paper was found that calibrates the deviation margin from the base policy's known optimality gap or from a statistical test on paired simulations in a routing/dispatching setting.
- The Lazy-MDP paper's Corollary 1 was reported by the automated summary as having the opposite inequality to the greedy rule in Eq. 19; I could not resolve whether this is an error in the paper or in the summary. The rule quoted above (defer when the lazy-gap is at most η) is the one consistent with the definition.
- Details of the policy-expansion selection rule, ThriftyDAgger's threshold calibration, and learning-to-defer in the classification setting (Madras et al. 2018; Mozannar and Sontag 2020) were not read in the primary sources.

## 4. With several imperfect planners available, what do multi-oracle methods guarantee and how are they implemented with policy-gradient training?

### Takeaway
All multi-oracle methods target the state-wise maximum of the oracles' value functions, f^max(s) = max_k V^k(s). With exact values, following the best oracle per state is no worse than every oracle, and the one-step improvement on f^max is no worse than f^max; the learned versions (MAMBA, MAPS, RPI) guarantee this up to policy-class error, gradient bias/variance and regret, and are implemented as generalised advantage estimation with f^max in place of the learner's own critic inside a PPO-style update. The known weakness is sensitivity to value-estimation error and ties.

### Cited Findings
- Definitions: f^max(s) = max_k V^k(s); the max-following policy π• executes, in each state, the oracle with the highest value; MAMBA's benchmark π^max is greedy on the advantage A^max(s,a) = r(s,a) + E[f^max(s′)] − f^max(s) and does not need the oracles' actions. — [Source](https://arxiv.org/abs/2007.00795)
- Lemma 1 (any baseline f with zero terminal value): V^π(d₀) − f(d₀) = T·E_{s∼d^π}[A^f(s,π)]. Corollary 1: if f is "improvable" by π (A^f(s,π) ≥ 0 for all s), then V^π ≥ f everywhere. Proposition 1: f^max is improvable by π•, and A^max(s, π^max) ≥ A^max(s, π•) ≥ 0. — [Source](https://arxiv.org/abs/2007.00795)
- Why simpler combinations fail: fixed mixtures are poor when one oracle is bad; multiplying action probabilities fails for two deterministic oracles; picking the single best oracle misses gains from switching (example: two oracles each worth 1/2, switching achieves 1); π• collapses to the best oracle when one oracle dominates everywhere. — [Source](https://arxiv.org/abs/2007.00795)
- MAMBA algorithm: each iteration samples a switching time and an oracle uniformly; the learner rolls in to the switching time and the oracle finishes the episode; these data update Monte-Carlo estimates V̂^k. A separate full learner rollout gives the gradient −T·E[∇log π·Â_λ] with a λ-weighted advantage built on f̂^max = max_k V̂^k. λ = 0 is AggreVaTeD-style one-step improvement, λ = 1 is the ordinary policy gradient. Only mid-episode hand-over to an oracle is needed (no reset to arbitrary states); rewards are needed, oracle actions are not. — [Source](https://arxiv.org/abs/2007.00795)
- MAMBA guarantee (Theorem 1): with an online learner of regret O(βN + √(νN)) (β, ν the gradient bias and variance), E[max_n V^{π_n}(d₀)] ≥ E_{s∼d₀}[max_k V^k(s)] + E[Δ_N − ε_N(Π)] − O(β + √ν·N^{−1/2}), with Δ_N ≥ 0 the improvement of π^max over the baseline and ε_N = 0 when π^max is in the policy class. Stated limitations: the gradient is not an estimate of the true policy gradient unless f̂^max equals the learner's value; value estimates worsen with more oracles under a fixed budget; λ must be tuned (λ = 0.5 performed worst in one study, 0.9 best). — [Source](https://arxiv.org/abs/2007.00795)
- MAPS: instead of a uniformly random oracle, roll out the oracle with the highest upper confidence bound of value at the switching state; MAPS-SE additionally hands over only when the uncertainty of the best oracle's value at the current state reaches a threshold. Sample budget to identify the best oracle at a state: about O(K + Σ_i (H²/Δ_i²)·log(K/δ)) for MAPS against O(Σ_i K·H²/Δ_i²·log(K/δ)) for uniform selection. Implementation: per-oracle ensembles of value networks (mean = estimate, spread = bonus), **PPO-style updates**, 19,200-sample buffer per oracle, 2,048 learner samples per update. Limitation: the MAPS-SE threshold needs non-trivial tuning. — [Source](https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.10259)
- RPI: adds the learner to the oracle set, Π^E = Π^o ∪ {π_n}, with baseline f⁺(s) = max over the extended set. Robust active policy selection compares upper confidence bounds of the oracles' values with the lower confidence bound of the learner's; robust policy gradient uses generalised advantage estimation against f̂⁺ (λ = 0.9), falling back to the learner's own value when the top oracle's uncertainty exceeds a threshold Γ_s = 0.5 — so the update is imitation-like where an oracle is confidently better and ordinary actor-critic where the learner is. Proposition 5.1 gives the MAMBA-type bound against f⁺, which improves as the learner improves. Experiments use five value networks per policy and three oracles per task; RPI "scores the best in all benchmarks"; the confidence-bound version beats the mean-only version by 40 % overall in an ablation; imitation-type methods do better with sparse rewards, PPO does better late in dense-reward training. X. Liu, T. Yoneda, R. L. Stevens, M. R. Walter, Y. Chen, "Blending Imitation and Reinforcement Learning for Robust Policy Improvement", arXiv:2310.01737 **[venue: prior knowledge, ICLR 2024]** — [Source](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.01737)
- MaxIteration (no policy gradient): builds the policy one time step at a time; at step h it samples states by running the policy built so far and calls a regression oracle for each constituent's value there. Theorem 3.1: the output competes, up to O(ε), with the **worst approximate max-following policy** (any policy that at each state follows a constituent whose value is within β of the maximum), using O(HK) regression-oracle calls; Lemma 4.1/Corollary 4.2: this is within O(ε) of the best constituent. — [Source](https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.16739)
- Negative examples from the same paper: max-following can be far from optimal (optimal return H−2 against ε); tie-breaking can change the return from H−2 to 0 (Obs. 4.5); greedy use of approximate values can return 0 where true max-following returns about H−2 (Obs. 4.6); "max-value ensembling is sensitive to slight differences in the values between constituent policies"; the max-following value function can leave the function class of the constituents' value functions (Obs. 4.7). — [Source](https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.16739)
- Exact-value counterpart: policy switching, V^{π_ps(Δ)} ≥ V^φ for every φ in the set. — [Source](https://arxiv.org/abs/2112.02177)
- Multi-teacher distillation without values: one expert teacher per task with a decayed cross-entropy term; separate per-teacher weights gave only a negligible boost over a shared weight. — [Source](https://arxiv.org/abs/1803.03835)

### Inferences
- When a different planner is best on different problem categories, the first-order gain is instance-level or state-level selection, which already has an exact-value guarantee (policy switching) and needs only value estimates V̂^k per planner. These can be fitted by regression on Monte-Carlo returns of planner-only episodes, which exist as a by-product of generating imitation data. The MaxIteration results warn that this selector must use a tolerance (treat planners within β of the best as ties, break ties consistently) because small value errors can flip choices.
- The MAMBA/RPI recipe is a small change to a PPO pipeline: (1) keep one value head (or small ensemble) per planner trained on "learner rolls in, planner finishes" episodes; (2) compute advantages with f̂^max (MAMBA) or f̂⁺ including the learner's critic (RPI) as the bootstrap/baseline; (3) keep the PPO clipped update. No change is needed for variable-size action sets because only the advantage target changes. The mid-episode hand-over is natural in an event-driven simulator (swap the controller at a decision event).
- RPI's design directly addresses the observed failure "RL fine-tuning falls below the planner": where the learner's value is not confidently above the best planner's, the baseline stays the planner's and the gradient pushes toward one-step improvement on the planner rather than toward the learner's own (possibly wrong) critic.
- Cost: each oracle-value sample costs roughly one episode (roll-in plus oracle roll-out); with K planners and uniform selection the value estimates dilute (MAMBA's stated limitation), which is why active selection (MAPS) matters when simulation is expensive.

### Gaps
- No application of MAMBA, MAPS, RPI or MaxIteration to routing, dispatching or scheduling was found; all experiments are on continuous-control benchmarks with learned (checkpoint) oracles, typically weak ones.
- An independent replication concern: the max-following paper reports failing to obtain non-trivial return from MAPS after tuning — [Source](https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.16739); no independent replication of RPI was found.
- Bertsekas's statement for rollout with multiple heuristics (running all heuristics from each next state and using the best cost) and the condition under which it is sequentially improving was not verified in a primary text.

## 5. What are the compute requirements of rollout-style improvement when simulation is expensive, and what truncation or value-function substitutes are used?

### Takeaway
Exact one-step rollout costs (number of candidate actions) × (remaining decisions) × (Monte-Carlo samples) base-policy decisions per real decision — on the order of a million base decisions per move in the classic study. The standard reductions are: truncating the rollout after m steps and adding a learned terminal value, certainty equivalence after the first step, one-agent-at-a-time decomposition, statistical pruning of candidates, evaluating only a few sampled candidates (Gumbel with sequential halving, which learns with 2 simulations), and amortising rollout into a network trained on rollout labels. Truncation and approximation void the strict guarantee (it holds only within error bounds).

### Cited Findings
- Cost of untruncated Monte-Carlo rollout: about 10,000 or more trials per candidate without pruning (hundreds of thousands per move); several tens of thousands with pruning; with 25–30 base decisions per trial, about a million base decisions per rollout decision; real-time play at about 10 s per move needed roughly 100,000 base decisions per second on 16–32 parallel nodes at about 90 % parallel efficiency. — [Source](https://arxiv.org/abs/2501.05407)
- Truncated rollouts (stop after 7 or 11 steps and use the network's value estimate) cut computing time by at least an order of magnitude because trials are shorter and have lower variance, with slightly worse decisions attributed to bias in the value estimate (9 to 65 s per move in the reported configurations). — [Source](https://arxiv.org/abs/2501.05407)
- m-step truncated rollout = m value-iteration steps under the base policy starting from the terminal cost approximation, followed by the one-step lookahead (Newton) step; "lookahead by truncated rollout may be an economic substitute for lookahead by minimization"; increasing m is increasingly beneficial for stability; failures typically arise when the terminal cost approximation lies outside the region of convergence of the Newton step. — [Source](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.00592)
- Certainty equivalence applied only to steps after the first lookahead stage (first disturbance kept stochastic) "maintains the Newton step character" of the scheme. — [Source](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.00592)
- Strict cost improvement need not hold for multistep, truncated or approximate variants (only "within error bounds"). — [Source](https://arxiv.org/abs/1910.00120)
- One-agent-at-a-time rollout: O(s·m) instead of O(s^m) Q-factor evaluations per stage for m agents with s choices each, with cost improvement preserved. — [Source](https://arxiv.org/abs/1910.00120)
- A fleet-routing implementation: base policy applied for 10 steps then a terminal cost (number of outstanding requests); 5,000 simulated trajectories per leaf in the main setup, 2,000 with certainty equivalence in the scalable variant (15 vehicles, 825-node map, control space about 10¹²); rollout labels (1.2×10⁶ state–action pairs) train graph networks (about 16 h and 6 h on one GPU); per-decision runtime is not reported. — [Source](https://arxiv.org/abs/2211.14983)
- Few-candidate improvement: Gumbel-Top-k selects n root actions without replacement and sequential halving divides the simulation budget over log₂(m) phases, discarding half of the remaining candidates each phase; chosen because it optimises simple regret for a known budget. Training with n = 2, 4, 16, 32, 200 simulations was studied; reported training-step speed-ups of 5.9× (n = 32) and 24.3× (n = 4) relative to n = 200. Unvisited actions are completed with the value network's estimate, so the policy target uses all actions. The value scale σ(q̂) = (c_visit + max_b N(b))·c_scale·q̂ used c_visit = 50, c_scale = 1.0 (board games) and 0.1 with normalised Q (Atari); a larger scale made the search ignore the policy logits and performed worse on one game. — [Source](https://object.cloud.sdsc.edu/v1/AUTH_da4962d3368042ac8337e2dfdd3e7bf3/ml-papers-txt/ICLR/2022/policy_improvement_by_planning_with_gumbel__15d5fc26.txt)
- Sampling cost of cost-to-go imitation: AggreVaTe needs, per training sample, a roll-in, one exploratory action and an expert roll-out to the horizon ("each cost-to-go estimate may require executing a full trajectory"); LOLS evaluates every action at every decision point with a roll-out and therefore needs resets to arbitrary states. — [Source](https://arxiv.org/abs/1406.5979); [Source](https://arxiv.org/abs/1502.02206)
- Value-function substitutes for full roll-outs: THOR uses a temporal-difference-fitted oracle value as a shaping potential with a k-step horizon (k as the single tuned parameter; k of 20–30 % of the horizon was needed on long tasks). — [Source](https://arxiv.org/abs/1805.11240)
- Classification-based policy iteration with rollouts: Direct Policy Iteration has a finite-sample bound depending on the number of improvement steps, rollouts per iteration and policy-class capacity (Lazaric, Ghavamzadeh, Munos, ICML 2010); DPI-Critic combines truncated rollout returns with a critic's prediction (Gabillon, Lazaric, Ghavamzadeh, Scherrer, ICML 2011); classification-based modified policy iteration uses rollouts of length m with an explicit policy and value representation and recovers Direct Policy Iteration as m → ∞ (Scherrer, Ghavamzadeh, Gabillon, Geist, arXiv:1205.3054, ICML 2012). The rollout length sets a bias-variance trade-off. **[search snippets]** — [Source](https://icml.cc/imls/conferences/2010/papers/303.pdf); [Source](https://icml.cc/imls/conferences/2011/papers/541_icmlpaper.pdf); [Source](https://arxiv.org/abs/1205.3054)
- Pre- and post-decision-state rollout variants were formalised "as a means of overcoming computational limitations imposed by large state and action spaces", with analytical results relating the variants' performance. J. C. Goodson, B. W. Thomas, J. W. Ohlmann, "A rollout algorithm framework for heuristic solutions to finite-horizon stochastic dynamic programs", European Journal of Operational Research 258(1):216–229, 2017, doi:10.1016/j.ejor.2016.09.040 **[abstract only]** — [Source](https://iro.uiowa.edu/esploro/outputs/journalArticle/A-rollout-algorithm-framework-for-heuristic/9984380376102771)

### Inferences
- Arithmetic for the stated simulator cost (0.05–2 s per decision, 100–1,400 decisions per episode; my calculation, not from a source): one episode costs 5 s to about 47 min. A full planner roll-out from a mid-episode state costs half of that on average. Exact one-step rollout at every decision with, say, 10 candidate actions, 500 decisions and 0.5 s per decision is 10 × 250 × 0.5 s ≈ 21 min per decision and about 7 days per episode for a single sample per action — not usable at decision time and too expensive as a per-step label generator.
- What remains affordable: (a) one deviation per episode (AggreVaTe/MAMBA style: roll in, deviate once, planner finishes) costs about one extra episode per labelled state; (b) truncated rollout with a fitted planner value: (candidates) × m × (seconds per decision), e.g. 4 candidates × 10 steps × 0.5 s = 20 s per evaluated state — feasible on a subsample of states during training, marginal at every decision; (c) Gumbel-style selection of 2–4 candidates by the policy's own logits, evaluated by a critic alone (no simulation) or by a short truncated rollout; (d) for deterministic categories, a single paired comparison per instance (run planner and learned policy, keep the better complete solution), which is the episode-level form of fortified rollout and costs two episodes.
- Common random numbers (same request and congestion realisations for all candidates) and Q-factor differences reduce the number of samples needed for stochastic categories; statistical pruning stops sampling candidates that cannot win. Only the pruning result is verified here (Tesauro and Galperin); the variance-reduction advice is standard but not re-verified.
- Because truncation voids the strict guarantee, the practical safeguard is the margin gate of Question 3 on top of the truncated estimate: deviate only when the estimated gain exceeds the estimate's error.

### Gaps
- No source gives per-decision wall-clock budgets for rollout in fleet routing with simulators as slow as 0.05–2 s per decision; the Garces et al. paper does not report per-decision runtime.
- Error bounds for truncated rollout (Bertsekas 2019, Sec. 5.1) were not read.
- Finite-sample bounds of the classification-based policy iteration papers and their Tetris results were not verified (search results only confirmed the algorithms and the truncation trade-off).

## 6. Which of these have been applied to routing, dispatching, scheduling or other combinatorial sequential decision problems?

### Takeaway
Rollout on a heuristic or planner is the method with a long, direct record in stochastic and dynamic vehicle routing — including an electric-vehicle routing problem with uncertain public charging-station availability — and it has been distilled into graph networks for fleet dispatch. Gumbel AlphaZero and best-of-samples self-imitation have been applied to routing and job-shop construction problems. The cost-to-go imitation, multi-oracle, safe-policy-improvement, lazy-MDP and jump-start families have, as far as this search found, not been applied to routing or dispatching.

### Cited Findings
- Electric vehicle routing with public charging stations whose availability is uncertain (queues): a decomposition yields the optimal static policy and **fixed-route-based rollout policies that respond dynamically to observed queues**; the decomposition also gives dual bounds (information relaxation) that measure policy quality; the policies "significantly outperform" the industry-standard strategy of recharging at a central depot. N. D. Kullman, J. C. Goodson, J. E. Mendoza, "Electric Vehicle Routing with Public Charging Stations", Transportation Science 55(3):637–659, 2021, doi:10.1287/trsc.2020.1018 **[abstract only; no gap figures obtained]** — [Source](https://ideas.repec.org/a/inm/ortrsc/v55y2021i3p637-659.html)
- Vehicle routing with stochastic demands: one-step rollout on an a priori solution, 1–4 % average improvement. — [Source](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:inm:oropre:v:49:y:2001:i:5:p:796-802)
- Multi-vehicle routing with stochastic demand and duration limits: one-step rollout plus pre-decision and post-decision rollout variants (Goodson, Ohlmann, Thomas, Operations Research 61(1):138–154, 2013) **[search snippet]**; general framework applied to a dynamic stochastic multi-compartment knapsack problem (2017). — [Source](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:inm:oropre:v:61:y:2013:i:1:p:138-154); [Source](https://iro.uiowa.edu/esploro/outputs/journalArticle/A-rollout-algorithm-framework-for-heuristic/9984380376102771)
- Dynamic vehicle routing with stochastic requests: offline value-function approximation combined with online rollout, with the improvement guarantee for base policies made of deterministic decision rules. — [Source](https://pubsonline.informs.org/doi/10.1287/trsc.2017.0767)
- Fleet routing and pickup with stochastic requests: one-agent-at-a-time truncated rollout on a greedy (or instantaneous-assignment) base policy, graph-network imitation of the rollout policy, one-step lookahead on the network at run time, and switching between networks trained for different demand models when the current demand leaves a validity radius (Wasserstein distance); about 9 % relative improvement from switching in one evaluated shift. Stated limits: network input grows with map size; a network is needed per demand model. — [Source](https://arxiv.org/abs/2211.14983)
- Constrained multiagent rollout was formulated for multi-vehicle routing, facility location and multidimensional assignment (no numerical experiments in the paper; operation counts only). — [Source](https://arxiv.org/abs/2002.07407)
- The original rollout papers target combinatorial optimisation and stochastic scheduling (Bertsekas, Tsitsiklis, Wu 1997; Bertsekas and Castañon 1999, cited as the original rollout algorithm in a survey of online planning). — [Source](https://www.mit.edu/~jnt/Papers/J066-97-rollout.pdf); [Source](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1401.3436)
- Gumbel AlphaZero for single-player planning ("play-to-plan": the learner plays against a greedy roll-out of a frozen earlier policy): 100 simulations per move for the travelling salesman problem and 50 for job-shop scheduling. Optimality gaps, travelling salesman with 20/50/100 nodes: 0.17 % / 1.55 % / 3.16 % against 0.34 % / 1.76 % / 4.53 % for the attention-model baseline; plain single-player Gumbel AlphaZero reached up to 27.15 % at 100 nodes. Job shop 15×15 / 20×20 / 30×20: 16.6–18.4 % / 21.3–22.0 % / 30.6–32.3 % against 26.0 % / 31.6 % / 33.6 % for a learned dispatching baseline. The authors state it is not a state-of-the-art solver. J. Pirnay, Q. Göttl, J. Burger, D. G. Grimm, "Policy-Based Self-Competition for Planning Problems", arXiv:2306.04403 **[venue: prior knowledge, ICLR 2023]** — [Source](https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.04403)
- Self-improvement by imitation of the best sampled solution: sample several solutions per instance with round-wise stochastic beam search (sampling without replacement), imitate the best, with a policy update derived from a provable policy-improvement result; performance comparable to training on expert solutions for the travelling salesman and capacitated vehicle routing problems and a wide margin over prior methods on job-shop scheduling. J. Pirnay, D. G. Grimm, "Self-Improvement for Neural Combinatorial Optimization: Sample without Replacement, but Improvement", Transactions on Machine Learning Research, 2024, arXiv:2403.15180 **[abstract only]** — [Source](https://arxiv.org/abs/2403.15180)
- Actor-critic with a combinatorial-optimisation layer in the actor against imitation of an expert: comparable to structured imitation on dynamic vehicle scheduling, 8 % better on dynamic assortment and 78 % better on gridworld shortest paths; 16 % / 77 % / 92 % better than unstructured PPO on the same three dynamic problems; about 30 minutes of training per environment. H. Hoppe, L. Baty, L. Bouvier, A. Parmentier, M. Schiffer, "Structured Reinforcement Learning for Combinatorial Decision-Making", arXiv:2505.19053, 2025 (preprint) — [Source](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.19053)
- Imitation of a heuristic solver followed by RL on vehicle routing: "Imitation Improvement Learning for Large-Scale Capacitated Vehicle Routing Problems" (ICAPS; heuristics as teacher, RL as student; reported to surpass a well-known heuristic solver on capacitated routing) and a two-stage hybrid imitation-then-RL routing method (IEEE) **[search snippets only; claims not verified in the papers]** — [Source](https://ojs.aaai.org/index.php/ICAPS/article/download/27236/27009/31305); [Source](https://ieeexplore.ieee.org/document/10490595/)
- Residual RL on a discrete routing heuristic: a learned policy augments a backpressure routing policy in satellite-terrestrial networks, trained with double DQN over a binary action set, and consistently outperforms the plain double-DQN baseline **[search snippet, 2026 preprint]** — [Source](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2601.13662)

### Inferences
- The closest published precedent to "learned graph policy that starts from a fleet planner and must not fall below it" is the rollout → graph-network imitation → one-step lookahead pipeline of Garces et al.; its distribution-shift table is a direct warning that the amortised network alone loses the guarantee off-distribution, while keeping a lookahead step (or the base policy as a fallback) preserves most of the gain.
- The electric-vehicle charging-station paper shows that fixed-route-based rollout (a plan re-evaluated as queues are observed) is an accepted way to handle congested public charging in the operations-research literature, with dual bounds to measure remaining headroom; a rolling re-optimised plan is the same kind of base policy.
- Since the surveyed learning-based families (AggreVaTeD, THOR, LOKI, MAMBA/RPI, Lazy-MDPs, SPIBB, jump-start) have no published routing or dispatching application found here, applying them with an operations-research planner as the oracle would be new; the risks to check first are the ones their own papers report (value-estimate bias, gradient bias when the baseline is not the learner's value, margin tuning).
- No discrete-action "residual on logits over a heuristic's choice" method was found as an established technique; adding a learned score to a base policy's log-probabilities (initialised to zero so the start equals the base policy) is a natural discrete analogue of residual policy learning but should be presented as a design choice without a literature guarantee.

### Gaps
- Quantitative results of Kullman et al. (gaps to dual bounds, gain of dynamic rollout over the static policy) and of Ulmer et al. (service-rate gains) could not be retrieved (publisher and repository pages blocked automated access).
- No verified application of AlphaZero-type or Gumbel search to dynamic (online, stochastic-request) fleet routing with an expensive simulator was found; the verified applications are static construction problems.
- The two imitation-then-RL routing papers and the satellite-routing residual paper were seen only as search snippets; their claims of surpassing the heuristic solver are unverified.
- Surveys on learning from sub-optimal demonstrations and on safe policy improvement were not read beyond the one audit paper (Scholl et al. 2022).
